Dream Journal

The Night I Became the Artist

I went to sleep expecting nothing more than rest, but instead I slipped into a world that felt like revelation. I woke inside the dream with a brush already in my hand, sweat gathering at my temples, my brow tight with focus. Before me stretched a blank expanse—an abyss waiting to be transformed. And from that nothingness, color erupted. It didn’t flow gently; it detonated, volcanic and alive, as if creation itself had been compressed inside me and was finally breaking free.

I watched myself paint with a precision I’ve never known in waking life. Every stroke had depth, width, intention. I measured instinctively, like a scientist calibrating an experiment that could alter the world. Yet I wasn’t just the painter. I was also the witness—hovering just outside my own body, astonished by the confidence and mastery I saw. It felt holy, as if some divine hand had reached down to guide mine. I didn’t feel like myself, and yet I felt more myself than ever.

The strangest part was how the real world seeped into the dream. Each time my eyes flickered open, the window beside my bed poured its light into the vision. Tree limbs cast silhouettes across the room, and those shapes bent themselves into the dream’s canvas. The shimmer of morning light became brushstrokes. The organic structures outside fused with the inner studio of my mind. Even when I woke, the dream didn’t stop. It waited for me, held its shape, and pulled me back in.

There was another version of me inside that dream—an older, steadier, impossibly confident self. He moved like a producer, a creator, an elder who had already lived the life I’m still reaching for. He never hesitated. He never doubted. He painted as if he had been doing it for centuries. And I realized I wasn’t just dreaming; I was being taught. I was the student, absorbing lessons from a future self who knew exactly what I needed to see.

The dream carried the same magic I knew as a child—the forests and glades where I once danced alone, imagining spirits in the trees and castles on the horizon. Back then, I bent reality with my thoughts. In this dream, that power returned, but refined, matured, sharpened. It felt like the child I used to be and the artist I hope to become finally met in the same room.

When I finally woke for good, the dream clung to me—vivid, emotional, impossibly alive. I felt gratitude, fulfillment, and a strange sense of being chosen. It was a gift, a reminder, a covenant. A message from the deepest part of myself saying: You are becoming. Don’t give up. The best version of you is already waiting.

V. 2  
There are dreams that feel like static—loose, dissolving, forgettable.  
And then there are the other ones.  
The ones that arrive like detonations.

This one began with tossing and turning, my body restless while my mind slipped somewhere deeper. Inside the dream, my hand moved with a precision I’ve never had while awake. Every brushstroke landed exactly where it needed to, like I was painting with the muscle memory of someone who had lived a thousand artistic lives before this one.

It felt like watching an atomic blast slowed into beauty—destruction reorganized into creation.  
I wasn’t just painting.  
I was witnessing myself paint.

The observer‑self hovered behind the artist‑self, stunned.  
And then the strangest thing happened:  
my subconscious spoke.

Hey. I got this.

Not cocky. Not loud.  
Just certain.

The dream swelled with a holiness I can’t explain. Not religious holiness—something older, something that felt like the universe leaning in to watch. I felt awe at my own creation, as if the dream had staged a ritual of self‑recognition. It was God reaching down and the artist reaching back. A covenant formed in the space between us:

The best version of you is near. Don’t give up.

When I woke, gratitude hit me like a warm wave. I didn’t have to convince myself I was an artist. I didn’t have to hype myself up or rehearse affirmations. The dream had done something cleaner, sharper, more permanent.

It showed me the truth:  
I could never not be an artist.  
Even in sleep, I was creating.

The dream didn’t stop there. It introduced me to someone I’d never met but somehow always known—the Elder‑Artist. The version of me who has already crossed the threshold I’m still approaching. He moved with a confidence I’ve never worn in waking life. No hesitation. No second‑guessing. Just pure, unbroken flow.

I was the student.  
He was the blueprint.  
My subconscious was the teacher.

And then the dream folded backward into childhood—forests, glades, wood spirits, trolls, castles rising from fog. The loneliness of those years returned, but not as pain. More like a reminder that I had never truly been alone. My imagination had always been my first companion, my first collaborator, my first world.

I realized the child I had been—the mystic, the dreamer, the architect of invisible kingdoms—was the first version of the artist I am now. He was the initiator. He taught me how to bend reality with thought. He taught me that imagination is not escape but power.

The dream fused everything together.  
The window light from my room seeped into the vision.  
Tree silhouettes bent themselves into the inner studio.  
The waking world collaborated with the dream world, as if the boundary between them had dissolved.

Even when I opened my eyes, the dream didn’t end.  
It hovered.  
It waited.  
It asked for a vow.

And I gave one.

I vowed to voyage inward.  
To walk again with spirits and trolls.  
To let castles rise on the horizon of my mind.  
To meet the elder‑artist who meditates in the deep.  
To unite the child who dreamed with the adult who creates.  
To honor the continuity between waking and dreaming.  
To let the world teach me through silhouettes and shimmerings.  
To create with the window open, letting branches bend into my canvas.  
To remember that I am the artist who erupts into beauty,  
the child who bent reality,

I reached back, and the world reached with me.

That was the moment.  
Not a dramatic revelation.  
Not a career decision.  
Not a motivational speech.

A dream.  
A vow.  
A recognition.

I realized I could never not be an artist because even when I’m unconscious, even when I’m lost, even when I’m asleep, something inside me keeps creating. Keeps reaching. Keeps becoming.

Art isn’t something I do.

once you see your own subconscious pick up the brush and whisper I got this—  
you understand there’s no turning back.

You were born for this.  
You were built for this.

And now you’re awake.

THE NIGHT I GOT IN HIS TRUCK

I was driving toward Rib Country on a gray, unremarkable afternoon, the kind of day that feels like it’s waiting for something to happen. The parking lot was half‑empty, the neon sign flickering like it couldn’t decide whether to stay alive. I stepped out of my car and started toward the door, thinking only of food and the quiet that comes after a long drive.

Then I saw him.

Kanye stood near the entrance, talking to Kim as casually as if this were their usual spot. For a moment I froze, caught between disbelief and the strange certainty that I was supposed to be there. Something in me pushed forward. I walked up to him, heart pounding, and told him the truth — that I was a huge fan, that his music had shaped me, that I hoped to follow a path like his.

He laughed, not mockingly but with a kind of knowing amusement, like he’d been expecting me.  
“Get in the truck,” he said.

I didn’t hesitate. I followed him to a matte‑black truck parked crookedly across two spaces. I climbed into the back seat beside a man who looked like an agent or manager, though he didn’t say a word. Kim slid into the front. Kanye started the engine, and without warning, new music filled the cabin — raw, unfinished, pulsing with the kind of energy that makes your chest tighten.

I listened for a moment, then something inside me cracked open. I started singing along, adding harmonies, slipping in undertones, weaving my voice through the beat like I’d been waiting my whole life for this exact moment. I didn’t think. I didn’t ask permission. I didn’t worry about being pitchy or presumptuous. I just let go.

Kanye turned, eyes bright, and started grooving with me.  
“Boy,” he said, grinning, “we’re gonna make you a star.”

It didn’t feel like encouragement.  
It felt like a command — like destiny snapping into place.

The scene shifted, and suddenly I was in his house. Except it wasn’t really his. The living room looked almost exactly like mine, down to the way the light fell across the floor. I sat on the couch, still buzzing from the truck, and began to rap and sing again. This time it wasn’t about impressing anyone. It was about releasing something I’d been carrying for years.

I poured my pain into the room — the memories, the losses, the versions of myself I’d outgrown. The words came out sharp and honest, shaped by a rhythm I didn’t have to think about. It was so good, so real, that I felt myself waking up inside the dream, scrambling to write it down before it slipped away. But the dream pulled me back, insisting there was more to see.

The room changed.

The furniture dissolved into something shabby and worn. Puddles gathered on the floor. Random junk lay scattered everywhere — broken objects, forgotten things, pieces of a life left unattended. A black‑and‑white cat wandered through the mess, weaving between piles like it had lived there forever.

I looked around and felt a quiet, heavy recognition.  
This wasn’t Kanye’s house.  
It wasn’t even mine.  
It was my mind — the parts I’d let fall apart, the distractions, the wasted time, the habits that didn’t belong to the person I was becoming.

But I didn’t feel disgust. I didn’t feel trapped.  
I felt responsible.

I had done this.  
I had let it clutter and rot.  
And I was the one who needed to clean it.

The glamour of the truck, the music, the recognition — that was the future calling me.  
This room was the past I had to clear to reach it.

I stood there, surrounded by the mess, and felt something settle inside me. A decision. A readiness. A refusal to waste another hour on anything that didn’t align with the life I wanted.

The dream didn’t end with applause or a spotlight.  
It ended with responsibility — and the quiet certainty that I was finally ready to take control.

The Fragile Versions of Us

I was driving through the Kansas grasslands, the world stretched out in every direction like a quiet, breathing animal. The sky hung pale and endless above me, and the land felt strangely hollow, as if it were holding its breath. There was something liminal about it, something that made me feel suspended between versions of myself.

The woman beside me didn’t belong to the landscape. She was blonde, bubbly, polished to a shine, the kind of ultra‑feminine Barbie aesthetic that looked almost artificial against the rawness of the plains. She laughed at something on her phone, not at anything I said. Her perfume was sweet and synthetic, like cotton candy melting in the sun.

I tried to speak to her—not to impress her, just to reach across the space between us—but she looked at me with a bored certainty, as if she’d already decided who I was before I opened my mouth.

“You probably just waste your time masturbating and obsessing over girls,” she said, amused by her own cruelty.

The words hit me harder than they should have. Not because they were true, but because they echoed an old fear, a version of myself I’d spent years trying to outgrow. I showed her she was wrong, calmly, without anger. But she didn’t care. She’d already written her story about me.

Something in me shifted. I didn’t argue. I didn’t plead. I didn’t shrink. I simply stepped out of the car and walked away, leaving her and the version of myself who would’ve once begged to be seen differently. The Kansas wind brushed past me like a quiet affirmation.

The dream didn’t bother explaining how I got home. It never does. One moment I was on the plains, and the next I was standing in my own space, phone in hand, inviting Katie and her sister Ella to go on a cruise with me. It felt natural, almost inevitable, as if the dream were pulling me toward something unfinished.

Then I was on the boat.

The deck rocked gently beneath my feet, the ocean stretching out in every direction. The air smelled like salt and sun‑warmed metal. Katie stood across from me, older somehow, her demeanor changed in ways I couldn’t quite name. She looked at me with a mixture of distance and recognition, as if she were seeing both who I was and who I had been.

“When I was most vulnerable with you,” I told her, “you saw my affection as a problem. A mistake. A fault.”

The words cracked something open. She cried. I cried. Not because I wanted her back, but because I was finally acknowledging the wound that had shaped so much of who I used to be—the boy who needed permission, who feared abandonment, who mistook rejection for a verdict on his worth.

I realized then, with a clarity that felt like a blade, that I had almost ended my life because of her rejection. But I had been the one feeding the pain, cultivating it, letting it grow. I had always had the power. I just didn’t know it.

The boat rocked again, and the scene dissolved.

I found myself in a different space—not quite a room, not quite a memory. The walls felt like fog, the floor like possibility. Ella was there. Not physically, but as an emotional presence. Poetry, flowers, quiet afternoons, shared meaning. She felt like the embodiment of a connection I had always wanted but had never been ready for.

And then it hit me—not as guilt, but as recognition. I remembered how young we both were, how unformed, how fragile. I remembered the fear that lived in me back then, the way I clung to affection like it was oxygen, the way I mistook panic for love. I remembered the moment I hurt her—not because I wanted to, but because I didn’t know how to hold myself yet. I remembered how she had feelings she never let surface, how we were both too unfinished to meet each other cleanly.

But in this dream‑space, she wasn’t a symbol of regret. She was a symbol of alignment, of the kind of connection I’m finally capable of. I felt something for her—not the frantic longing of my younger self, but a steady, grounded recognition. This was the kind of love I wanted now. Depth. Presence. Artistry. Spirit. A partner who creates with me, not for me or against me.

I wanted to ask her to be with me—not out of desperation, but out of clarity. But the dream didn’t let me speak. It let me stand there instead, holding the truth that I was no longer the boy who needed permission, no longer the boy who feared abandonment, no longer the boy who hurt out of panic.

I was someone else now—someone formed, someone steady, someone capable of loving without collapsing.

The dream didn’t end with an answer. It ended with a feeling. I was ready. Not for the past, but for the future. For the partner who would meet me in artistry, depth, nature, and spirit. For the love I would treat like my greatest work of art.

And then I woke.

Where the Light Pauses

I was standing in the back of the old church when the light gathered—slow at first, then sudden, like a breath held too long finally breaking free. A woman near the altar cried “Jehovah,” her voice cracking open the air. The stones brightened around her, their rough faces washed in a glow so clean it felt like a kind of mercy.

Shadows fled the pews in a scatter of darkened hues, slipping past my legs like startled birds. Some vanished toward the windows, but others rose and curled into my vision, blurring the line between what I saw and what I feared. I couldn’t tell if I was meant to witness the moment or be swallowed by it.

The light thinned, stretched, and folded into something else. The church dissolved—not with a jolt, but with a soft, cinematic fade.

I found myself on a quiet street beside a homeless man slumped against a brick wall. His breath was shallow, a fragile rattle that seemed to echo off the pavement. I knelt beside him. His eyes opened once more, catching the last of the afternoon sun. Gold flickered, then green, then a dimming sheen—colors shifting like a final attempt to rhyme with the world before slipping out of it.

When his chest stilled, the silence that followed felt heavier than grief. It pressed against my ribs, a reminder that stillness can be louder than any cry.

A figure stepped into the frame—his adult son, hands trembling at his sides. He didn’t speak. He didn’t weep. He simply watched his father’s face, waiting for a breath that would not return. The streetlight above him flickered, casting a brief halo before dropping him back into shadow. His sorrow moved too fast for sound, so the dream slowed it, freezing him in a moment where pain and time refused to rhyme.

The world shifted again, this time like a camera panning through a doorway.

I was in a small bedroom. A mother stood beside a bed, her hand resting on the chest of someone already gone. The light here was warm, filtered through curtains that swayed with a breeze I couldn’t feel. It wrapped around her shoulders like a shawl, softening her outline, turning her grief into something almost holy. She inhaled slowly, as if gathering the last warmth left in the room.

A child’s voice pulled me toward the hall.

A little girl stood in the doorway, her eyes wide and wet. She didn’t step inside. She simply watched, her fingers gripping the frame. Her tears fell quickly—each one catching the dim light as it dropped, each one a tiny spark against the dark. Time slowed around her, stretching the moment thin, letting her grief fall in a rhythm all its own.

I moved toward her, though she didn’t see me. Her breath trembled, and for a second the entire dream held still—caught between her inhale and the soft fall of her tears. Cold and warmth, shadow and spark, all meeting in the space where her heart tried to understand what her mind could not.

The hallway behind her lengthened, darkening into a corridor that felt both endless and familiar. I stepped into it. The scenes behind me—church, street, bedroom—bled together like wet paint, their edges merging into a single, pulsing memory.

Light and shadow weren’t opposites here. They were partners, trading places, shaping each other’s form. A rhythm. A rhyme. A quiet duet.

At the end of the corridor, a final breath waited. I felt it before I saw it—a soft exhale, the kind that belongs to someone who has finally stopped fighting. A muted glow rose from the darkness, gentle as dawn breaking over a sleeping field.

In that glow, I understood: the beauty in defeat isn’t the fall, but the pause after—the breath where everything goes quiet and something unseen begins to rise. The soul, unburdened at last, finding its wings.

And then the dream let me go.

The Storm and the Buoy

About a year ago I had a terrible, enlightening experience even though I didn’t know it at the time. Mentally, there I was clinging cold and wet to a buoy bobbing with the force of the ocean, waves crashing around me, dark water swirling and frothing like a mad dog. The sky overhead bled dark clouds like the mark an ink brush makes when it is aggressively slammed against a fresh white sheet. The whole sky was a chorus and the stage was alight with a thunderous cacophony of lightning. I was terrified, alone, and in that moment a nasty voice grew inside me telling me to let go, that no one would miss me, that it wasn’t worth the pain of holding on. I almost believed it. But that’s where the story ends and begins anew in a different place.

Lying on the bed again, I lay in a restless slumber but my mind hadn’t stopped. I kept flashing in and out of a new storm, sweating through the sheets, rolling from side to side as if I could break my mind free from the vision. Every time I closed my eyes I was back, clinging to that metaphorical buoy, fingers slipping, waves pounding, thunder shaking the sky. I kept thinking I couldn’t hold on, that I was about to lose myself to the dark water. But even in the panic, even in the heat and the terror, I held on.

The scene looked something like this: I was standing with my sister, my brother, and my brother‑in‑law. It felt like I’d been dropped into a memory I hadn’t lived yet. I had just shown them something — something I’d poured myself into, something that had cost me more than time. It felt like I’d handed them a piece of myself. And they responded the way they always do. My sister watched but didn’t really notice. My brother admired it for a moment and then let it drift away. And Nathen, last of all, said the line that hit me like a slap: “It’s good, but how are you gonna make money?”

It felt like being reduced, like being stripped down to something useful instead of something alive. I snapped. I yelled “shut up,” and the moment cracked open. Then the scene shifted — not just the surroundings but the identity — and the whole world dissolved around me.

And again I was back in the storm.

Back on the buoy.

Back in the moment I’d been flashing in and out of on the bed. The waves were still towering, the lightning still splitting the sky, the terror still in my chest. But this time something changed. Instead of clinging, instead of waiting for the storm to stop, I pulled myself up. I rose out of the water and stood on top of the ocean. The storm didn’t calm. The thunder didn’t fade. But I wasn’t powerless anymore. I wasn’t drowning or begging the world to ease up. I was standing in it, above it, because I had taken my life into my own hands.

And even though the challenges were still there, I knew I could face them because of that moment — because I had already survived the part where I thought I couldn’t.

Two scenes, a year apart, but they feel like one scripture written across my heart.

The Couch and the Breath

Date: March 6, 2026  
Clarity: High  
Emotional Tone: Sensual, fulfilling, revelatory

I was on a couch with a woman I knew to be Mya — or someone my unconscious dressed in her likeness. The room was quiet, unhurried, the kind of stillness that doesn’t ask to be filled. Nothing rushed toward the physical. The world had narrowed to two people and the space between them, and for once, I was in no hurry to close it.

I put my arm around her shoulder. Leaned in. Kissed her cheek. And then something happened that became the axis the entire dream turned on — her breath picked up. It hitched, just slightly, a small involuntary tremor that no one could perform. I felt it against my skin before I heard it. And in that moment, pride rose in me — not pride in what I was doing to her, but pride in having been present enough to catch what was happening for her. Her reaction felt real. Even inside the dream, her expression carried weight, texture, interiority. She wasn’t a surface. She was a center.

More came to pass. But the details matter less than the orientation, because the orientation was the revelation.

I wanted to give.

Not perform. Not take. Not prove something about myself through her body. I wanted her to experience joy, pleasure, bliss — and I wanted to witness it. I wanted to watch her face as she let herself feel something fully. My fulfillment wasn’t waiting on the other side of my own sensation. It was living inside hers. I was both in control and deeply respectful of the fact that she was a whole person, and those two things didn’t compete. They fed each other.

Something inside me recognized the difference between sex and love. Not as a concept, but as a felt distinction — the difference between taking from a moment and being present inside one together. One leaves you emptier. The other makes you more whole. And the dream let me feel which one I was choosing without ever needing to name it.

Then a scene break, sharp and uninvited. Adult entertainment flickered into the frame — not as temptation but as contrast. The dream placed two orientations side by side the way a painter places complementary colors next to each other to make both unmistakable. Here is attunement. Here is consumption. Here is the version of you that notices her breath. Here is the habit that will make you forget how.

It wasn’t a lecture. It was a juxtaposition.  
And the message landed clean.

I think my anima wore Mya’s face that night — the feminine figure Jung says lives inside every man’s unconscious, the part of us that knows how to feel, how to receive, how to be in relation with another person instead of performing at them. For most of my life she’s been developing in the dark, learning to stand on her own, waiting for me to stop treating her like an object and start treating her like a presence. In this dream, she breathed. She reacted. She had her own interior life. And I didn’t try to flatten her into something I could use.

That’s movement. That’s the anima climbing out of appetite and into eros — not desire as hunger, but desire as relatedness. The force that connects. The force that makes two people more than the sum of their separate wanting.

The dream didn’t end with a vow this time.  
It ended with a knowing.

Something inside me already understands how to love like this — attentive, generous, present, unhurried. The work isn’t learning it. The work is clearing away everything that makes me forget.
