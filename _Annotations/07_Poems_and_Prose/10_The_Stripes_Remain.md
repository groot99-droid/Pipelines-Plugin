Wikilink back to [[07_Poems_and_Prose/10_The_Stripes_Remain|The Stripes Remain]].

The poem opens with a quoted line from an unnamed "she" — "A tiger can't change its stripes" — delivered "like a sentence passed," and the whole piece is structured as a rebuttal built over time rather than in the moment: the speaker admits believing her ("That was my first defeat") before tracking a fall-and-rise arc through failure, endurance, and eventual reclamation. Its central technique is taking the antagonist's own image — the stripes — and inverting its meaning without changing the symbol itself: "The stripes remained, / but they were no longer her prophecy. / They were my regalia." The trial-by-fire language ("I walked through fires that stripped the flesh from my pride... I endured until endurance became my crown") escalates into royal imagery — throne, crown, sovereign — that would read as grandiose if the poem hadn't earned it through the preceding stanzas of falling and enduring. The final couplet's quiet cruelty — "She will never see the man I became... for the one who swore I could never change / will never know how high I have risen" — makes the triumph private and unwitnessed by design, which is a more specific and bitter victory than a poem about being forgiven or being seen would offer.
