Unlike every other piece in this batch, this poem is told entirely in third person about an unnamed "she," which distances it from direct confession and pushes it toward allegory — a story about trust and betrayal in general rather than a named personal event. The poem's most unsettling move is fusing the stabbing with an embrace: "sliding between ribs with the intimacy of a whispered secret," and later she "draws him closer... and presses her lips to his" even as she is dying from his hand, collapsing violence and intimacy into the same gesture rather than treating them as opposites. The line "wolf's don't only hide beneath sheep's skin" (an apostrophe error in the source, kept verbatim) lands as the poem's thesis about camouflage failing — the danger was never disguised, only unrecognized. Formally, the poem refuses a clean ending: rather than closing on her death, it reframes the whole scene as "an inheritance: / a story older than memory, / repeated like a sacred curse," turning one woman's betrayal into a cyclical, almost mythic pattern that will recur rather than resolve — the same cyclical-harm logic that structures "Inherited," though here it's staged as narrative rather than direct address.
