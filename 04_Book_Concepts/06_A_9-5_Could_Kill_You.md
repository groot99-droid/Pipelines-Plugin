# A 9-5 Could Kill You

**Type:** Novel outline in five parts  
**Source:** `Master_Volume_1`, lines 5544–5566  
**Text:** complete and verbatim — nothing cut, condensed, or rewritten.  

---
Part One— A 9-5 Could Kill You

A man in his early twenties finds it hard to make friends. He is isolated, lonely, and he hates himself for it. He works a 9-5 and the position is drab: no excitement, he spent years in college for it but in the end he is not fulfilled. He’s never let himself get hurt ever since his first break up. It was what he would’ve considered true love. Mostly it was just him sacrificing himself but that isn’t brought up till much later. After that break up he has become an incel. He was out of shape and unattractive his whole high school history. It wasn’t till he got to college where he made friends and got fit but his toxic personality towards women after his breakup furthers his position as an incel. After college his friends stopped talking to him because he never went anywhere. He’s struggled with mental problems since he was little but for the most part he doesn’t know. He takes medication that he was prescribed at a very young age never told what they did. One day after another he had enough — after meeting up with a few of his ex-friends and trying out a few to many toxic dates, he decides to quit taking it because he doesn’t care anymore. He calls his job and he quits. His life goes downhill, he bed rots, doomscrolling till his fingers get stiff and blistered. He’s depressed and desperate but this doesn’t stay the case. The first half of the book ends with what seems like the start of another social failure.


Part Two — Romance is in the Air… or in This Case the Mirror

The second opens with the break of the depression episode. He gets out of bed, takes care of himself, and begins to move from it all. This is where the book takes its turn. Strange things happen to him. Stories start to mesh together. This is when the book starts to combine tropes and the main character realizes it. He gets into his car dressed for church — maybe he’ll try religion… as he gets off the train to go into work he wonders how he got here. Didn’t he start in his car? And didn’t he lose his job? With the storyline further branching and seemingly impossible to understand, it all comes backtrack to reality. He wakes up in his bed. He fell asleep and slept for 48 hours after stopping his medication. Again the scene of him escaping the depressive episode plays out. Except this time he starts to talk to himself in the mirror. While he resumes his life (which he only hallucinates leaving the house), he creates the idea he’s a great man, friend, and employee. He got his position back at his job and it turns out he’s really important — everyone loves him. This is the point where no bad things happen except small occasions where it feels so dramatic. Slowly he begins to talk to himself more and more. He arranges dates to listen to himself. Over time it almost seems like he isn’t the only one answering the conversation. His reflection and himself essentially start dating, leaving a good portion of this book to an almost perfect romance story of two men who happen to live together and over time started dating but can’t remember why. Once this part of the story happens the mirror is no longer his means of talking to his lover. He has now fully personified a version of himself and talks to it as if it was another person. Eventually the relationship turns sinister marking the third part of the book.


Part Three — Hate Rears Its Ugly Head

In this part his self-hatred begins to come out. His memories seem to ebb and flow like strange episodes that bring him more and more hatred. Eventually he cannot stand his partner (himself personified separately) leading to him offing his imaginary partner. This is the lowest point since he was on the medication. He remembers. He has multiple existential crises. He is confused and doesn’t understand what could be happening. At the loss of his partner he has weird episodes where he rampages through the house breaking things, screaming, crying, and cursing his partner. Eventually the neighbors complain and one fateful evening while he is in the middle of an episode, he hears a knock on the door and it is loud. The sound echoes through him almost as if his soul had left himself and he was hollow. In a turn of events he believes it is his partner come back for him. He knew he wouldn’t leave him. But the mess — he’ll see the mess. So he leaves a candle on the kitchen counter and turns the gas on the stove to clean up the mess (later incinerating his house). He then proceeds to walk out the door where he is met by the law. His illness next to leaves him. This is the first time since he could remember seeing another face other than his own. He breaks down crying, begging that they help him. Marking the beginning of the fourth part of the book.


Part Four — Time with His Therapist

He sits in a softly lit office. The air smells faintly of coffee and antiseptic. The therapist doesn’t start with the fire. She starts with the mirror. At first, he tells her about his “partner” as if it were real. She doesn’t correct him. She asks questions that feel like scalpels, peeling back the layers. The sessions are slow, repetitive, and exhausting. Sometimes he swears he hears the partner’s voice in the room. Sometimes he sees a flicker of movement in the therapist’s glasses. She teaches him to name the shadows without becoming them. One day she asks him to imagine the partner sitting in the empty chair across from him. Instead of rage, he feels grief. He admits — for the first time — that he loved that version of himself, even if it was toxic. This is the first crack in the armor.


Part Five — Living After It All

Months later. A small apartment. No mirrors. The light is warm but the silence is heavy. He works part-time stocking shelves at night. He likes the anonymity. He walks in the mornings. He cooks simple meals. He reads. Sometimes he talks under his breath, but it’s not to the partner — it’s to himself, as himself. Recovery is not a triumphant montage. It’s a negotiation with boredom, loneliness, and the temptation to relapse into fantasy. One evening, he passes a shop window and catches his reflection. For a moment, he thinks he sees the partner smiling back. But instead of fear or longing, he just nods — a silent acknowledgment — and keeps walking.
