> # Pillars: the rules every game in esc-ZINE99 follows
>
> These rules come from the author. Every game's `DESIGN.md` has to meet all of them, and
> `docs/COLLECTION.md` shows how the ten games meet them differently.
>
> ## 1. Length: 45 minutes to 3 hours
>
> A first playthrough lasts at least 45 minutes and at most about 3 hours. Each design states its
> own target, splits it into chapters with minutes per chapter, and the minutes add up to the
> target. Replays to reach other endings do not count toward the 45 minutes.
>
> ## 2. Every game earns the player's investment
>
> A game has to give the player a reason to keep playing, not only a premise. Every design names:
>
> - **The hook in the first 5 minutes**: the image, question or action that holds the player.
> - **What the player comes to care about**: a person, a place, a skill, or their own changing self.
>   The player has to have it before the game threatens it.
> - **The question that pulls them to the end**: something the player wants answered or decided.
> - **Pacing**: a beat chart showing tension rising and falling. No stretch longer than 15 minutes
>   without a new mechanic, place, revelation or choice.
>
> ## 3. Lore and immersion
>
> Each game has a lore bible: history, rules of the world, places, people and objects, and how the
> player learns each one in play (found, told, seen, or done). Lore is given through play first:
> objects, environment and the 1D games, with dialogue sparse, as in the author's prose.
>
> ## 4. Conditional endings
>
> A story ends how the author wrote it. The choices are what make it a game.
>
> - The author's written ending is always in the game, marked **as written**, and it is what follows
>   when the player keeps to the story's own path.
> - Other endings open when the player chooses differently. Every game has at least four endings.
> - Every ending has exact, testable conditions, stated as flags and meters in `data/endings.json`.
>   A player must be able to reach each ending on purpose, and a designer must be able to say why a
>   given run got the ending it did.
> - No ending is unreachable, and no two endings can both be true at the end of a run.
> - Where the author has not written an ending, every ending is a proposal and none is marked.
> - The author's craft rules still apply. In a horror game the endings keep a cost: there is no tidy
>   rescue, even in the kindest ending.
>
> ## 5. Three layers: 3D, 2D and 1D
>
> Every game uses all three, in several scenes each.
>
> | Layer | How it progresses | What the player does |
> | --- | --- | --- |
> | **3D** | Through space, in depth | Moves and looks freely through a scene. |
> | **2D** | Through space, on a plane | Moves or tilts along X and Y while the world moves forward. Like tilting your eye at a peephole, or travelling between scenes like a side-scrolling level. Progress is spatial. |
> | **1D** | By objective, with no travel | A full-page game within the game, flat in nature: searching a map page, picking a lock, a card game, a board game, crafting, cooking. The play is mental and passive in movement; you do not walk a path through it. |
>
> The test for sorting anything: in 2D the player is **getting somewhere**; in 1D the player is
> **working something out**.
>
> Each game has at least four 3D scenes, two distinct 2D modes, and four distinct 1D games. Each 1D
> game has rules, inputs, a win or finish state, a difficulty curve, and a reason in the story for
> being played. A 1D game always feeds back into the story or the endings.
>
> ## 6. Original and diverse
>
> No two games in the collection share a core verb, a 2D mode or a 1D game type. Where two games
> touch the same idea (becoming the monster, losing memory, investigating), `docs/COLLECTION.md`
> states how each one handles it differently, and the designs must keep to that.
>
> ## 7. Faithful to the source
>
> - The author's works in the `Pipelines-Plugin` vault (`creative-writing/vault/`) are certified
>   verbatim and are never edited. These games adapt them; they do not change them.
> - Lines quoted from a work are verbatim and cited by vault path.
> - Every design has a section listing what was **added** for the game and is not in the author's
>   text, so the author can approve or strike it.
> - Victims have interior lives and are never props (the vault's ethical-restraint rule). Graphic
>   detail stays suggested, not instructed.
>

> ## 5. Shared ideas, handled differently
>
> **Becoming the monster.** The Stuffed Ones and Man Eater both turn the player into the thing.
>
> - *The Stuffed Ones*: the change is sudden, an act break. You become one of a pack and share a
>   pulse with your friends. The horror is social: you hunt together, and the test is stillness.
> - *Man Eater*: the change is gradual, measured in your own body every day, and you are alone. The
>   horror is appetite: the test is what you eat, and your strength and your clarity pull against
>   each other.
>
> **Memory loss.** Four games touch it.
>
> - *The Stuffed Ones*: you lose **your own** memories. It is internal and it is identity. Stuffing
>   decides what you keep.
> - *Missing Campsites*: the **world** loses things, and you are among the only ones who keep them.
>   Nothing can be restored. The question is what you carry to safety.
> - *WrymWretch*: the world forgets, and memory is a **resource you restore** by recording and
>   retelling. It is reversible and it is the combat economy.
> - *The Lord of the Sea*: deliberately **not** about memory. Alexi's power comes from the sea's
>   wildness and sailors' devotion, a political and social power, not a remembered one.
>
> **Investigation.** Four games make the player notice wrongness.
>
> - *Behind the Peephole*: deduce **other people's** hidden lives. There are true answers and they
>   are scored.
> - *Missing Campsites*: compare **records against reality**. There is nothing to deduce; the choice
>   is what to save.
> - *Lira Calyx*: test **hypotheses with instruments**, knowing the data can be falsified.
> - *Man Eater*: **you are the evidence**. You observe your own body.
>
> **Isolation in a building.** Peephole (a hotel) and Man Eater (a base) both keep the player in
> one place at night. Peephole never lets you enter the rooms you watch; Man Eater lets you go
> anywhere, at the cost of being seen and of the cold.
>
> **Night cycles.** The Stuffed Ones, Peephole and Man Eater all run night by night. The Stuffed Ones
> counts three nights per group and loops groups; Peephole spends a fixed number of hours across six
> doors; Man Eater alternates a day of duties with a night of choices, and the 10:00 call divides them.
>
> **A god.** People of the Bark and The Lord of the Sea both involve divinity. In Bark the player
> is the people who mistake a squirrel and a hand for gods, and must never be told. In Sea the
> player is a real god who has lost his following.
>

### Source file: docs/DESIGN_TEMPLATE.md

> # DESIGN.md template
>
> Every `games/<slug>/DESIGN.md` has these sections, in this order, under these headings. A section
> that does not apply says so in one line rather than being left out.
>
> 1. **Summary**: the game in two sentences; source work and vault path; target length; players;
>    the core verb from `docs/COLLECTION.md`.
> 2. **Why this game**: what makes it different from the other nine, in a short table against the
>    one or two games nearest to it.
> 3. **Source fidelity**: what the author wrote, in a short beat list with verbatim quotes and
>    vault paths; and the author's ending, if there is one.
> 4. **Investment**: the hook in the first 5 minutes; what the player comes to care about and how
>    the game makes them care before it threatens it; the question that pulls them to the end.
> 5. **Lore bible**: history and timeline; the rules of the world; places; factions or groups;
>    objects that matter; for each item, how the player learns it (found, told, seen, done).
> 6. **Characters**: every named or speaking character, with want, fear, voice (two or three sample
>    lines, verbatim from the source where the source has them), and their arc in each ending.
> 7. **Structure and pacing**: a chapter table (chapter, minutes, layers used, new mechanic or
>    revelation, tension 1–5). Then a beat chart of the whole run. Minutes add up to the target.
> 8. **Levels**: one subsection per chapter. For each: goal, where it happens, the 3D scenes, 2D
>    sequences and 1D games in order, encounters or obstacles, what the player learns, flags set,
>    and how it ends.
> 9. **3D scenes**: one entry per scene: id, place, size, camera, controls, interactables, lighting
>    and mood, audio, triggers, entry and exit transitions.
> 10. **2D modes**: for each of the game's two modes: movement type (`scroll` or `tilt`), controls,
>     physics or rules, hazards, how it shows progress, failure and retry, and every sequence that
>     uses it.
> 11. **1D games**: for each: purpose in the story, rules, the full-page layout, inputs, win or
>     finish state, failure, difficulty curve across its appearances, hints, and what it writes to
>     flags or meters.
> 12. **Core systems**: every meter, economy or rule outside the 1D games, with numbers: ranges,
>     rates, thresholds, and what each threshold does.
> 13. **Endings**: each ending with its name, whether it is as written, its exact conditions in
>     plain words and as they appear in `data/endings.json`, its final scenes, and its last line or
>     image. Then a short "how a player reaches each one on purpose".
> 14. **UI and HUD**: every screen and what it shows; diegetic versus overlaid; menus; how the three
>     layers look different from one another.
> 15. **Art direction**: palette, references by mood (not by copying any existing game), materials,
>     lighting, how 3D, 2D and 1D share one look.
> 16. **Audio**: music by chapter; ambience; key sound effects; voice approach.
> 17. **Content list**: every model, texture set, 2D sprite sheet, 1D page layout, music cue, and
>     voice line group, with a count, so the build can be estimated.
> 18. **Data files**: what this game's `data/` folder holds, and anything in it beyond the shared
>     schemas.
> 19. **Build plan**: milestones from greybox to finished, each with what is playable at the end;
>     the first vertical slice.
> 20. **Risks and open questions**: risks with mitigations; questions only the author can answer.
> 21. **Added for the game**: every element not in the author's text, so the author can approve or
>     strike it.
>
> **Since 2026-11-15** every design also has an **Accepted development (2026-11-15)** section, between
> "Risks and open questions" and "Added for the game". It records the development the author accepted
> from the game's `DEEP_DIVE.md`, what each of the three phases built, and what is still not built.
> Its number differs from game to game (21, 20A, 20a, or none), so in six designs "Added for the game"
> is section 22. "Added for the game" stays the last section.
>
