 How to Help People

When I was younger I carried a strange conviction: that somewhere inside me lived a destiny to save the world. It wasn’t the loud kind of heroism you see in movies, but a quieter, sacrificial certainty—an idea that if I could throw every ounce of myself at a problem, someday the pieces would fall into place and I would know, in the marrow of me, that I had mattered. I imagined a life of obsession and small victories, a ledger of good deeds that would let me rest easy when the work was done.

For years I chased that ledger. I measured my worth by how much I could give away, how many people I could lift, how many problems I could carry until they were no longer mine. There were nights I stayed awake thinking of solutions, mornings I missed because I had been up planning someone else’s rescue. The impulse felt noble, but it also began to hollow me out. The more I gave, the less I recognized the person doing the giving.

The turning point came in a moment as ordinary as any other: an airline safety demonstration on a flight I almost slept through. The attendant’s voice—calm, matter-of-fact—said the line that had always sounded like a cliché: secure your own mask before helping others. It landed in me like a small, stubborn truth. If I became a liability, my efforts would do more harm than good. If I lost myself in service, there would be no one left to serve.

So I changed the shape of my mission. I stopped treating self-care as indulgence and began to see it as preparation. I learned to align helping others with my own goals—so that my compassion had roots and my generosity had boundaries. I still wanted to make a difference, but I no longer wanted that difference to be the only thing that defined me. Helping people became part of a life I loved, not the whole of it.

There is a fierce selfishness in loving your life, and it is the kind that saves others. Our lives are the only things in this world that belong to us alone; everything else we touch we share. Hold that life close, tend it, and you will find that the help you offer is steadier, wiser, and truer. In the end, the world doesn’t need a martyr so much as it needs people who are whole enough to give without losing themselves.
