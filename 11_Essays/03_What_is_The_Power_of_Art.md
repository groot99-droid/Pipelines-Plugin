What is The Power of Art

The first hands to make art belonged to our long‑forgotten ancestors. With charcoal‑smeared palms, they pressed their stories onto cave walls and created the earliest record of human memory.

Those marks weren’t elegant. They lacked contrast, polish, and technique. But they meant something. Even when survival consumed every waking moment, humans still made art. They honored beauty. They spoke to the future. We stand today on the bones of humanity’s past, lifted by scaffolding we never saw raised and hands we never learned to praise.

Across centuries, countless artists lived and died in service of making the world more beautiful. Painters filled halls with meticulous devotion. Musicians carved melodies that still echo in our chests. Unknown architects shaped skylines that expanded our imagination. Writers gave us worlds we could never enter without their life’s work.

Yet society has forgotten to honor them. Artists are dismissed as myth‑makers, dreamers, or people without “sensible” professions. And still, the world complains about its lack of beauty — as tasteless facades rise, films grow predictable, and music feels copied and hollow. We forget why artists matter.

There would be no Star Wars without George Lucas, no Lord of the Rings without J.R.R. Tolkien, no Starry Night without Vincent van Gogh, and beyond them are the countless creators they inspired. Imagine how many voices, visions, and worlds would never have been born if even one of these works had been lost.

There would be no Michelangelo without the chisels of Donatello, no Donatello without the revival sparked by Giotto, and no Giotto without the nameless icon painters who first dared to give spirit a human face. Each artist is a continuation of another’s unanswered question.

There would be no Frida Kahlo without the surrealists who fractured reality, no surrealists without the symbolists who blurred dream and waking life, and no symbolists without the romantics who insisted that emotion was worthy of worship. Every movement is a torch passed hand to hand.

There would be no James Baldwin without Richard Wright’s fire, no Wright without Zora Neale Hurston’s folklore, and no Hurston without the oral storytellers who carried entire worlds in their voices. Literature is a lineage of breath.

There would be no Hayao Miyazaki without Osamu Tezuka, no Tezuka without Walt Disney, and no Disney without Winsor McCay’s impossible imagination. Animation is a chain of dreams that refused to stay still.

There would be no Kendrick Lamar without Tupac, no Tupac without Gil Scott-Heron, and no Gil Scott-Heron without the poets of the Harlem Renaissance. Music evolves because someone keeps listening backward while moving forward.

There would be no modern architecture without Frank Lloyd Wright, no Wright without Louis Sullivan, and no Sullivan without the classical builders who first imagined that space could shape the soul. Every structure stands on the shoulders of another.

Art is not a series of isolated miracles. It is a continuous inheritance — a relay of vision, technique, rebellion, and devotion. Every artist is both a descendant and an ancestor, shaped by those before them and shaping those yet to come.

If we continue to treat artists as expendable, the world will not collapse all at once. It will dim slowly, quietly, almost politely. Beauty will thin out. Wonder will become rare. The future will inherit a world built only for function, never for feeling.

When we stop seeing artists as artisans, we forget that they are builders — not of walls or roads, but of meaning. Their craft shapes the invisible architecture of a culture: its symbols, its stories, its sense of what is possible. Without them, a society may still stand, but it will no longer live.

If we keep dismissing the artist, we will raise generations who know how to produce but not how to imagine. We will create cities that are efficient but soulless, technologies that are powerful but empty, and lives that are comfortable but devoid of awe. We will inherit a world that works, but does not move us.

Art is not decoration. It is direction. It tells us who we were, who we are, and who we might become. When we fail to honor the hands that shape it, we sever ourselves from the very lineage that made us human.

And so the question is not whether we can afford to value artists.  
The question is whether we can afford not to.

Because if even one artist’s voice is lost, an entire branch of the future goes silent. And if enough branches fall, the tree stops growing.

If we continue to overlook the artist, the loss will not announce itself. It will arrive quietly — in the stories that never get written, the songs that never find their first note, the paintings that never touch canvas because the hands that would have made them were told their craft had no worth.

Somewhere right now, a child is drawing on scrap paper, humming a melody, building a world out of nothing but imagination. And somewhere else, another child has already stopped. Not because they lacked talent, but because no one told them their vision mattered. That is how beauty dies — not in flames, but in forgetfulness.

Every artist you love was once a trembling beginner who needed one person to say, “Keep going.” Every masterpiece was once a fragile idea that could have been abandoned. Every cultural shift began with someone who refused to let their imagination be dismissed as impractical.

If we do not honor artists, we will inherit a future built only by what is profitable, never by what is meaningful. We will live in a world engineered to function, not to feel. A world where children grow up efficient but empty, where adults forget how to dream, and where humanity survives but does not soar.

But it doesn’t have to be that way.

We can choose to protect the hands that shape our inner worlds. We can choose to value the artisans of emotion, memory, and myth. We can choose to tell the next generation that beauty is not a luxury — it is a lineage, and they are part of it.

So let this be the moment we remember.

Let this be the moment we lift the artists still among us, honor the ones who came before, and safeguard the ones yet to be born. Because somewhere in the future, an artist will look back at us the way we look back at those charcoal‑stained ancestors — and they will either thank us for believing in them, or mourn the world we left behind.

The future is watching.  
The next artist is listening.  
And what we choose to value now will decide whether their voice ever reaches us.
