# The Two Who Woke the Sea

Sevrain had died carrying a fire he could not let the other gods finish fighting over, and when he went under, the fire went out, and his heart split the way ice splits — not evenly, but along whatever crack was already there. Grief went west. Hunger went east. That was the first thing the coasts remembered, though neither shore any longer knew it was a memory.

---

Yrsa woke with someone else's grief laid over her own like a second, wetter skin.

She was still kneeling at the mourning-bier when it happened — a fisherman's widow paying her, as always, to weep the professional tears a widow could not spare from her own dry eyes — and the grief that came up in her throat was not for the fisherman at all. It arrived whole, already grown, the way a wave arrives already having crossed an ocean to get to you: a love that had been drowned, and someone standing on a shore that was not this one, unable to stop looking at the water.

She kept weeping. It was, after all, her trade. But her own tears had always tasted of salt and performance, and these tasted of someone else's actual loss, and she could not spit that taste out for three days.

---

Corvin woke on the far coast with his hands still in the larder, and the larder empty, and his stomach as loud and unanswered as it had been before he'd eaten everything in it.

He was a scavenger of the tideline, used to hunger the way other men were used to weather. But this hunger did not belong to any meal he'd missed. It had a shape he almost recognized — the shape of wanting to gather something up before it could be taken, to hold a whole coastline in two arms and not let the water have any more of it. He ate a second time. It did nothing. He understood, distantly and with a scavenger's practical dread, that whatever he was hungry for did not live in a larder.

---

The first tremor came to Yrsa as a bell.

It hung in the mourning-house's narrow tower, cracked and old, rung once a season to mark the tide's turning. On the third night it rang itself, once, without a hand on the rope — and when the sound had finished, the bell's name (she had always called it *the widow's tongue*, a joke passed down from mourner to mourner) simply would not come back to her. She stood under it and could not remember what she had always called it. The bell had not fallen. It had un-become a little, the way the old stories said the second flood had un-made a capital city — not by breaking it, but by teaching it that it had never been separate from the water at all.

That was enough. Yrsa left before dawn, walking toward the middle of the kingdom the way water finds a low place without being told to.

The tremor found Corvin as a boat.

It was tied at the third piling from his shack, a boat he'd known since he was a boy, and it did not sink or splinter — it simply, over the course of one low tide, stopped being distinguishable from the water it sat in, planks and hull and painted eye on the bow going soft and grey and finally gone, as though the sea had decided the boat had only ever been a shape it was making, temporarily, out of itself. Corvin stood on the dock a long time with his hands empty, and then he understood that emptiness was the whole of what he had left to lose, and he could not sit still and watch it happen to anything else. He went inland, or as inland as a coast allows, walking toward the middle of the kingdom the way hunger finds a meal it has not yet located.

---

The crossing, for each of them, was less a journey than an argument they carried the whole way, the way you carry a stitch in your side.

Grief kept telling Yrsa to sit down. It had a soft, reasonable, exhausted voice, and it pointed out — at every river, every low salt-marsh, every place the road ran close enough to water to hear it — that the tide only wanted what already belonged to it, and that stopping, here, on the wet ground, and simply letting it in, would be so much kinder than all this walking. She sat down twice. Both times she stood up again, not because she'd won the argument, but because something east of her kept pulling like a hook set in a rib.

Hunger kept telling Corvin to seize. It had a bright, tight, practical voice, and it pointed out — at every orchard, every granary, every well-fed farmhouse he passed — that a coast which fed itself first would survive whatever the sea intended to take, and that a man who gathered enough might build a wall out of what he'd gathered. He filled his pockets twice. Both times he emptied them again into some stranger's hands, not because he'd won the argument, but because something west of him kept pulling like a tide he was already standing in.

On the fifth night, in a barn that smelled of dry hay and old rain, Yrsa dreamed of a hand reaching for hers across water she could not see the far side of, and heard, without any mouth speaking it, a voice that felt like her own thought worn a size too large: *I am the shore your grief keeps looking for.*

On the same night, on the other side of the kingdom's spine, Corvin dreamed of a woman standing very still while everything around her wanted to lie down, and understood, without deciding to understand it, that this was the other half of the hunger in his chest — not a meal, not a hoard, but a person-shaped place his wanting had been travelling toward the whole time. He answered her, in the dream, the only way the dream let him: *then I am the low tide you've been mistaking for an ending.*

Neither of them remembered, waking, that they had spoken in whole sentences. They only remembered the warmth of being answered, and walked faster.

---

They met at the strait on the falling tide, an hour before it was due to turn a third time.

Yrsa saw a man standing at the tideline with his fists loosely closed around nothing, and knew him the way you know a word you have been trying to remember all day the instant someone else says it aloud. Corvin saw a woman with salt-tracked cheeks who was not currently crying, and felt the specific, enormous relief of setting down a weight he had not known he was still carrying with both arms.

They did not embrace. There was no time, and besides, the shape of what they were to each other was not the shape of lovers — it was the shape of two halves of a broken thing recognizing the break.

"It rises tonight," Yrsa said.

"I know what it wants," Corvin said. "It wants us to go under and let it have the rest of him."

Below them, where the strait narrowed, the water at low tide had drawn back far enough to show the roofline of something that should not have been visible from any shore — a drowned court, columns furred with weed, a threshold worn smooth by two floods already. A handful of watchers stood at a distance on the headland, coastfolk who had built a poor living out of selling charms against the third rising and did not want it to end; they made no move to stop the two strangers, only murmured among themselves, uneasy at seeing their trade about to become obsolete. Yrsa and Corvin did not look at them long enough to be afraid of them.

---

They went down into the drowned court together as the tide began, at last, to turn.

The water came in the way memory comes back to a person half-asleep — not as a wall, but as a gradual, total realization. Yrsa felt the old, patient invitation to sit down and let it take her, and this time she did sit, on the lowest worn step, but she did not stop being herself while she did it. She let the grief that was not entirely hers rise all the way up through her chest and out of her mouth, mourning the god's fire and the god's drowning and the love he had lost the same night he lost everything else, mourning it completely, the way she had never once let herself finish mourning anything for money.

Corvin felt the old, bright command to seize and hoard, and this time he opened his hands instead, on the step beside her, and set down — into the rising water, deliberately, the way you set down something you have finally admitted you cannot carry — the hunger to keep the whole coast from ever being touched by loss again. He let the sea have back the grasping. He did not fight the water and did not flee it. He simply stopped closing his fists against it.

Grief finished mourning. Hunger finished wanting. Between them, for one moment, the drowned god's two halves of heart lay down together in the same body of water they had been split from, and the tide, having been given what it actually came for, did not need to rise a third time to take it by force. It came up gently around the columns, filled the court to the old flood-line and no higher, and began, with something almost like relief, to go back out.

---

The rite took its price the way any true rite does — not as punishment, but as balance.

Yrsa surfaced from the shallow court remembering that a man had stood beside her and that something enormous had passed between them, but she could not, reaching for it, find his name. Corvin climbed out beside a woman he knew he owed everything to and found the same emptiness where her name should have been. The dream-voice that had called each of them *the other shore* was gone entirely, as if it had spent itself completely in the one true thing it was for. They stood together a moment longer, two strangers who had just done something neither could fully account for, and then, because there was truly nothing further to say that either of them still had the words for, they went back to their own coasts.

---

The sea did not rise a third time as catastrophe. It simply, in the seasons after, behaved like water again — going out, coming in, keeping its own accounting without asking either shore to pay it in more than the ordinary tribute of tides.

On the western coast, Yrsa still wept other people's grief for a living, and was good at it, and once in a while, for no reason she could name, found herself walking down to stand at the tideline for a long time without weeping at all, only watching, with a faint, unplaceable sense that the water and she had once agreed on something important.

On the eastern coast, Corvin still gathered what the tide gave up, and kept less of it than he used to, and once in a while, for no reason he could name, found himself standing at his own tideline at the same hour, watching the same water, with a faint, unplaceable sense that somewhere across all that distance, someone else was standing very still and looking at the sea in exactly the same way.

Neither of them ever learned that this was true of the other. The sea, having taken back what it was owed, kept that much for itself.
