The Fitting

Incident One

The waiting room smelled like carpet cleaner and old magazines. She'd been here eleven times before, maybe twelve. She'd stopped counting the way you stop counting steps on a staircase you know by heart.

"Right on time," the technician said. Same greeting, every visit. Same clipboard under his arm, same pen clicked twice before he wrote anything.

He knelt, unbuckled the socket, ran two fingers along the liner where it met her skin. "A little loose at the distal end. We'll tighten that."

He read her circumference aloud while he measured — a number, then another. The second one made her blink. Smaller than she remembered from last time. Not by much. A fraction.

"Has that changed?" she asked.

"Normal fluctuation," he said, already writing. "Tissue changes day to day."

She nodded. That tracked. Bodies did that. She let it go before it had finished landing.

Incident Two

He measured her differently this time — not just the residual limb but the sound leg too, her shoulder height, something with a level held against her hips. He read the numbers off like weather.

None of them were wrong, exactly. They were numbers a body could have. They just weren't numbers her body had ever had.

She looked at herself in the mirror across the room while he wrote. Her shoulders sat different. Not lower — settled somewhere new, like furniture rearranged in a room she used to know blind.

"That seems different from before," she said, keeping her voice light, testing it like a floorboard.

"We calibrate against tolerance windows," he said, not looking up. "You're well within range." He smiled the same smile he always smiled. Nothing in his face moved wrong.

She told herself: tolerance drift. Normal adjustment variance. She'd read that phrase somewhere, or he'd said it before, or she was inventing it now because she needed it to be a real thing. She booked the next appointment on her way out, the way you book a dentist you don't want to think about.

Incident Three

"There," he said, stepping back once the adjustment was set. "That's more natural for you."

For you. Two small words. She turned them over on the walk to her car and couldn't get them to sit flat. Not more natural — more natural for you, like there was a specific her being solved for, and the her walking to the car was only an approximation still catching up.

At home she stood in front of the closet mirror a long time. Her weight fell forward onto the ball of her foot in a way it hadn't used to. Her hip didn't ache where it always ached at the end of a day. She pulled out an old photo from three years back, held it beside her reflection, tried to make the comparison hold still. The evidence was thin. Deniable. A body changes on its own, she told herself. That's not a horror. That's just aging, just healing, just an adjustment that's finally sticking.

She almost believed it enough to sleep.

Incident Four

She saw it before he could turn the screen away — an ID field with digits that weren't her intake number, a measurement column going back further than her file should. A note, half a sentence: prefers left-side load transfer.

She'd never told anyone that. She didn't know if it was true.

"What is that," she said. Not soft this time. Flat, direct, the question with its spine straightened.

"Historical baseline," he said, unhurried, turning the screen a careful two degrees. "It helps us track trend lines across the fitting." He said it the way you'd explain a receipt. Nothing in it closed the question. Nothing in it opened it either. The appointment simply continued around her objection, the way water continues around a rock, and by the time he was fastening the strap she found she had let the moment pass without deciding to.

Who is this fitting for, she thought, and the thought arrived without punctuation, without drama, just settled into her like a stone dropped into still water.

She sat in the parking lot a long time before starting the car. She thought about not booking the next one. She booked the next one. The limb had to work. She had a life to walk through. She was not, she told herself, the kind of person who made a scene over a feeling.

Incident Five

"Hold there," he said, and she was already holding there — weight shifted, wrist angled, the grip adjusted before the instruction finished leaving his mouth. Her body had started anticipating him. It knew what he wanted before he asked it, the way a hand knows a doorknob it's turned ten thousand times.

She noticed this about herself with a strange, borrowed calm. Not panic. Panic would have meant some part of her still expected to be able to stop it.

"Good," he said, in the tone he'd use for someone he'd been seeing for years. Not first-visit tone. Not new-patient tone. The tone of continuity, of familiarity, of a relationship much longer than the one she remembered having with him.

She didn't correct him.

She let the implication stand in the room with them, unchallenged, the way you let a guest sit in a chair that used to be someone else's.

Incident Six

The mirror agreed with her now. That was the strange, quiet fact of it — no gap left between the number on his clipboard and the shape looking back at her. The wrongness hadn't been corrected. She had simply arrived at it, the way a coastline arrives at the shape the water always intended.

"Last adjustment," he said, and reached for the strap.

She was already there first.

Her hands found the buckle, drew it snug, set the angle he would have set, in the rhythm he would have used, before he had to lift a finger. It wasn't obedience. It wasn't even decision. There was no seam left between wanting to and doing it for her to fall through.

Something in her chest settled that she didn't have a word for and didn't look for one. Not triumph. Not grief. Something closer to the stillness after a held breath finally lets go.

She knew what this was. She knew what she'd let happen across every appointment that had led here, knew it the way you know your own name, without needing to say it aloud. That knowing was hers — quiet, private, whole — and it was the last thing she was sure belonged only to her.

He closed the file. Clicked the pen twice, the way he always did, and set it down.

"All set," he said, courteous as ever, already turning toward the door.

He opened it onto the waiting room, glanced at his list, and called the next name — same even tone, same unhurried patience, already recalibrated toward whoever was next in the chair.

The door swung shut behind her on its own quiet hinge.
