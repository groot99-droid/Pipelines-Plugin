Through the Eyes of Wolf and Lamb

Content warning: this book satirizes institutions, language, and the stories we tell about violence. It refers to harm and culpability but does not describe methods or revel in injury. If you need to step away at any point, do so.

I write this as a set of promises and as an experiment. A reader deserves to know the rules before they enter a house whose rooms are lit by irony and whose windows sometimes show things you would rather not see. I also want to test whether a voice can hold both a joke and a wound, whether satire can indict without erasing, whether a ledger can be a kind of mercy.

I will not use real names, ongoing cases, or private details of people who have suffered. I will not provide instruction, technique, or anything that could be taken as a how‑to. I will not make victims into props for cleverness; they will have interior lives, small habits, and the dignity of being more than plot points. I will aim my satire at systems—press releases, checklists, risk assessments, the language of mitigation—and at the human vanity that dresses itself in procedure.

These are not legal shields. They are not moral absolutions. They are, at best, the scaffolding of an ethical experiment: can a writer hold a mirror up to the machinery that makes harm legible without becoming part of that machinery?

Four is a shape that feels like a sentence: setup, escalation, chorus, reckoning. One book to show the private ledger; one to show the public forms; one to show the witnesses and the rumor economy; one to show the aftermath and the market for remorse. Four books let me move from intimacy to spectacle and back again without stretching the conceit into a carnival.

Expect an unreliable intimacy. Expect lists and receipts, memos and mock press releases. Expect a voice that can be charming and then, in the next breath, cold. Expect irony that is not a shield but a scalpel: it cuts to reveal the rot beneath the varnish. Expect omissions. Sometimes what I do not say will be the loudest thing in the room.

If you have read this far trusting me, then I have done my job as a rhetorician. If you have read this far suspicious of me, then I have done my job as a provocateur. Both are useful states for what follows.

There is a paragraph I could have placed elsewhere, but I put it here because the foreword is the place where a narrator can be most theatrical about ethics. I will be theatrical now.

I have been present at scenes that will appear in these pages. I have read the forms that will appear as epigraphs. I have signed the letters that will be quoted. I have, in small and large ways, been part of the machinery that turns private harm into public language. I do not mean this as a boast. I do not mean it as a confession in the legal sense. I mean it as a fact of proximity. Proximity is not innocence. Proximity is not guilt. Proximity is a kind of responsibility that sits in the throat like a stone.

I remember a blue mug. I remember washing it once because the handle fit my thumb the way a small kindness fits a day; I remember the sound the spoon made against the ceramic. That sound is why I could not pretend distance. That sound is why the admission in this foreword is not an abstract gesture but a personal ledger entry.

You will find, in the second book, a killer who keeps lists. You will find, in the third, a chorus of witnesses who misremember and misreport. You will find, in the fourth, the market for apologies and the ledger of what can and cannot be repaired. Read them now with the knowledge that the person who wrote the disclaimers is not a neutral observer. Read them knowing that the voice that promises dignity has, at times, been the voice that helped translate dignity into a line item.

This is not a plea for forgiveness. It is not a demand for absolution. It is a refusal to hide behind the convenient fiction that satire is always a higher ground. I have been on the ground. I have seen how the forms we invent to manage risk can become the very instruments that obscure harm. I have seen how a press release can be a kind of erasure. I have seen how a checklist can be a way of not looking.

If you are looking for a narrator who will be pure, you will not find one here. If you are looking for a narrator who will be honest about the ways language can wound and the ways language can be used to avoid responsibility, then you will find that here, in awkward measures.

There will be moments in these books when the humor is sharp and the satire lands. There will be moments when the humor is a thin veneer over something that is not funny at all. Those moments are intentional. They are the point. Satire that only amuses is a party trick. Satire that unsettles asks a question of the reader: what do you do with the knowledge that the teller is not innocent?

I will end this foreword not with a defense but with a ledger entry—small, precise, and unadorned—because the books that follow are full of lists and because lists are how we try to make sense of what we cannot fully understand.

Ledger entry: I was there. I wrote the forms. I signed the letters. I watched the language do its work. I will not tell you how to harm. I will tell you how we made harm legible, how we packaged it, how we sold it back to ourselves as something manageable.

Read the quartet as a confession and as a critique. Read it as a satire that turns inward. Read it as an experiment in whether a voice that has been complicit can still be useful in naming the systems that made complicity possible.

I wrote the rules so you would trust me. I broke them so you would notice.

I begin with a list because lists are how I make the world legible. They are tidy, finite, and they let me pretend that consequence is a checkbox. Today’s list is ordinary: milk, batteries, call the landlord, pick up the suit. I write the items in a small notebook with a pen that leaves a faint blue smear when I press too hard. The smear is a kind of signature; it tells me I was here.

I narrate myself as someone who notices details. I notice the way the coffee shop’s loyalty app calls a free pastry a “customer appreciation gesture.” I notice the way the local paper rewrites grief into a headline that fits a template. I notice the way people say the word “incident” as if it were a neutral weather event.

There is a pleasure in the ritual of checking things off. The pleasure is not the same as joy. It is a small, private economy of control.

A receipt folded into a pocket: wholegrain bread; two bananas; note: call Mom on Thursday. The handwriting on the note is slanted, quick, the kind of slant that belongs to someone who writes lists to outrun forgetting.

I rehearse explanations in the shower. Explanations are like scripts; they keep the voice steady when the world wants to shout. I practice the tone that will sound reasonable in a press conference: contrite, measured, forward-looking. I practice the sentence that will make people nod and then forget.

My mind arranges motives into tidy categories: stress, miscommunication, a lapse. Motives are useful because they make moral complexity look like a spreadsheet. I like spreadsheets. Spreadsheets have columns and totals and a final cell that sums everything up.

A voicemail saved on a cracked phone: “Hey, it’s me. Don’t forget the plant. Water it on Friday. Also—if you get a minute—call me back. I found a place that does pottery classes.” The voice at the end laughs, small and private.

I tell myself that language is a tool and that tools can be repurposed. I take the language of care and bend it into policy. I draft a memo about boundaries that reads like a love letter to procedure. I write: We must balance empathy with operational integrity. It is a sentence that sounds like both a promise and a disclaimer.

There is a small, comic distance between the person who writes memos and the person who lives inside them. The memo author believes in systems. The person who lives inside them believes in outcomes. I am both.

A half-finished letter found in a drawer: “…and if you ever come by, bring the blue mug. I keep thinking of the way the light hits the sink in the morning. I miss the way you hum when you’re making coffee. If this is the last letter I write, know that I kept your postcards in a shoebox under the bed.” The sentence stops as if interrupted by something ordinary and then not.

Humor is a defense mechanism and a rhetorical strategy. I use it to soften edges, to make the reader lean in. I tell jokes about forms and about the absurdity of checkboxes labeled Mitigated. The jokes land because they are true: mitigation is often a verb we use to make ourselves feel less culpable.

I begin to notice the gaps. The victim’s handwriting on the receipt is a different rhythm than the memos I write. The voicemail’s laugh is a sound I cannot replicate. The half-finished letter contains a tenderness that resists translation into policy.

A photograph tucked into a book: two people at a fair, one with a paper crown askew, both squinting into the sun. On the back: June 12. Best day for cotton candy. The ink has faded at the edges.

The checklist grows teeth. Items that once fit neatly into boxes now resist categorization. Apologize is not a checkbox. Make amends is not a line item. I try to write them as such anyway. I draft a form: Restorative Action Request. It has fields for sincerity, for timeline, for measurable outcomes. The form feels ridiculous and necessary at once.

I begin to feel the weight of proximity as a physical thing, like a stone in the throat. It is not guilt in the melodramatic sense. It is the slow recognition that language can be used to tidy away a person.

A grocery list with a doodle in the margin: eggs, coffee, soap and a small sketch of a cat with one ear bent. The doodle is the kind of thing you make when you are thinking of someone else and the world is otherwise ordinary.

I tell myself the truth in small increments. The truth is not a single, dramatic confession; it is a series of adjustments to tone, to omission, to what I choose to emphasize. I begin to see my own voice in the memos I wrote. I see the same rhetorical moves: the passive construction, the appeal to process, the invocation of “lessons learned.”

There is a moment—quiet, almost bureaucratic—when I realize that the forms I drafted did not merely describe a world; they helped make it. The checklist did not only record risk; it taught people how to think about risk. The language of mitigation became a way to avoid looking.

A voicemail, older: “Don’t forget to laugh today. Even if it’s small. Even if it’s at something dumb on TV. Promise me you’ll laugh.” The promise is a small, human insistence.

Midweek, I write a memo titled “Boundaries and Care.” It is careful, the kind of document that will later be praised for its balance. Two days later, a press release uses the same sentence: “We are committed to learning and to doing better.” I see the line on the news and recognize my own phrasing repurposed as corporate absolution. The recognition is a small, cold thing.

There is a chapter where the checklist unravels. The list becomes a single paragraph of breathless fragments: milk, batteries, the way the light used to fall, the way I pretended not to hear, the pen that smudges blue, the sound of a spoon, the sentence I keep erasing and then writing again until the paper looks like a palimpsest of excuses. The paragraph is a fissure. It is the moment the tidy voice cracks.

I begin to notice the manuscript itself. I keep drafts in a shared folder. One night I open a file labeled FOREWORD_FINAL.docx and find my sentences rearranged. The top of the page reads “COMMUNICATIONS HOLD: Key Messages.” A paragraph I wrote about light in the kitchen has been replaced by a checklist titled “Apology Talking Points.” My cursor blinks in the margin where a new sentence—cold, procedural—has been pasted. The file properties show a later timestamp and a device name I do not recognize: COMM-LAPTOP. I print the page and the paper smells faintly of coffee and toner. In the margin, in a hand that is not mine, someone has written: We will learn and grow.

There are marginal notes in the printed drafts. My neat, looping hand appears beside a sentence: (emphasize dignity; avoid sensationalism) — 3/12 09:14. Beneath it, in blocky capitals, a later note: We will learn and grow. — added 3/13 02:07, device: COMM-LAPTOP. The handwriting is different. The tone is different. The insertion reads like an occupation.

I take the printed draft to the office copier because I want to see the physical evidence. The copier jams. When I open the tray, a sheet falls out with a paragraph glued in, the glue still tacky. Someone has physically pasted a new paragraph into my manuscript. The glue leaves a faint sheen on the paper. The act is small and intimate and violent in the way a theft can be.

At a PR meeting I watch language being chosen to erase a person. A junior comms person says, “We should say their name. We can’t keep calling it ‘the incident.’” The counsel taps a pen. “Names complicate liability,” she says. “We need a line that reassures stakeholders.” The junior opens their mouth and closes it. The final statement reads: We are deeply saddened by recent events. The choice is not accidental. Erasure is a policy decision.

The town hums with ordinary life until a series of small absences—missed shifts, an unanswered voicemail, a canceled pottery class—becomes a public story. The witnesses assemble themselves like a chorus: the barista who remembers a laugh, the cop who files the wrong box, the neighbor who swears they saw nothing, the influencer who turns grief into content. Each vignette is a shard of truth and a mirror of omission.

The barista rewrites a customer’s order in a notebook; later the same notebook contains a line that reads like a checklist rather than a memory. The cop files a report under PROPERTY instead of PERSON because the form looked similar and the day was long; the misfiling becomes a motif for institutional mis-seeing. The influencer posts a clip that reframes grief as spectacle; comments and metrics drown out testimony.

The misfiled box is a small, tactile thing. The clerk’s fingers hesitate on the tape. The label reads PROPERTY in a hand that had once been neat; someone has scrawled PERSON in a different ink. She breathes dust into the room and feels the weight of the folder like a small animal. “I thought it was paperwork,” she says later, voice small. “I didn’t think it would be a person.” When the box is opened, the smell of old paper and dust rises. Inside are a receipt, a photograph, a voicemail transcription. The items are not new. They are only newly visible.

At the community meeting the vignettes converge. Official reports, viral clips, and private testimonies are read aloud. The meeting becomes a theater of authorship: who gets to narrate the event, and whose voice will be believed? One witness reads an unedited fragment from my original manuscript—an honest, unadorned sentence that refuses the checklist’s grammar—and the room goes quiet. For a moment the town hears the difference between being described and being named.

The manuscript fragments in the margins begin to show a struggle. My neat notes—(emphasize dignity; avoid sensationalism)—are followed by blocky insertions that read like directives. I find deleted lines in the file history: a sentence naming the person removed, a paragraph about light in the kitchen replaced by a paragraph about lessons learned. I try to restore the original text. My edits reappear overwritten. The timestamps tell a story: my draft at 09:14; an insertion at 02:07 from a device labeled COMM-LAPTOP. The takeover is not metaphoric. It is logged.

The hearing is advertised as accountability. It is scheduled in a municipal auditorium with a livestream link and a press table. The clerk who misfiled the box testifies. She speaks in a voice that is small and honest. She says she misread a label. She says she filed a folder under property because the form looked like property. She says she did not think the difference mattered.

A lawyer objects. A headline runs: Procedural Error Hampers Case. The livestream chat fills with shorthand outrage and performative grief. The hearing admits evidence in technical terms. A key witness statement is excluded because it arrived one day late. The misfiled box is found in a storage room behind a stack of old risk assessments. The items inside are not new. They are only newly visible. The hearing produces a ruling that is procedural rather than moral. There is no criminal conviction. There is a civil settlement with nondisclosure clauses. There is a press release template that the killer’s team circulates to partners. The town watches a legal grammar protect a person more than it serves the harmed.

Interleaved with the hearing are the victim’s reconstructed entries: small domestic facts that refuse to be flattened. I kept lists because lists were how I remembered to be alive. Bread. Water. Call the neighbor about the mail. The lists are small things. They are not heroic. They are the shape of a life. I liked the blue mug because it fit my hand. I liked the way the cat slept on the radiator in winter. If you want to know me, start there.

When they asked me to describe the day, I told them about the light in the kitchen. They wanted a timeline. I gave them a timeline and then I gave them the light. The timeline fit into a box on a form. The light did not.

I do not want my life to be a lesson. I want it to be a person. If you must tell a story, tell the small ones: the postcard kept in a shoebox, the laugh that came before the sentence. Those are the things that do not translate into press releases.

Restitution arrives in forms that look like choices. A corporate donation is announced with a logo and a pledge to fund mental health initiatives. A grassroots fund is started by neighbors to pay for counseling and to maintain a small memorial. A restorative circle convenes in a church basement with no cameras and no press releases. People speak. People listen. The circle produces no legal remedy but it produces names and promises and a plan to check in.

The killer signs a book deal. The advance is publicized as a way to fund the settlement. The killer goes on a podcast tour. The interviews are edited to emphasize learning. The market for remorse is brisk. Publishers call it a necessary conversation. Critics call it commodified contrition. The killer’s life continues with new income streams and curated vulnerability.

The community’s appetite for closure is uneven. Some accept the settlement and the garden fund. Some refuse the hush clauses and demand public naming. Some want the killer to be shunned. Some want to move on. The town fractures along these lines. The fracture is not dramatic. It is a slow, social erosion: friendships that once were easy become careful; neighborhood barbecues have a seat left empty.

I decide to reclaim the pen. I publish an annotated edition of the killer’s memoir excerpts alongside the unedited fragments of the foreword. The side-by-side exposes rhetorical sleights: passive voice, euphemism, the conversion of personhood into lesson. The annotations are not vindictive. They are forensic. They show how language was used to sanitize and to sell.

I testify at a public forum, not as an expert but as someone who watched their own voice be colonized. I read aloud the sentence that began the foreword before the invasion: I will not make victims into props. The room listens. The reading is small and precise. It is a reclaiming.

A garden is planted on a strip of municipal land that had been a vacant lot. The garden is funded by small donations, by the grassroots fund, and by neighbors who bring shovels and seedlings. There is no ribbon-cutting with the killer on stage. There is a bench with a plaque that names the person who is gone and lists three small facts: the blue mug, the cat with the bent ear, the postcard shoebox.

The killer walks by on the other side of the street, a figure in a crowd, not confronted, not forgiven. The killer’s apology has been rehearsed; the garden’s work is not. The victim’s sister kneels in the soil and presses a seed into the earth. She does not speak into a microphone. She does not sign a release. She plants.

Before the planting, a diary fragment is read aloud: “I like the blue mug because it fits my thumb. If I forget everything else, remember the mug.” The sentence is small and stubborn. It refuses the ledger’s grammar.

I sit on the bench with a notebook and a pen that leaves a faint blue smear when pressed too hard. The smear is a kind of signature. I write one sentence, unadorned and final.

We planted what we could; the rest we learned to name.
