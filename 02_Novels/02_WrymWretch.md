ACT I — READER‑READY NARRATIVE

The first sign came quietly, the way rot always does—soft, almost apologetic, as if asking permission to exist. Thalen of Cedarheart noticed it while tracking a silver‑antlered stag through the western groves. The forest should have been humming with life, but the air felt thin, as though something had taken a long breath and forgotten to exhale.

He found the tree at the edge of a clearing. Its trunk had split open from within, not by lightning or age, but by something that pulsed like a second heartbeat. Black ichor seeped from the wound, thick and slow, and when it touched the moss, the moss turned gray and brittle, collapsing into dust that didn’t even remember how to be soil.

Thalen reached out. The tree recoiled—not in movement, but in spirit. A shiver ran through him, a sensation like brushing against a memory that wasn’t his. He stumbled back, heart pounding, and for a moment he thought he heard a whisper beneath the bark, a voice trying to form a name it no longer possessed.

He ran. Branches clawed at him as he sprinted toward the outpost, and the forest seemed to lean away from him, as if ashamed of what it had shown. By the time he burst through the wooden gates, he could barely speak.

“The forest is… forgetting,” he managed. “Something is making it forget.”

The rangers exchanged uneasy glances. They had heard rumors. None dared speak the name behind them.

Not yet.

Far to the north, where the sky‑cities drifted like pale islands in the clouds, Lady Aeris Galewing stood on her balcony and watched her people tremble. Sky Elves were not meant to shiver. Their bodies were tuned to the cold, their minds sharp as the winds they commanded. Yet now they lay wrapped in blankets, wings drooping, eyes unfocused.

A healer approached her, his voice barely above a whisper. “They speak of voices in the wind. They say the stars are calling them by name.”

Aeris had heard the voices too. Soft at first, then insistent. A whisper threading through the clouds: Remember me.

The Priests of the Gale summoned her to the storm chamber. Lightning flickered across the crystalline walls as they spoke of omens, of sickness, of something brushing against the minds of the living. Aeris was chosen to descend from the sky and seek aid from the other realms.

As she prepared her sky‑sail, she looked once more at her people—fevered, trembling, whispering to unseen stars. Something ancient was stirring, and it was reaching for them.

In the deep southern forests, Elder Rhyla Mosswhisper knelt before the Heartstone. The crystal’s glow, once steady as the earth’s pulse, flickered like a dying ember. She placed her palms upon it, and a tremor ran through her bones.

The vision struck her like a blow: a prison without walls, a name dissolving into silence, a cosmic shape pressing against the veil of existence. She gasped and pulled her hands away. The Heartstone dimmed further, as though exhausted by the effort of remembering.

“Not yet,” she whispered. “You cannot fade. Not now.”

But the crystal knew the truth. Its power was tied to memory, and memory was being erased.

Rhyla rose, leaning heavily on her staff. She would journey to the Deep Core, where the oldest crystals slept. If any wisdom remained, it would be there.

Along the eastern shores, Lunessa Tidecaller waded into water that should have been alive with darting fish and swirling light. Instead, the sea lay still—murky, heavy, suffocating. She dipped her hand beneath the surface, and the water clung to her skin like oil.

Behind her, the elders murmured in fear. The ocean was losing its memory of life.

Later, lantern‑crystals strapped to her belt, Lunessa descended into the depths with the divers. The water grew darker, colder, heavier. Shapes moved in the gloom—shapes that did not belong to the sea.

At the bottom, half‑buried in silt, she found a monolith carved with symbols her mind refused to hold. Every time she tried to read them, the meaning slid away like a dream upon waking.

She surfaced screaming.

In the Underworld, Kael Duskbane pushed open the stone door of the forbidden chamber. Dust swirled. Candles flared. The prophecy lay open on a pedestal of bone.

When the last memory fades, the Astral Architect shall rise.

Kael’s blood ran cold. He had heard the name once, whispered by a dying sage: Shallock Izladar. A name the world was trying to forget.

He closed the tome and drew his blade. Someone was erasing memories. Someone was killing witnesses.

Kael would hunt them.

High above the world, in the star‑lit halls of the Luminary Priory, Scholar Seraphyne watched the constellations flicker like dying embers. The heavens were unraveling. She traced the patterns with trembling fingers, and the stars formed a sigil she had hoped never to see again.

The Sigil of Izladar.

She summoned the Priory. Their faces were grim.

“The prison weakens,” she said. “The cosmos trembles. The Remnants move in shadow.”

“And the name?” an elder asked.

Seraphyne hesitated. “Already fading.”

And far beyond all realms, where the land cracked open into a vast abyss of swirling astral winds, WrymWretch stirred.

The bones of ancient wyrms hummed with a resonance not heard in millennia. A ripple of light tore across the chasm, illuminating the cliffs in ghostly fire. From the depths came a voice—distant, echoing, half‑formed.

I… remember… me…

The world shuddered.

The first seal had cracked.

Act I Ends.


ACT II — THE AWAKENING OF THE TWIN SOULS

The night after the forest’s first forgetting, Thalen could not sleep. The Cedarheart Enclave was restless; even the leaves seemed to whisper in uneasy spirals. Sylvan lay coiled beneath the great elder‑tree, its massive form half‑hidden by vines that grew and shifted with its breath. The dragon’s presence steadied the forest, but only barely. The corruption pulsed at the edges of the grove like a bruise.

Thalen sat beside Sylvan’s flank, feeling the warmth of the dragon’s heartbeat through the bark‑like scales. His own pulse matched it, as it always had. He had known since childhood that he was different — that the forest spoke to him in ways it didn’t to others, that vines curled toward his hands, that animals watched him with knowing eyes. But he had never understood why.

Now he did.

Sylvan’s voice entered his mind like a breeze through leaves.

You were born when I was born. You breathed when I breathed. You are my other half, Thalen Cedarheart.

He swallowed hard. “And the rot?”

Sylvan’s green eyes dimmed.

It is not rot. It is memory dying. And if memory dies… so do we.

A tremor ran through the forest. The vines around Sylvan tightened protectively.

The others are awakening, Sylvan murmured. The bond calls to them. The world calls to them.

Thalen stood, heart pounding. “Then we must go.”

Sylvan unfurled its wings, and the forest bowed as the twin souls rose into the night.


Aeris Galewing felt the change before she saw it. The winds shifted, spiraling upward in a column of shimmering air. Her lungs tightened, her vision blurred, and for a moment she felt weightless — as if her body remembered how to be something else.

Aetherius descended through the clouds, runes glowing along its parchment‑colored scales. The dragon’s presence calmed the storm inside her chest. She felt the bond lock into place — a sensation like a forgotten melody returning.

“You’re real,” she whispered, though she had always known.

Aetherius lowered its head, touching its snout to her forehead.

We were born together, Aeris. You are the breath I cannot take. I am the thought you cannot speak.

Her knees weakened. “The sickness in the sky‑cities… is it because of me?”

Because of us, Aetherius corrected gently. Our bond was dormant too long. The winds are confused. The stars are remembering things they should not.

Aeris looked north, where the constellations flickered like dying embers.

“Then we fly.”

Aetherius lifted her onto its back, and together they soared into the night, leaving silver trails across the sky.


Deep beneath the earth, Rhyla Mosswhisper reached the Heartstone Cavern just as the tremors began. Crystals shattered along the walls, their light dimming to ash. The ground split open, revealing a chamber she had never seen — a chamber that pulsed with molten light.

Gaia emerged from the fissure like a mountain waking from sleep.

The dragon’s scales glowed with the colors of stone and soil, and its eyes burned with ancient memory. Rhyla felt her bones resonate with its presence. Her breath synced with its rumbling exhale.

“You came,” she whispered.

Gaia’s voice echoed through her mind like shifting tectonic plates.

We are one, Rhyla. When the earth remembers, I remember. When the earth forgets… we both fall.

Rhyla touched the dragon’s snout, feeling the warmth of magma beneath the scales.

“The crystals are dying.”

Because the world is losing its story, Gaia replied. And something is stealing the ending.

The cavern shook violently. Dust rained from the ceiling.

We must go south, Gaia said. To the place where the first stones were laid. The others will meet us there.

Rhyla climbed onto Gaia’s back, and together they plunged into the tunnels, leaving the dying crystals behind.


Lunessa Tidecaller stood on the shoreline, staring at the dark water. The sea had always been her comfort, her teacher, her home. But now it felt foreign — as if the tides themselves were grieving.

A ripple broke the surface.

Then another.

Then a massive shape rose from the depths, water cascading off iridescent blue scales. Aquatica emerged with a roar that shook the shoreline, its wings unfurling like the fins of a colossal leviathan.

Lunessa felt her heart stop.

Her skin shimmered with the same blue glow as the dragon’s scales. Her breath synced with the rhythm of the waves. She felt the bond snap into place — a tidal pull deep in her chest.

“Aquatica,” she whispered.

The dragon’s voice flowed into her mind like a current.

Little tidecaller… you are my other half. And the sea is forgetting us.

Lunessa’s eyes filled with tears. “The monolith I found… it erased itself from my mind.”

Not erased, Aquatica corrected. Devoured.

The sea surged behind them, waves rising unnaturally high.

We must go east, Aquatica said. To the place where the moon first touched the water.

Lunessa climbed onto the dragon’s back, and together they dove beneath the waves.


Kael Duskbane moved through the Underworld tunnels with silent precision, his obsidian skin blending into the shadows. He felt the presence before he saw it — a cold ripple in the air, a whisper of darkness that matched the rhythm of his own heartbeat.

Umbra stepped from the shadows like a living eclipse.

The dragon’s violet eyes glowed with predatory intelligence. Its scales absorbed the torchlight, swallowing it whole. Kael felt the bond snap into place — a surge of shadow through his veins, a clarity he had never known.

Umbra circled him, its tail brushing the stone.

You have hunted alone too long, Kael, the dragon whispered into his mind. We were born together. We kill together.

Kael’s jaw tightened. “The prophecy speaks of an Astral Architect.”

Shallock Izladar, Umbra hissed. A name the world is trying to forget. A name someone is trying to erase.

Kael nodded. “Then we hunt the Remnants.”

Umbra’s wings unfurled, shadows spilling across the cavern.

And we start at #Wrymreach.


High above the world, in the luminous forests of the High Realm, Seraphyne felt the stars pulse beneath her skin. She staggered, gripping a crystalline tree for support. Her vision blurred, filled with swirling constellations.

A beam of starlight struck the clearing.

Astraia descended in a cascade of shimmering cosmic dust, wings glowing with iridescent light. The dragon’s presence filled the air with warmth and gravity, as if the cosmos itself had taken form.

Seraphyne fell to her knees.

Astraia lowered its head, touching its forehead to hers.

We are one soul split by the moon’s sorrow, the dragon whispered. And the stars are crying out. The prison weakens. The Architect stirs.

Seraphyne’s breath trembled. “Then we must go.”

To #Wrymreach, Astraia said. Where the sun’s anger and the moon’s tear first collided. Where the King of Dragons and Queen of Elves once stood together.

Seraphyne climbed onto Astraia’s back, and the dragon launched into the sky, leaving a trail of starlight behind.


Across the world, six dragons and six elves moved toward the same place.

Toward the abyss where reality thinned.

Toward the birthplace of their kind.

Toward #Wrymreach.

And deep within that chasm, something ancient stirred — a voice half‑formed, a memory refusing to die.

I… remember… me…

The second seal cracked.

The world shuddered.

Act II ends.

#WrymWretch Chapter One — The Shore Between Worlds

The bottles woke him before the light did.

They rolled across the cabin floor in a clattering, off‑key xylophone of glass and regret, chiming with every swell of the sea. The old boat rocked gently, as if trying to soothe him, but the motion only stirred the thunder in his skull. He lay there a moment, eyes half‑open, watching the ceiling beams sway like slow pendulums. The air smelled of salt, rum, and the faint ghost of last night’s pipe smoke.

The walls around him were crowded with the artifacts of a life lived in restless devotion: sketches pinned beside foreign poems, photographs curling at the edges, maps annotated in a hurried hand. Shelves sagged under the weight of books from ports he barely remembered leaving. Cabinets rattled with jars of spices and teas collected like talismans from far‑off markets. Every inch of the cabin carried the evidence of a man who had never learned to travel lightly.

He exhaled, long and ragged, and only then noticed the bottle still in his hand. A few amber drops clung to the bottom like stubborn memories. He set it aside and pushed himself upright, the familiar ache of a seasoned hangover settling behind his eyes. This was not the clumsy rise of a novice drunk; this was the practiced, almost graceful ascent of a man who had greeted many dawns in this exact condition.

The light outside was shifting. Thin threads of orange stretched across the porthole, weaving themselves into the gold of early morning. He blinked at it, squinting as though the sun were an adversary he had not prepared to face.

With a soft groan, he stood and made his way through the cluttered cabin, stepping over scattered notes and half‑finished sketches. The stairs creaked beneath his weight as he climbed to the deck.

The world greeted him with a hush.

The sea was calm, a pane of molten gold reflecting the newborn sky. The shoreline lay not far off—a quiet crescent of pale sand, untouched and waiting. He stared at it with bleary, unfocused eyes, as though trying to remember why it mattered. Something in him stirred. A pull. A promise he couldn’t name.

Before he could think better of it, he swung himself over the rail and dropped into the dinghy tied alongside. The small boat rocked, then steadied. He took up the oars with the muscle memory of a man who had crossed many uncertain waters, and began to row.

The journey was short, but each stroke felt like a negotiation with the remnants of last night’s rum. The dinghy slid onto the sand with a soft, wet squelch. He stepped out, boots sinking slightly into the cool grit.

He walked toward the place where the tide kissed the shore—a narrow, shimmering boundary between sea and land. The air there felt different, charged with something he couldn’t name. He paused, letting the waves lap at his ankles, letting the morning settle around him.

For a moment, he simply stood in that liminal space, neither fully arrived nor fully departed.

A man between worlds.

A man on the edge of remembering something important.

And the day, still young and golden, waited to see what he would do next.

Chapter Two — The Princess on the Shore

His feet sank into the sand with a wet, sucking sound, each step a slow negotiation with the earth. My toes—his toes—had long since grown rough from the constant embrace of salt and grit, the sea forever sculpting them into something less human and more… tidal. He trudged forward with the heavy, deliberate gait of a man who had only recently remembered he possessed legs at all. The tide had retreated moments before, leaving the beach slick and gleaming, as though he were walking across the exposed ribs of the ocean itself.

Then came the voice.

“Alexi!”

Smooth. Sweet. Too clean for this wild place. It drifted over the tropical wind like a ribbon of perfume—synthetic pineapple and coconut, the unmistakable scent of someone who had never once applied her own sunscreen. Even the breeze carried the memory of hands that had rubbed lotion into royal shoulders.

He turned toward it, blinking through the haze of rum and morning light. And then—because instinct is faster than thought—he rushed toward the sound.

Well, “rushed” is generous. He lurched. He zigzagged. He performed what future scholars might generously describe as a bold and experimental interpretation of running. But he moved with purpose, and that counts for something.

He stopped a little too late.

Which is to say: he didn’t stop at all.

He collided with her—a small, startled figure in a pale dress—and the two of them went down in a tangle of limbs, sand, and royal disbelief. Her dress, once pristine, now bore the full signature of the beach. And some say his hands, in the chaos of the fall, traveled further than any man’s had before—for she was a princess, after all, and princesses are not accustomed to being tackled by hungover sailors at dawn.

He scrambled backward, found his balance, lost it again, and collapsed into something that might have been a bow, or a grovel, or a man attempting to fold himself into a shape the gods would find amusing.

“Your Highness,” he managed, though it came out sounding more like “Yer Hine‑ness.”

She sat up, brushing sand from her sleeves with the resigned grace of someone who had long ago accepted that her life would never be normal. Her eyes—sharp, ocean‑colored, and far too awake for this hour—fixed on him.

“Alexi,” she said, “I bring a warning.”

Of course she did. Warnings always arrive when a man is least prepared to receive them. Even the waves seemed to pause, listening.

“My father’s debts have grown too great,” she continued. “He has sold his halls to the Great British Tea Company. And now the Lord of Tea has set his eyes on the ocean.”

Alexi blinked. “Tea has a lord.”

“Everything has a lord,” she said. “But this one… he is hungry. He wants every ship, every vessel, every soul that sails. He wants to turn the ocean into his fleet. He will drain the empire, and then the people, until all that remains is a wasteland of scraps and ash.”

The wind shifted. The scent of pineapple faded. Something colder took its place.

Alexi felt the weight settle on him—not the weight of her words, but the weight of what they meant. The world tilting. The tide pulling back. The kind of moment a man remembers long after he has forgotten his own name.

He imagined a life without the sea.

A life where no one knew the warm blush of sunrise over open water.  
Where no one tasted the salt on the rim of a bottle weathered by storms.  
Where no one sang drunken hymns to the tide, praising her like a lover who always returned.  
Where the ocean was not a wild, living thing—but a factory, a fleet, a machine.

He felt it like a punch to the ribs.

And somewhere deep inside him—somewhere older than memory, older than the man he had become—something stirred. Something that knew the sea was not meant to be owned.

The princess watched him, waiting.

The waves whispered their own warning.

And I—who am him and not him, who walk beside him and inside him—felt the first tremor of the story that would swallow us whole.
