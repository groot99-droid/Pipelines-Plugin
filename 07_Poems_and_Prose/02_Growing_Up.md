# Growing Up

**Type:** Poem  
**Source:** `Master_Volume_1`, lines 3565–3598  
**Text:** complete and verbatim — nothing cut, condensed, or rewritten.  

---
 
 Growing Up

Growing up is realizing that no one dies of old age.  
Just another fairytale; they don’t mention their pain.  
But why? Youth clings to illusion and fades faster than it,  
and with its passing you see those who died are no different than you and me.  
It is sad, but it is true; that is why we give the passing their due—  
we lay them in boxes their lives’ worth can barely afford.  
Bountiful blessings fall upon deaf ears, past curses forgotten from the years,  
loved ones who never knew them look teary-eyed down at a stranger.  
Go on, pray to some God born from a manger; no plea will bring them back.  
Once the light is extinguished, there’s no coming back.  
Death is neither sad nor happy; like life, it is the tool by which we measure.  
Every point has an end, or else it defies its premise; every life has a death that makes you live.  
So I learned to measure life by its textures: peanuts in bobbing in a cold Coke, baseball on the weekends, the faint smell of dirt under his nails.  
Saturdays were baseball on the tv, the crack of a bat, him calling out names like  he knew the players.  
He built raised beds the way some men pray—measured, squared, patient—  
each seed tucked in like a promise he could keep; soil under his nails smelled of time.  
His office was a pile of paper and tiny things, a life in clutter;  
when I was small I called him an archivist, later I called it what it was: hoarding.  
Still, those piles were his language—how he said he mattered.  
He loved in small rituals: peanuts in Coke, a game on the radio, a row of tomatoes.  
For it is simple to see that life is full of what you look for—  
not why for or who for, but the looking you do when you don’t see.  
Through each breath you are living life, and through each thought of the future you are closing in on death.  
No sermon will bring back the ordinary; only the echo of habit remains.  
So plant your beds, keep your odd comforts, learn the geometry of a double play,  
notice the textures—the fizz, the crack, the dirt, the shuffle of paper—and hold them close.  
Only you can decide what your last breath will look like.  
Make it beautiful, make it memorable, make it a… CRESCENDO!!!
