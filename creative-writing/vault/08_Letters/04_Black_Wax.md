# Black Wax

> *A cylinder is addressed to its slot, and not to a name. The slot is not a name. What is put in a slot is the slot's.*
> Ordinance of the Sovereign Sourdough Council, on the post-loft and the station slots

---

## I. A house of the Gallery, to the holder of slot forty
*The eve of the Feast of the First Loaf, November 1788. Black wax, a fleck of gold leaf pressed into it.*

To the slot,

You will forgive the paper. Its edge was gilt, and a servant has been at it with a knife. Badly. A house cannot write on anything else, and a house does not wish to be known by its edges.

We write on a small matter.

There is a man of the dish pit, a Plongeur by birth, called Bastien Leclou. On the third night of this month he stood in Gallows Square and counted the Hearth's dead aloud. He reached a number the Council has not published. On the fifth night, in the Tavern, he said a Council name without its title. Those are the whole of it. We do not add to them, because a house does not need to.

You will find with this a vial of Nightshade Glaze. You will know what is done with it. A house does not write such things.

Our terms are three.

Before the Feast, so that supper is not disturbed.
Not on a Thursday. The Chancellor dines on Thursdays.
Quietly. We are giving the Feast.

We enclose the card so you understand the evening we are protecting:

*Cod cheeks in white butter. The First Loaf, gilt. A swan of spun sugar. Pears in the Abbey's wine. The cloches lifted at the eighth bell.*

What is one platter, against a Hall?

For your trouble, your platter will be registered for one year and your tariff paid to the token. You may lift your own cloche at dawn without fear. If you want the Morning Ferry, there is a place on it. We cannot raise you. We can keep you.

Answer in the same wax. Do not write the name back. The bird knows its loft.

A house is not cruel. A house is only kept.

---

## II. The holder of slot forty, to the house that did not sign
*The same night, after the dusk loaf. In the same wax.*

To the house,

The bird came in late, after the seating, which no bird should. Nobody logged it. Nobody logs birds.

I am the old Apprenti. You know the office even if you don't know the man. L'Apprenti is the one who watches, and the disgrace of the office is speaking. In forty-one years I have not spoken once at my bench. They forbade my mouth. They forgot my hand.

So I will use the hand.

I have watched the bake. I have watched the Hall at the seating, every loaf called and every cloche set down. I have watched the Square on the mornings of the Fire: the cloches, the music, the bell, the supper after. I keep a list of everything I have watched. It is longer than any list of things I have done.

My tariff is three weeks behind. The Ledger-Clerk has the stamp inked. I have seen the word on other men's platters, and I have watched what the Council stops feeding.

What is a man who only watches, if not a knife no one has picked up?

You have picked me up. I won't pretend I'm sorry. I want the stamp off my platter. I want to lift my cloche at dawn and find my own name under it. That is the whole of why. I will not dress it as anything better.

I know the man you mean. I will not write him back.

I will do the thing you ask, to the one you name.

Keep the ferry place. I have never been off this rock, and I am too old to learn the water.

You have bought a hand. You have not bought a witness. I was always both.

## III. Bastien Leclou, to his sister at the dish pit
*The night before the Feast, 1788. Folded twice, with the comb inside.*

Agathe,

I'm putting the comb in with this, so you can't tell me again it isn't finished. It is. It's cod bone, from the big one off the Flats last spring. I've been rubbing it down with sand for a year because I wanted the teeth even. They aren't. Wear it anyway.

I've been frightened all week and I can't tell you of what. Nobody has said anything to me. Nobody has done anything to me. Maybe that's it.

You know what I do at dusk. The Aboyeur carries the loaf down the Hall and sets it on the empty platter nearest the Hearth, for a name under seal, and the night starts from that loaf. Then I stand outside the great doors and say the Unregistered names out loud, every one I know. There is one loaf I can't say a name for. I say the rest.

Somebody has to. The board doesn't. The ledger doesn't. The Hall calls only the names that are paid.

And the count. I know they heard me count. I'd do it again. The Hearth has taken more than its number, and a number isn't a crime just because the Council won't print it.

Who counts the counters?

Here's a strange thing, and a good one. The old Apprenti, the one who's watched from his bench for forty years and never said a word, has started watching me. In the Square. In the Tavern while I mend the chairs. At the doors at dusk. I think he's the only one in the Hall who sees me. I nodded to him on the stair. He didn't nod back, but he can't, can he. It's his office.

Kneel on the seal, Agathe, because they make you.
Kneel on the seal, and count while you kneel.
Kneel on the seal, and don't let them tell you the number.

I'll come down after the Feast with whatever they scrape off the plates. Cod cheeks, if the gallery leaves any. It never does.

They can take the number off the board. They cannot take it out of my mouth.

Your brother,
Bastien

## IV. The Committee of the Common Loaf, to the holder of slot forty
*March 1793, the week before the spring ferry. Black wax. The Committee found no other in the loft.*

Citizen of the slot,

The Committee of the Common Loaf, sitting in the Hall, has taken into common keeping the loft of the citizen formerly of the house of Maillard.

In it the Committee found: one bird, still wearing black wax on its ring. The house's copy-draft of a commission, November 1788, naming Bastien Leclou of the dish pit. And an answer in the same wax, unsigned, from a slot. It names no one. It says: *I will do the thing you ask, to the one you name.*

A slot that has answered once will answer again.

Bastien Leclou was found at his station in the dish pit at the dawn bell on the Feast-day, with his hands still in the water. The Hall ruled that he had died Unregistered. It fined his platter the cost of his own burial. His sister paid it on her knees, at the rate a Plongeur is paid, over two years.

The Committee encloses his last letter, so that you know what kind of house it is.

The citizen formerly Maillard is charged with these:

Gold leaf on the cuffs, in a hungry year.
White bread on his table in the black year.
The keeping of a loft.
Feeding the loft's birds by hand every morning, and giving them names. One of them is *Douce*. The Committee lists it because it was found in his own hand, and the Committee lists everything it finds.

The Committee has taken into common keeping, from the Apothecary's register, the Nightshade Glaze the house left behind. A vial comes with this. You will know what is done with it.

Our terms are three.

After the Supper of the Equal Loaf, so that supper is not disturbed.
Before the spring ferry brings the Razor, so that the Committee is not obliged to share him with the Square.
Quietly. We are giving the Supper.

The Committee holds the Counting-House ledgers now. A ledger is read from the top.

The Hall is ours. The forms were always the Hall's.

*For the Committee, the Secretary, who knelt in the dish pit*

*In my own hand, and not the Committee's: the citizen enclosed was my brother. The comb came folded in his letter. I have worn it five years. I want it back. Send it with your answer. That is my surety and not the Committee's. I am not asking you whose hand it was in 1788. I am asking you to be a hand now.*

## V. The holder of slot forty, to Bastien Leclou
*March 1793, the night the bird came. No wax. Folded once and left in the slot.*

Bastien,

I'm writing to you because you are the only one on this rock who can't read it.

The bird is on the sill. It's the same bird. I would know it anywhere: it holds its left foot up when it waits. It flew to the only slot it knows, and the only slot it knows is mine.

Your letter came in with it. I have read it nine times. *I think he's the only one in the Hall who sees me.*

You were right. I was the only one who saw you. That is how I found you.

I watched you at the doors. I watched you at the chairs. I watched you on the stair when you nodded to me. I couldn't nod back. You thought it was my office. It was the vial in my apron.

I said I wouldn't write you back. I am writing you back now, all of it, to a slot that cannot answer.

The Feast-eve. The vial. The dish pit at the dawn bell. Your hands in the water. That is all of it I will set down, and it is more than the Hall ever did.

The house kept me for a year, exactly as it promised, to the token. Then it stopped. In 1790 the Commons stamped my platter Unregistered anyway, for having been kept. I turned the ferry down because I had never been off the rock. That was the reason I gave. The truth is meaner. The Hall is the one place where my watching was ever worth anything, and I would not give it up.

Your sister has your comb in her hair. She wants it back. She is asking for it from the hand that killed you, and she says she isn't asking whose hand.

Is it justice if the same hand does it twice?
Is it penance if I'm paid?
Is it anything at all, if no one ever reads it?

One bird. One slot. One hand.
Two masters. Two waxes. One hand.

I could refuse. Then the Committee reads the ledger from the top, and my answer of 1788 is near the top. I could take the ferry at last. It casts off at dawn, and the Razor comes in on the next one. I could do nothing. I have done nothing for forty-one years, and I know what nothing costs: it costs exactly what someone else decides.

So I will choose this time, and not be chosen. I will choose the house that bought me. I will choose your sister's surety. I will choose a man who names his birds, because a Committee told me to. I don't know if that makes me better or worse. It makes me the same.

At dusk, after the loaf, I'll seal the comb in the cylinder and write the answer under it. The same words. I have only ever had the one answer:

*I will do the thing you ask, to the one you name.*

This letter stays in the slot. It's yours.

A slot does not choose what is put in it. A hand does. I have chosen twice.
