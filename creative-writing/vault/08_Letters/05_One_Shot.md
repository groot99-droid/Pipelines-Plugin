# One Shot

> *One shot, and the whole square goes quiet.*
> said in HollowMere

---

## I. The Ranger, to the Magistrate of the Territory
*Monday, 4 December 1893. Hale Barn, under the pelt. The goat watching.*

Sir,

You signed my paper standing up, in a room with one chair, and you did not look at me while the ink dried. One arrest, once, in the name of the Territory and not of the Company. I have carried it folded in my coat since. I am not going to send you this. I am writing it because your paper says a thing I did this morning cannot be done, and somebody ought to be told so in writing.

The bow I carry slung. The bolts I carry in the quiver. One I carry apart, in a loop on my belt, its fletching tied with white thread so that I cannot use it by mistake. That one is your paper. The rest is wolves.

I have not used it. I have been in HollowMere longer than the talk that says the town is waiting for someone like me, and I have not used it once.

On Saturday night I was out past the Gallows Wood edge, where the planks stop. I go out there to count. The pack was at the den and along the far side of the Fenwood, and I counted them there by my torch. There were no wolves in the square. Not one.

At the foot of the Rise somebody cried that there were. The Watchman left the wash-house door to go and meet them, because that is what a lantern is for. Hattie died behind the door he left.

In the morning at the Bell a voice said it plainly: there were no wolves in the square last night. It said it louder than he had told the truth. It was true. I knew it was true better than anyone on that square. And it was used. It came down on his heel, and the square looked at his heel, and the voice said one more word, and the square took the word up until it belonged to nobody.

They did not give him the rope. They gave him three months' scrip, and they took his lantern off him on the table beside the thread, and they stood him down.

I said nothing. I stood at the back with your paper in my coat and said nothing.

In November I told the new priest I was more afraid of being right than of being wrong. He told me to wait. I waited.

Here is what being right looks like in HollowMere. Cuffs. The square. A meeting that opens on a face already closed. Then the beam at the Bell, and the crows. If I am right, your paper does that. If I am wrong, your paper does that too. The paper does not know the difference. Only the hand does.

Is a right still a right when it changes hands at a pump?

This morning the Watchman came past the pump without a lantern. You hear him before you see him; he drags his left heel. They took the light off him and he walks the lanes anyway, in the dark, where the wolves do not run from him, because he does not know what else he is for.

I did not give him my paper. I did not give him my name. I gave him the one shot.

The bow, and the white bolt, and nothing to go with it. I cut the loop off my belt and tied it to his sling, so he would carry it where I carried it. One, so that he would have to choose. He took them without a word. His heel went once on the stone, and he went on up the lane.

I kept the snare. I kept the torch for the den. The wolves were never what I needed a bow for.

The Territory gave me one arrest. I gave it to the only man in HollowMere who already knows what being wrong costs.

## II. The Watchman, to Hattie
*Wednesday, 6 December 1893. Before the Station Rush. No lantern.*

Hattie,

You paid me in sheets. You said it would be the first bed I'd had with no holes in it. It was. I still sleep in them. I sleep in them by day now, because the nights are not mine to sleep.

You can't read this. Nobody in this town will hear me say the word *door*, so I am saying it to you. Door.

I left yours. Somebody cried wolves in the square from the pines side, high, pitched to carry, and I looked at your door, and I looked at the dark, and I went. There were no wolves. You came in humming with the last basket, the tune with no words left in it, and you thought I was still on your step. I know that from the vote. I don't know it from you.

Three months' scrip. My lantern on the table beside the thread. Stood down. Nobody stands at any door now. I walk the lanes in the dark instead, and if you hear a heel, it's mine.

There are two voices I have listened for at the Bell every morning since. The one from the pines side. The one at the table that said *rope*. I would know either of them in the dark. I don't know either of them by day. By day every voice at the Bell is loud, and every face asks who did it.

On Monday the Ranger put her bow in my hands at the pump, and one bolt with it, tied with white thread, in a loop she had cut off her own belt. She did not say what it was for. She didn't need to. It was the arrest. I meant it for a voice. I will not write which, because I am not sure, and I was sure once at your door, and I was sure of the wrong thing.

This is last night. It took six seconds. That is as long as a wolf wants you. Then it goes home.

The last hour before light. Three lanterns on the square gone out. The fourth still burning, over the chalk and the well, because she lights it last. Agnes coming down the Row in the dark to be first at the stone, the way she is every morning, so she can tell the Row the name before it asks.

The wolf came over the pump. They hunt the edge of the light, the town says, and never cross it. The mere-rot makes them bold. This one was bold.

**One.** I knew it was a wolf. Nobody had to cry it.

**Two.** The bolt on my sling was the arrest, and the arrest was for a voice.

**Three.** It had Agnes by the arm. Through the wool, through to the bone, shaking her the way a dog shakes a rag. Her blood on the pump handle. She did not scream. She said, *Oh*.

**Four.** Last time the wolf was a word behind me, and I ran toward it. This time it was in front of me, and there was nowhere to go that wasn't away from her.

**Five.** I spent it.

**Six.** One shot, and the whole square went quiet.

It lay at the foot of the pump with the white thread standing up out of its neck, and its jaw still shut on her sleeve. I opened the jaw with my hands. There was grey wool in the teeth, and some of her under the wool. Doc Mallory came down in a nightshirt and took her arm, and would not let me watch what the Doc did with it.

Agnes lives. When she could talk, the first thing she said was not about the wolf. She had not been up yet. The Row would have no name today. She asked me to go up and read the stone for her. I didn't. I don't read stones.

The loop on the sling is empty. There is no arrest left on that paper. Nobody will be dragged to the square on my word. The voice is still loud at the Bell. I still know it in the dark.

Was it the right shot, Hattie, or only the sure one?

I couldn't keep your door. I couldn't keep my lantern. I kept the bolt for a voice, and I gave it to a wolf.

Once I ran from your door toward wolves that weren't there. Last night one was there, and I stood still and shot it. Neither one opens your door.

## III. The Gamekeeper, to Aldous Vane
*Wednesday, 6 December 1893. Dusk. Hale Barn, the piece across my knees.*

Vane,

You paid me to keep the wood. My wages stopped with you. I keep it anyway. I have not gone to the Company window to ask, and nothing has come to the barn. You'll not read this. Dead men pay nothing and read less. You are the only one I answer to, and the only one who can't answer back, and that suits me.

The piece is across my knees. You paid for it out of my first wage, and I have oiled it every night since. It has one shot in it that is not for the wood. It is for the end. If they come for me, I'll not go alone. I made that bargain with myself the day you died, and I made it in your name.

At first light I was at the Gallows Wood edge, where the planks start, and I heard one shot from the square. One shot, and the whole square went quiet. The pines went quiet with it. For the length of that quiet I thought it was mine. I thought I had fallen and not known it, and the piece had gone off at somebody on its own account. I put my hand on my own chest to see if I was there. I was. The piece was cold.

It was a wolf. The Watchman, with the Ranger's bow and no light, at the pump, and Agnes's arm in its mouth.

I went down and took it off the square. It is one of the den. I know them the way you knew your crates. Tonight I'll go past the den and count, and the count will be one short, and the short one is on the town's gallows.

I hung it from the beam at the Bell. That is the wood's custom with a varmint: you hang it where the rest can smell it. Nobody stopped me. By noon the crows had it. The eyes first. Then the soft of the belly, opened from below, the crows going in and out of it as if it were a door. The white thread still in its neck. Grey wool still in its teeth. I left the wool.

So the beam at the Bell had a wolf on it this week, and not a man. It is the only thing I have seen on that beam that the whole town agreed was guilty.

At the Bell this morning there was talk. The wood came into the square, they said, and the wood has a keeper. Nobody put up a hand. Nobody said rope. But I felt the shot in the piece lift, the way a dog lifts its head at its name.

Here is the arithmetic, Vane, since you liked figures. If they had stood me on the trap this morning, I would have had my one shot. I would have had to take somebody. I do not know whom.

There is one voice at the Bell that has been louder than the rest since October. I could find it in the dark. I have found worse in the dark. But finding a thing in the dark is not the same as its being the one who came.

The new priest came out to the barn in November and sat with me and the goat. I told him about the one shot and where I was afraid it would go. He said, "Be sure." Nothing else. He preaches at dusk, I hear. I have not gone to hear it. The wood's not Christian. I'd be lying to it.

Be sure. The Watchman could be sure. A wolf on a woman's arm is sure. What is a face by lantern, at the end, when you are already going?

What did you pay me to be sure of, Vane: the wood, or the town?

Who keeps the keeper's wood when the keeper falls?

I keep the wood. I keep the count. I keep the one shot.

So here is my order, written down, since there is nobody left to give me one. If I fall to a face I have seen by my own lantern, coming at me, I'll take it. That is the one who came. If I fall in the dark, the shot goes into the pines. The wood gets every shot I can't be sure of.

I have written it. I'll keep to it while the piece is mine to keep. I have kept your wood longer than I have kept most things.

One bolt in a wolf is the only sure shot this town has had. The rest of us carry ours like a vote with one hand.
