# Five Letters at the Fork

> *Mail that has been read shall be resealed and stamped. The stamp is not evidence of reading.*
> Articles of Incorporation and Bylaws of the Thorne & Expanse Line, Art. VI §2

---

## I. The Baker, to Aldous Vane
*Thursday, 26 October 1893. After the oven is raked.*

Dear Aldous,

I am not writing this for you. You are eight nights dead by the town's count, or the wire's, and no ferry I know of carries letters to the Hearth. I am writing because the crock did not rise on the hour tonight, and I need something in this house to answer me.

Up before anyone. Down after everyone. In between, I have been deciding things.

No flour came Friday last. None Saturday. None Monday. None today. The last sack went into the dead's loaves and the living's halves, and I have the sweepings. I don't know what comes next. That is still the first thing I knead into everything: I don't know.

On the Saturday I cut the mourners' loaves to halves so the dead could keep their three. I did it with the scraper in one stroke. Nobody thanked me. Nobody stopped me. That is what I have to tell you, Aldous. A scraper is a law if nobody stops it.

I cut the loaves. I set the plates. I chose the halves.

Who gave me that? You did, with the cup.

The gold coin is still behind the crock, in the place a hand can go. Gold asks where it came from. Tonight there are two. I did not put the second one there. I counted them on the island's hour and again on the Company's, and there were two both times.

Last night there was a sack on the cold-room step with the stencil scraped off it, and no note, and a boot in the frost. I could not tell you how long a boot, because I swept before I looked. I sweep everything. Whose boot? I write that I don't know, and this time I mean it, and I do not like how little it costs me to mean it.

At the Shift the Clerk stood at my counter with his hat on and asked for my book: who bought which loaf, to set against the Indebted. The Company does not recognise my table, he said, but it would like to read it. I said the book was in the oven room. It was in my apron.

So here is the whole of it. I could bake to his list. I could bake to my own. I could take what comes to the back step in the dark and pass it on in the crust, the way I pass the papers, folded twice, laid flat. Every road out of this kitchen goes through my hands.

People used to ask whether I came inland for you or for the cup. I still don't know. I know what you did with the tin money and the custom. You built one thing in HollowMere the Company could not tax. You left it with me. You did not leave me the flour.

Is it freedom, if there is only one sack of it?

I have decided. I did it the way you do a thing you have already decided, in one stroke. The step is empty now. The book is not in my apron. I fed the crock on the island's hour and it rose, late, and I took that for an answer, because it was the only one I was given.

The island taught me to feed the dead. Hunger taught me that whoever holds the scraper holds the town.

## II. The Postmaster, to Hester
*Tuesday, 7 November 1893. The kettle still warm.*

Dear Hester,

You can't read this, and that is why it is to you.

Every letter I ever sent home, the neighbour's girl read to you aloud at your own table. So this one I will not send. It is the only letter in this office nobody will open. I have read every other.

The Company bought me a kettle. It is in the Articles: kept for the purpose at the Company's expense, which expense is charged to the mails. So the town pays for the steam that opens it. I have held every envelope in HollowMere over that spout, and steam has taught me three things. Who owes. Who lies. Who is leaving.

I know which letters go east and come back with no answer. I know how many Ivy Cotter wrote, and how few were answered. I know whose name Arthur's brother signs at the bottom of his letters to his Ma, and I will not write it, not even to you, who could not tell it from a fence-rail. I reseal them all. I stamp them. The stamp is not evidence of reading. The Articles say that too. They thought of everything but me.

This morning there was black wax in the second bag.

Black wax is the Company's. It is exempt from the right to read. I am to deliver it unopened and unsteamed into the hand it names, and not write down that I did. In October one went past me altogether, straight to the Conductor. This one came through my bag, and lay on my sorting table, and the kettle was already on.

I steamed it.

One line, printed: *Burn the Letter & Keep Your Mundane Life, OR Accept the Baron's Contract.*

The name on the outside is someone I have sold stamps to. I have counted change into that hand. I know how it holds a pen. I will not write the name, Hester. Not even here.

If the stamp is not evidence of reading, what is a reseal evidence of?

Here is my confession, since this is the only page in the Grant nobody will steam. What I have read is worth more than my wage. I have known that since the summer. Once, in September, I let a man see across the counter that I knew what was in his letter. Only my eyes. He bought a second stamp he did not need, and paid for it in coin, and I let him. That was all. That was the first rung.

When we were small I read your letters to you before you heard them. I knew your news first. I held it on my tongue for a whole breath while you looked up at me, waiting, and I liked that breath more than I should have. You trusted the reader. Should anybody?

It is resealed. The wax took the iron cleanly; you could not tell. It is out of my hands now. The kettle is back on the stove, where the Company keeps it.

Every letter in this town is read but this one, and this is the only one that tells the truth.

## III. The Undertaker, to Father Bray
*Sunday, 19 November 1893. Maud on the table.*

Dear Father,

Either you are in the ground somewhere I did not put you, or you are somewhere a letter from me cannot follow. The Articles say I receive every body within the Grant, by whatever cause and wherever found, the bed of the mere included. I never received yours. That is the first thing I have to confess, and it is not even mine.

I write to you because you were the last man who sat with me over a body and did not ask what it was worth.

Maud is on my table. Doris laid her out at four and went home without her supper. I have done the inventory to the last button, as the Company wants it entered against the account. One darning needle, in her collar, threaded grey. A card of red wool. A card of brown. A thimble worn through at the crown. Eleven buttons, none of them off her own clothes; she kept the ones she cut from other people's mending in case they were wanted back. She took her pour at the Lantern as a wage, never as a gift. Last night she darned Bar's other cuff to match the first. She was square with everyone.

She had no household and no lodgers. Her effects are held thirty days, then sold, and the proceeds applied to the account. I priced the needle before I closed her eyes. I want you to know that. I want somebody to.

Pruett counts the coffins going out and I count them coming in, and we have not always agreed. In October my boy carried a paper reading *four* to Mrs Crumb's when she had three dead to bake for. I write the number before I have the name. That is the trade. I will not tell you who the others were. I don't know that I could.

Does a person become a number at my table, or were they one already, and the table only finds it out?

The Company would like a Chief Coroner: a man who writes the cause in a fair hand, so the Tribunal can read it and the account can close. Here is my difficulty, Father. Maud's name was on the stone with tomorrow's date. She came into the Lantern at dusk anyway, and darned, and asked for her Rotgut as a wage. I cannot write *the stone*. I cannot write *the cough* and look at her hands. I have left the line blank. A blank is the most honest thing a coroner could sign, and the Company does not take blanks.

Pig lead comes up in Car 2 every day for the railyard. Lead keeps a thing where you put it. I have been thinking about that, and I would rather not tell you why.

I measure everybody by eye. It is the habit of the trade, and I cannot stop it. Today I measured Bar coming in with his cap off. I measured my boy, who has grown. I measured the new Vicar in the chapel door, the way I measured you your first week, without meaning anything by it. I measured myself in the window glass after, and none of us came out a size I liked.

I have made up the box. I have entered what I entered.

You buried the dead for God. I bury them for the account. Neither of us was ever asked what they were worth, and only one of us wrote it down.

## IV. The Bartender, to the barman who went into the mere
*Monday, 20 November 1893. After closing. Low water at two minutes past nine.*

I wrote it twice. One goes in the stove.

### The first letter

You,

I don't know your name. Mrs Grieves carries you at the top of every page as a little lantern in ink, and nobody drinks on your line, so that is what I call you. I'm not writing this for you. I took your place behind the bar and never once asked what you would have done with it.

Maud darned my right cuff in October and the left one on Saturday, to match. Today I carried one end of her box to the back car. Both cuffs are mended now. I have to decide which hand.

On the Tuesday I stood the platform crew nine Rotgut for the strike. Mrs Grieves asked me whose strike. The one we're having, I said, and told her to put it on my own line. That night twelve men came to the back room for the Burial Club, and nobody was buried. Len had sung Stan out at the back car at Dusk. Arthur's brother still pays a Rotgut on a closed line every week. A round is a vote you can drink. They drank it.

Who drowned you, the mere or the Company that named the saloon? You went in after its lantern. I'd like to think you were going after something of ours.

I'll open the back room Tuesday.

I'll leave your Liquid Courage on the slate, unpaid, the way she keeps it. Tuck says the lantern in the Fenwood is lit again. I don't ask who. A drummer told Mrs Grieves that every town at the end of a line drowns its barman sooner or later. She doesn't believe him. Neither do I.

A man who goes into the water for a lantern was never asked which way. I am being asked.

### The second letter

You,

I don't know your name. Mrs Grieves carries you at the top of every page as a little lantern in ink, and nobody drinks on your line, so that is what I call you. I'm not writing this for you. I took your place behind the bar and never once asked what you would have done with it.

Maud darned my right cuff in October and the left one on Saturday, to match. Today I carried one end of her box to the back car. Both cuffs are mended now. I have to decide which hand.

In October Frank said a thing about the girl Nell's fever, and I had him out the door before Mrs Grieves had her hand off the tap. A stool went, and a tooth. I found the tooth two days later under the rail, and I kept it. I don't know why. On the Wednesday I took two shells off the train. The dogs came past the shutters, and I cleaned the gun on the counter and did not look at them. They looked at me. The gun lives under the counter so I don't have to.

Who drowned you, and did anybody ever answer for it?

I've loaded what I loaded.

I'll leave your Liquid Courage on the slate, unpaid, the way she keeps it. Tuck says the lantern in the Fenwood is lit again. I don't ask who. A drummer told Mrs Grieves that every town at the end of a line drowns its barman sooner or later. She doesn't believe him. Neither do I.

A man who goes into the water for a lantern was never asked which way. I am being asked.

## V. The Company Clerk, to Ivy Cotter
*Friday, 15 December 1893. After the night wire.*

Dear Miss Cotter,

East or the back car, no letter of mine will reach you. I wrote *Settled. Departed by the Line* against your name. Somebody else wrote *Gone east*. They say a box went down the line with a thimble on a string in it. I have never asked which of us was right. You will not read this. That is why I can write it.

You are the only entry I ever made that I still read. *In arrears by the price of a lamp-wick.* You paid the wick. You sat in the dark to pay it.

Yesterday the Baron came in on the train, and on the platform he stopped being the Baron. I was at the window of the Telegraph Room with the key under my hand, and I wired it east before the Constable had his whistle out. That is what I am for.

Every bed slept in, and every bed that should have been. In person, in scrip, at noon. On the ninth, Vera's bed on Mill Row stopped being slept in. It is still on my round.

On the sixth I entered Ada Brook of Mill Row, third door, Indebted: two weeks, to the Mill at Midday. That week she lost a grey scarf at the Rush, her mother's, darned in red where the moth had it. She had paid ahead for ten wake loaves at Crumb's, which is the kind of thing you do when you think there is a week coming you won't be in. That night the key woke, and the Eastern Office asked IS ADA BROOK LIVING. I sent back ADA BROOK LIVING STOP AT MILL STOP STONE IS RUMOUR. I had sent her to the Mill myself, at noon. I had not seen her since.

Is a woman living because I wire that she is?

Today's train brought the writ. It is in the drawer, on Company paper, under the seal. It raises the Clerk to Heir to the Baron. The Articles let the Heir declare martial law and hold every power of the President, and keep the books besides. He signed it in his own hand in November, the week he came through with the dogs. It waited back east for him to stop.

He came through this office twice and looked at my rent book and never at me. He never wrote his name in any book of mine; the Company did that for him. My mother never said his name either. She said *the Line*, the way the women on Mill Row say *the weather*. The writ names the town where she kept rooms. I never put that town on any Company form. I have been through every one I ever filled in about myself, tonight, one by one. It is not there. The writ knows it anyway.

There is more than one thing in this town called an heir. I am only the one with paper.

Here is the rest. The rate went three to the dollar, then four, then five. I post the rent in scrip and the Company counts it in dollars, and between the board in the morning and the box at noon there is a gap a careful man could live in. I am a careful man. Nobody reads my books but me. From tomorrow, if I want it, nobody may.

I was never the cruel one, Miss Cotter. I was only the one who wrote it down. Is that worse? You would know. You sat in the dark while I wrote.

I have signed. It wanted my name either way.

You sat in the dark to pay me. I sit in the light and keep the books. The name you sign at midnight is who you are.
