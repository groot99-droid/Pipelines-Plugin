# Censored

> *Outgoing correspondence shall concern the writer's health, work and family. Names, numbers, places, weather, the condition of other persons, and any reference to the program shall be struck. Struck matter is lined through in the file copy and blacked in the copy sent. A letter that cannot be struck shall be held. The censor's mark is not a reply.*
> Grounds 99, Standing Order 14 (Correspondence)

## I. Foreman Hask, to his daughter
*Sunday, 19 October 1952. After the second roll. For Friday's train.*

Dear girl,

I am allowed to tell you about my health, my work, and my family. So.

My health is a cough I keep short. Short is the rule here for coughing. A long one gets you sent to the doctor. The doctor gives you the tonic, and the tonic gives you a longer one.

My work is the yard and the office. I give a man a crowbar and he gives me four timber, every day, and if he gives me three I write three. I keep the key to the office. I keep the yard. And I call the roll.

I have to tell you that part, because you asked what my work is. You were always a girl who asked twice.

At midnight the whistle goes, and the men have fifteen seconds to stand by their slots. Then I read the numbers out. Not names. Numbers. I am ███ on the rack myself, so I am not above them, only in front of them. I read out ███, ███, ███, and a man answers to each one, or he doesn't.

I'll tell you about one number, since numbers are what I have.

~~He came in with a neck like a bull and a laugh you could hear across the yard. He kept a comb in his breast pocket and combed his hair before every roll, as if the searchlight were a girl. By the third week I could count his ribs through his shirt from where I stand. By the fifth his skin had gone tight over his knuckles, white, like a glove a size too small. His cheeks went in until his face was the shape of what's under a face. He showed me his gums once, laughing, and touched a tooth, and it moved. His toes went black in his boots and he stood on them for roll anyway, because standing is the rule. At the end the hair came away in the comb and he went on combing. At the end he stopped answering to his number. He answered to the whistle, before I'd said anything, the way a dog lifts its head. Then one night his slot was empty and I said the number to nobody.~~

When a man doesn't answer, there are words for it. Here are the ones they let me write: transferred. Rested. Sent down. Gone to the Blade. The camp has a word for everything that happens to a man, and none of them is the thing.

My family is you. That is the only line in this letter nobody will touch.

It ██████ on the ridge Thursday. That is all I'll say about that.

I don't know who runs this place. I mean it the way a man means it when he has said it to himself a hundred nights. There are men on the rack who carry tales, and I don't know which ones, or to whom. I don't know whether the man I'd tell would be one of them. The telephone in the office is dead. It rang once last week. I picked it up. Nobody was on it, and nobody asked me afterward why I'd been in the office at that hour, which is worse than if they had. The ███████ ███████ does not need to ask. A dozen men in the mess have started humming together when the whistle goes, all at once, the same note, and I did not teach them that.

One more thing. It is not a number, so maybe it will reach you.

In the office drawer, under the payroll ledger with its torn page, there is an old freight tag. Card, gone soft. The ink has gone brown. It has one word on it: HOLLOWMERE. I asked about. Nobody here has heard of it. And separate from that, because I want to be honest about what I know and what I don't: there is an old railroad bed out past the ██████ ███████, over the █████████, and it goes ███. Nobody keeps it. The ties are mostly gone. It still goes.

I don't say the tag and the bed go together. I say a rail goes somewhere, and so does a tag.

If a rail goes somewhere, where does a man go who is called and doesn't answer? ~~Not down any rail. I have seen where. I have seen what he is when he gets there.~~

Don't write back about the rail. Write me about you. What you're reading, what you're cooking, whether the step is still loose.

I read the numbers out so nobody has to hear their own name. That is the kindest thing I do here, and it is not kind.

Your father

## II. Ada Kerrow, at the Forge, to her brother Carl
*Sunday, 16 November 1952. The hearth banked. For Friday's train.*

Carl,

Health. Work. Family. In that order, they tell me, as if that were the order.

Health: my hands are good. The left one burned in ████████ and healed shiny. I keep it out of the trough now. I keep the trough clean, too. A clean trough is the one thing in this place I can make true by Sunday.

Work: I am the forge. The warmest light in the camp, the men say, and they stand in the door to say it. They bring me ore from the seam, and I tell every one of them the same thing: "Three tin ore from the seam. I will make you a lantern for it." When the ore is good I tell them, "Good ore. This burns bright." It nearly always is. They pick it as if their lives were in it.

I name each lantern while I seal it. Under my breath, not out loud. Not their numbers. Names I make up. Bess. Little Hob. Jenny Wren. It's a habit, and I'm keeping it.

The men take the lanterns down. Some of the lanterns come back up. Lanterns issued: ███. Returned: ███. You see how it is. I am not allowed my own arithmetic.

So I'll say it the other way. There are ten billets on a rack. I have made a lantern for every man on a whole rack. Then for a whole rack again. Then again. Then again. I don't count the lanterns that come back. I count the ones that don't, the way you count steps in the dark, and I am past the end of more racks than I like.

And some come back that I wish hadn't.

~~Little Hob came up in the ore cart on Tuesday under a tarp, still lit, still hooked on the man I made it for. Two of his fingers were through the bail and would not come out of it. They were blue to the nail, and the skin of that hand had gone loose and white and soft, like a glove you've been washing in. His face had been in water a long time. I did not know him by his face. I knew him by the lantern. He'd scratched a mark on the base with a nail, a little house, I don't know why. They sent the lantern back to me to be cleaned. I cleaned it. That is my work.~~

~~Another came up dry. A man walked it up the ladder himself, which they say is luck. His ears were black. The tips of his fingers were black and hard, like the end of a burnt match. His lips were split and gone grey. He handed me the lantern and asked if he could warm his hands at my fire, and I said yes, and he held them out to it and could not feel it, and he kept them there anyway, smiling, because he thought he ought to.~~

There is a house on the row that my forge cannot warm a nail in. I took a hinge to it once. I don't say which house, and I don't go back.

Family: you.

They don't let me write about why I'm here, so I will write about you instead, and you can work out the rest. You sat in front of the ████████ ██ ███-████████ ██████████ in a pressed shirt. I know the shirt. I ironed it for you in ████. They read you a list, and you said one name off it, and the name was mine.

Did they ask you to say it, or did you offer?

I don't need an answer. I have had a long time at the anvil to write your answer for you, both ways. Both ways you were frightened, and both ways I am here.

I make a light for every man they send down. Who made one for me?

You did, once. Do you remember? The tin lantern from the dump, the one we soldered on the back step, that leaked oil down your sleeve. I still have it. They let me keep it because it doesn't work. I have never fixed it. I could. I'm the best smith in this camp. I haven't.

I don't forgive you. I'm not cursing you either. I haven't the oil to spare for it.

You gave them my name. I give the dark a light. Only one of us did the work we were sent for.

Ada

`PASSED. Struck as marked. CENSOR No. 4.`

## III. The Collier, at the kilns, to Mrs. ██████
*Sunday, 14 December 1952. Clamp lit. Not for any train.*

Dear Mrs. ██████,

You will not know my hand. I sold your son charcoal.

I am allowed to write about my health, my work and my family. My health is the kilns. My work is the kilns. I had no family but the men who bought from the kilns, and Tony was one of them. So this letter is within the rules. I have read them.

I burned on this slope before there was a fence on it. Before there were numbers. Your boy would come up the skid road at the end of a shift, black to the eyes, and buy a sack for your stove. He lit his lamp at the kiln mouth because, he said, my fire was cheaper than his matches. It was a joke. He made it every time. I laughed every time. That is how you know a man, Mrs. ██████. By what he says every time.

He went down into the ████ █████ one morning and he did not come up. You know that much. They told you "lost". Here is the rest.

When the fence came, men came with it, from ███ █████ ████, in good coats, with maps that were wrong. They needed someone who knew the old workings. I knew them. I showed them the way down. I did not tell them who was still in it.

Why didn't I? I have asked it every night since, at the clamp. Because they were paying. Because they would have found it anyway. Because I was afraid. Pick one. I have picked all three, on different nights.

In ████████ they took me down again, to show the new men the water. So I have been to his light. I'll set it down plain, because you are owed plain.

There is a refuge at the bottom of the old workings, with a table and a bunk. His lamp is on the table, and it is burning. Brass, wiped clean, the wick trimmed. It has been burning all this time.

The men in the bunks say nobody fills it. That's a story for the bunks. They fill it. Every man they send down to the water fills that lamp before he goes in. The oil is the camp's. I have seen it go down in the cans.

Tony is not there. I looked for him, Mrs. ██████. I will tell you that much for myself. I'd have known him by his coat, which was his father's and too big for him, and by his left hand, with the two fingers short from the saw before he ever went under. There is nothing to know him by. The bunk is empty under its dust. The dust on the table lies thick and unbroken round the lamp, as if no one had ever sat there. Whatever took him took all of him.

I did not go in. I stood on the ledge above the water with the new men, and I looked at what was there instead.

~~The water comes up to the sill of the refuge and stops there. At the edge of the light, where the lamp gives out, there are men crouched. Or there were men. They have gone long, as if something had pulled them out at both ends. White like bread left in the rain. The skin drawn tight to the bone, the ribs standing out like the slats of a crate, the nails gone off their fingers and the tips black with frost. One still has camp issue on, his number stitched on the breast, the shirt hanging off him like a flag off a pole. Their chests don't move. Then one of them heaves, once, a great drag of air, and goes still again. They don't look at us. They look at the lamp. All of them. The way you'd watch a door for somebody due home.~~

~~Those are the detail men. They filled his lamp, and went into the water, and that is what came back to the sill.~~

That is what sits round your son's light now. The camp keeps it burning, and they keep it company.

His name is on the board in the Workings, from the last shift before the camp. Chalk. Most of them can't read it. I can. I don't say it to anybody. Who would I tell? Who would let me? Who would they send down next, for asking?

A man came on the Friday train with his own chair, ███ █████████-███████. Friday last they took another off the platform in a good coat, and since then nobody asks anything about anything. That is the weather here. That is all the weather I'm allowed.

My work is the clamp. Every man who comes to the kilns, I tell him: "Three good stones for the clamp, and take a torch with you." And when he has set them, I tell him, "It will hold. Keep that torch up." I say it to every one of them, every time. Like Tony's joke. It is how they will know me.

I don't know if you're living. I don't know if this goes past the fence. I am writing it so that it is written somewhere: he was a man who made the same joke every time, and paid in coin, and his name was Tony, and his light is still down there.

It will hold. Keep that torch up. A name on a board at dawn is who you were.

`HELD. Not to be forwarded. Writer to be spoken to. CENSOR No. 4.
