# The Red Crust

> *A white crust under a red wash is a Gallery loaf pretending, and is to be cracked before the Square.*
> The Recipe Book of the Great Hearth, the Red Crust, 1791

---

## I. Aurèle, of the house of Sérigny, to Madame his mother
*Candlemas, 1793, the night of the reading. Sealed with a thumb of crumb, there being no wax for citizens.*

Madame,

You will have heard the bell. You will not have come down for it, so I will tell you what it rang for.

At the first bell after Candlemas the committee read the Ordinances aloud in the Hall of Platters, as directed. Every article, the struck ones too, so that the Hall would hear what had been struck. When the reading was done they called me to the Square, and I went.

I have taken the red crust.

The Aboyeur lifted my platter from among ours, four chairs from the Vanes, and carried it down the whole length of the Hall to the far end, where the red crust's platters stand. He called its name as it passed, the way he calls every loaf. It was not my name. The committee has entered me in its book as *Citizen Mie*. They told me it means the inside of the loaf, which is the same in every loaf. I bowed when they said it.

Then they gave me a knife from the dish pit, and before the rope pen I scraped the gold from my own cuffs. The leaf came away in flakes and fell into a dish held by a Plongeur, who did not look at me. Afterwards I ate my crust, standing. It tastes of nothing but bread.

Then I bowed to the Square.

You taught me that bow. You taught every child of the house in the long room, counting aloud. *Once to the Hearth. Lower to the gallery.* I gave the Square the first and not the second. Once, and not low. My knees remembered your counting, and I had to tell them to stop.

I have taken the red crust.
I have taken the far platter.
I have taken a name that is a crumb.

You will want to know why, and you will want it to be a better reason than it is.

I am afraid, Madame. I have read the gallery's seating plans. I know which names are near the top, and I know a ledger is read from the top. That is one reason. The other is that some evenings I believe them, and on those evenings the bread does taste like bread and nothing worse. I cannot tell you which of the two lifted my hand tonight. I have been trying since the bell.

Is a man who changes sides to live a convert, or only a guest?

Come down. The Gallery stands in the rope pen at every sitting and is counted. You can stand there, or you can stand with me. They are twenty steps apart, and only one of them is in the rain.

I kept back a little of what came off the cuffs. It is mine, and it is small. I tell you so that you will not hear it from anyone else.

A house can change its seat in an evening. It takes longer to change its knees.

Your son,
Aurèle ~~de Sérigny~~

## II. Citizen Mie to Madame his mother
*March 1793, the week before the spring ferry. Crumb seal.*

Madame,

Your steward writes that Madame does not receive the citizen. He wrote it in five words and signed it. I keep it in the book I keep the committee's minutes in.

I will write anyway. A citizen may write to anyone. It is in the new articles.

The committee did not take me for my conversion. I thought it would, and I was ready to be tested on it: the crust, the bow, the name. Nobody tested me. What they wanted was what I remembered.

Who sat where at the Feast of 'eighty-eight.
Which house had its loaves gilded double.
Whose window is whose.

They had the gallery's books by then, every seating plan back to 'eighty-seven, open on the rail. They could read the names. They could not read the rooms. A seating plan tells you who sat beside whom. It does not tell you who lowered his voice when the Chancellor came in, or which house fed birds from its upper window every morning, so that the birds knew it.

I told them that. I told them which window the birds come to at the house of Maillard. I told them the citizen formerly Maillard feeds them by hand every morning and gives them names. I did not know the names. They found those for themselves, written in his own hand. The committee lists everything it finds.

The Secretary took it all down. They say of her that she knelt in the dish pit, as if it were a rank, and I think in that room it is one. Every plate I ever ate from at a Gallery supper went down to her sink. I could not look at her while she wrote, so I looked at her pen. She wrote my words exactly. She did not make them larger.

What is a turncoat but a man who knows where the other coat was hung?

The committee thanked me. Then, because there is a form for thanking and they keep the forms, they thanked me a second time.

They did not take me for my heart. They took me for my memory, and it is the only part of me that was ever the house's.

Mie

## III. Citizen Mie to Madame his mother
*Friday, 29 March 1793, after the ferry. Crumb seal.*

Madame,

Your steward writes that Madame's platter is a fortnight behind. He did not write anything else. I think he meant me to understand that he was not allowed to.

On Lady Day the Gallery gave its ball in the Hall. Out of habit the house's printer sent me a card, addressed to a name the committee's book no longer carries. He is paid by the year. I did not go in. I stood with the Commons outside the south doors in the Square and heard the hautboys through them. I did not look for you. I tell you so, because you will want to know whether I looked, and the answer is that I kept my eyes on the man in front of me until the music stopped.

On Tuesday the Razor came off the Morning Ferry, in crates stencilled for the committee. Guild carpenters put it together beside the Hearth, and the consort played while they did it. No one had told the consort to stop.

On Thursday they drew the lot at the committee's supper.

You know the article. You have heard it read every Candlemas of your life. *The sentence is read twice, once by the High Chancellor, and once by a member of the Gallery chosen by lot, who shall be thanked.* The committee has retained it in every particular. The question was whether my name went into the bowl. They took it gravely, the way they take everything. They ruled that Citizen Mie is red crust at the platter and gold leaf in the bowl. They did not laugh. I did not laugh. The ruling was entered.

The bowl gave them my name.

This morning, at the dawn sitting, I read the sentence of the dowager of the house of Belcourt for the second time.

You remember her. She carried her linnet's cage to the gallery window every morning of her life, so that the bird could see the harbour. At my first ball, when I was twelve and had been counted through my bows a hundred times, she danced with me because nobody else would, and she told me I bowed like a man apologising.

I read it to her from the paper. I did not look up until the end.

She thanked me. As the article requires.

I read it in my own voice.
I read it in the house's voice.
I read it in the voice you taught to bow.

The citizen formerly Maillard did not come before us. His name was on the committee's list in the week before the ferry, and before the ferry it was off it. No one struck it in my sight. I did not ask. Not asking is the form.

The Secretary wears a comb now, of cod bone, the teeth not quite even. I do not know where it came from or what it means. I noticed it because she touched it twice while the lot was drawn, and I have learned this spring to notice what people touch.

A sentence read twice is not twice as true. It only has two men to thank.

Mie

## IV. Citizen Mie to Madame his mother
*Thursday, 2 May 1793, after the sixth bell, at the committee's table. Crumb seal.*

Madame,

They tried Évariste Vane this morning. I am writing to you from the gallery, where the supper is waiting.

I sat in a gilded chair. Red crust sits in them now. Mine was four from where the Vanes used to sit, because the ushers follow the stenographer's plan and the stenographer has not changed her hand.

The south doors opened at the fifth bell, and the Square sang. Évariste was brought up from the cold pantry and set on the stool. It is the same stool. He bowed to the Square, as the Order now says, and not low. You taught him too, Madame. He was in the long room with me, counting.

The President read the charge. Then she had the stenographer read Exhibit 1, which is the Book of the Morning Judgment for the eleventh of March, 1788, read in full and standing.

I was there in 1788. You were too. I had forgotten it, and this morning I learned that I had not.

The seating was read out: the house of Vane first, and then the rest by precedence. We were the rest. Then the boy, Toinot, of the shaping bench. A bite of a gilded heel, weighed. Eleven grains. The fingernail held out over the rail. Then what he said, which the ruling would not have as testimony and the stenographer wrote down anyway: a square of sailcloth, flakes of gold leaf scraped from our crusts in the dish pit, pressed flat to be counted, for his mother's tariff. *It came to very nearly the fine.* The cracks of a loaf coming out of the oven. Ice going on the mill pond.

Then the supper card, read as served. Quince, after the Fire.

I ate the quince, Madame. I remember now that it was good.

The whole of the reading, I had my hand on my coat, over the place where the twist of paper is.

Évariste said he sat at the supper and did not speak for the boy. He did not make that into a defence. Then he asked who would go down at dawn, if his hand were stopped, since no hand but a Vane's has fed her since the Bringing. Nobody answered. The Wardens turned out his coat on the trial table: a handkerchief, a pencil, a little tin with his daughter's leaven in it, dried grey. They gave him water. He took it in the hand that does not feed her. He keeps the other from bread.

Then the committee voted, and I counted with them.

Nineteen for the Razor. Four against.

I was one of the nineteen.

The four kept their hands up a long time, as if to make sure the stenographer had them. I kept mine up exactly long enough to be counted, and then I put it in my lap and covered it with the other.

Was I the boy's judge in 1788, because I ate?
Am I Évariste's in 1793, because I voted?
Which of the two meals was the sentence?

The President said the Mother has no other hand, and that he would feed her at the sixth bell, and after that the sentence. At the sixth bell the Wardens took him across the Hall to the sub-basement door. He went down the ninety-one steps. I could not see past the door, and I do not know the hour. The stenographer's form has a blank for it.

The broth has not come up. The supper waits on the Razor, and the Razor waits on the Mother. The red crust is on the table, warm, and I have eaten some. The committee does not rise for the bell.

I have now sat at both suppers. The quince is the same.

Mie

## V. Citizen Mie to the steward of the house of Sérigny
*Michaelmas, 1793. Folded once, no seal. A twist of paper inside.*

Steward,

I am writing to you because she returns nothing and receives nothing, and you have answered every letter with a line. It is Sunday. You will be winding the clocks, all of them, the way you have every Sunday since I was small enough to follow you from room to room. The committee keeps the bells now. I hope you wind them anyway.

Nothing has been sentenced since May. The Razor stands beside the Hearth under a cloth. This morning, as the article requires, the committee measured the nail at Michaelmas and entered it. I was there to sign as witness. I am still on the committee, and the committee has nothing left to judge, so I come to the sittings and sit. They keep me the way they keep the stenographer: because I know the form, and in case.

Your last line said that Madame's platter is three nights from the stamp. You did not need to write more. I know the article. *The Gallery's quarter has come.*

The paper inside holds what I kept back from my cuffs in February. I weighed it yesterday on the scales at the Counting-House, by myself, after the clerks had gone. It came to very nearly the fine.

I know what this is. In law it is a second theft, gold of a house that is no longer anyone's to keep. I heard a boy's gold leaf read into the record this spring, and I know what his cost him. I raised my hand for less than this.

I gave them the windows.
I gave them my voice for the lot.
I gave them my hand at the count.

But I kept this.

Do not tell her whose it is. Tell her it is the house's. It is the only part of me that still is.

I go through the Hall at dusk and see the far platters and my own among them, its crust baked red with the rest. Every morning I wait for someone to crack it before the Square, and every morning no one does. Nobody has cracked me. That is the sentence.

Tell her the crust is red. The crumb was always hers.

Mie
