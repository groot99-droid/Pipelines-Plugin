# Persons of Note

*Who to Ask in HollowMere. For the Use of Persons Arriving by the Daily Train.*
*Issued from the Post Office & Telegraph Room, HollowMere, Coldwater Territory. December 1893.*
*Copy handed across the sorting table, Mon. 11 Dec.*

> *Approved for issue. — Clk.*

---

Nobody comes to the end of the line without a reason. I do not ask yours. Your letters will tell me, or the lack of them will.

This is a list of who to ask. It is not a list of who to trust. The Company prints the one and not the other.

## Before You Ask Anyone

1. Ask by daylight. After Curfew, the answers come from people you did not ask.
2. Ask in pairs. The town counts alibis in twos, and so should you.
3. Pay in scrip. It was three to the dollar in October. It is five now. Do not ask what it will be.
4. Rent is collected at the Shift, by the Clerk, from everyone under a roof. A guest is under a roof.
5. Do not read the stone in the churchyard aloud. Agnes reads it to the Row each dawn. Let her.
6. Do not follow a light in the fen unless you know whose it is.
7. Don't miss the train.

> *Rule 5 is not Company policy. — Clk.*

---

## I. The Landmarks

**Mrs Grieves — the Drowned Lantern**

*Where to find her.* Behind the bar, from Station Rush until the shutters come across at Curfew. After the shutters, what burns in there is her business.

*What she asks.* That you pay your tab, and that you never try to close anyone else's.

*What she gives.* Whiskey off the train, four cases a week with one short-filled, which she notes and does not complain of. A lantern on loan if you need one, and she will not ask where you mean to carry it.

*What not to say.* Do not count the frames behind the bar out loud. There were six when I last stood there: drowned sailors, one frame empty, and one face nobody admits to hanging. Do not ask after Frank. Bar, her hired man, keeps the sawed-off under the counter. Do not ask him which side of the strike it is for. He has been asked, and he has answered both ways.

She keeps a line open for Arthur, who died of the cough last winter. His brother pays for a Rotgut on it every week. She has never struck the line through.

*Post.* None. She says anyone who wants her can walk in.

**Old Fennick — the light at the marsh edge**

*Where to find him.* At his light below the Fenwood, every dusk. On Saturdays he is at the Lantern, filling his can.

*What he asks.* Nothing. If you bring him oil he takes it and tells you its price, to show you he knows.

*What he gives.* He will tell you which light is his, which is worth more than it sounds. Stand at the end of the planks on a fog night and count the lights on the fen. One of them is steady. Ask him which.

*What not to say.* There is a lantern under the water by the old oak, and it burns. Do not ask him about it, and do not ask him who keeps it lit. Some say it is the lamp he kept when there was a lake, gone down with the limb that held it. Some say it is the sign of the old Rusty Lantern, carried into the mere by a barman who never came up. Fennick has heard both, and he says neither.

He was nineteen when he had a lake to keep. He is old now, and his light guides nobody anywhere in particular. He keeps it all the same. The Lamplighter lights the square and does not light his, and he would not let her.

*Post.* None that I have carried.

**Father Bray — St Brannock's (not to be found)**

*Where to find him.* You will not find him.

*What he asks.* —

*What he gives.* The bell. He had it off the Company for the chapel, and it hangs at St Brannock's still.

*What not to say.* Do not say he died, and do not say he fled. Either earns you a look across the bar. Both together earn you a drink, because the town says both, and says them louder after the second pour. The Gazette answers no enquiries regarding Father Bray, and neither do I.

The town remembers that he darned his gloves in red wool. It remembers this the way you remember a man's hands.

Letters still come for him from back east, in two different hands. I hold them. I do not send them back, because there is nowhere certain to send them.

For confession, go to the new Vicar, who came up on the train on the seventeenth of October with one trunk. He preaches at dusk. Everyone in HollowMere confesses to him sooner or later; he is patient, and it is a small town. Say what you came to say. When you leave, look at who is kneeling at the back, and do not let them see you look.

*Post.* See above.

**Doc Mallory — Mallory Apothecary**

*Where to find the Doc.* At the bench by day, and at the Bell whenever it rings. At other hours, upstairs under the green lamp in the loft, at times you should not be passing.

*What the Doc asks.* Your name, for the stock book. Every bottle on the shelf has a line in that book, and every line has a name and a date. Give a true name.

*What the Doc gives.* Salts and bandages. Notice of the fen-cough, posted on my board and in the Gazette and worded as the Company asks: *miasma*. And when it comes to it, the hour you died, which is the one thing in this town that breaks an alibi.

*What not to say.* There is a shelf marked NOT FOR SALE with one slot empty. Do not ask what stood in it, or when it went. Mallory knows both.

*Post.* Tonics for the morgue come up crated on the train. I sign for them, and Mallory signs again. Nobody has explained to me why it takes both of us.

**Tuck — the ferry post, marsh dock**

*Where to find him.* At the post where he ties the ferry, which has a tar line on it at the water's edge. Or out in the channels, when the fog allows.

*What he asks.* A fare. Scrip or a crust: I have seen him take both and could not tell you which he prefers.

*What he gives.* The crossing. And what he has seen, if you wait for it. It was Tuck who said the lantern in the Fenwood was lit again. Gulls follow his ferry out and do not follow it back.

*What not to say.* Do not ask him to pole toward the lights that are not steady. Do not ask anything of him after dusk.

He cannot read. When the Gazette comes up I read him the front of it on the platform, the stone's name first, because he asks for that first. He ties the ferry with a knot nobody in this town taught him, and he will not say who did.

*Post.* None.

**Tamsin Crumb — Crumb's Ovens**

*Where to find her.* At the ovens before anyone wakes, flour to the elbows. The wake table and its ten plates stand out front.

*What she asks.* The name of your dead, if you have any here, and the count of your mourners.

*What she gives.* Wake loaves, the island way. She came up with Vane from the island, before the Line. One loaf goes to each mourner and three to the dead, each with a crumb token sealed in wax. Keep the token. People here keep them.

*What not to say.* Do not ask what her bread is raised on. Do not ask about the fourth wake in October, the one she could not account for. And do not tip her in gold. Gold has been left in her shop before, and she did not care for it.

Her flour has come up short on the train since October. Mourners' loaves are halved so that the dead get theirs whole. A woman of Mill Row paid ahead for ten loaves of her own, and I carried the receipt. On the seventh the stone had her name. Whether she has needed the loaves is not mine to say.

*Post.* Flour, short.

## II. Those Who Keep Terms

**Harbourmaster Pell — the marsh dock**

*Where to find him.* At the dock, by the skiff, which lies pulled up with a stone in it.

*What he asks.* His terms, which he states the same way every time:

> "The skiff leaks. Bring me resin and a plank of timber and I will lend you a lantern."

*What he gives.* The lantern: lent, not given. He will want it back, and he will want it back lit.

*What not to say.* Do not call it a marsh dock to his face. HollowMere has no harbour. He keeps the title out of courtesy or out of habit, and you will keep it too. Do not ask him why a fen three hundred miles from any sea keeps a tide. He has a chart with the next low tide circled, and he does not explain it.

Nora has mended her nets on his boards since the twenty-ninth of November. She does not need you to say anything at all.

*Post.* Years back, a tide chart came up the Line addressed to *the Harbourmaster, HollowMere*. There was no such office. He signed for it anyway.

**Mother Moll — the charcoal hut, the Fenwood**

*Where to find her.* Past Gallows Wood, beyond where the planks give out, by the charcoal clamp that is always smoking low. The hanging oak is close by, and so is the den. Her door is shut. Knock once.

*What she asks.*

> "Four sprigs of herb, dear. The fog is in everyone's chest."

*What she gives.* A twist of paper for the chest. I have never heard of anyone who took one complaining of it.

*What not to say.* Do not stand in front of the den, and do not ask her about the oak. She keeps two bottles. Do not ask her for the other one.

*Post.* None. The Fenwood has no box.

Up the Line, they say, there are others who keep terms: a foreman on the BlackPine yard, a woman at a forge, someone at the kilns. Men come down saying so. I have had no letter from any of them, and I enter it as said.

## III. The Law

**The Constable — the Constable's Office & Morgue**

*Where to find him.* In the office. At first light, at the door of whoever is behind with the rent.

*What he asks.* That you keep the peace. He keeps the rest: the cuffs, the whistle and the key.

*What he gives.* Under Article IX he may deputise you, and he may undeputise you without cause.

*What not to say.* Do not declare Unrest. The word belongs to him or to the soapbox, and you are neither. Do not ask him who held the Detective's office before the man who holds it now.

The Watchman, who drags his left heel, used to report at this door. He was fined and stood down after Hattie. You may still hear a heel in the lanes after dark. I do not say whose.

*Post.* Wires from the Eastern Office, which come in at the key. The Clerk works the key. I carry the slips across the square, folded.

**The Detective — a desk at the Constable's**

This is an office, not a name. The man who holds it kept time at the railyard before he was deputised. He has a magnifier, a case file and a stamp.

*Where to find him.* Wherever something has been left lying.

*What he asks.* What you saw, and the hour you saw it. He will want to look at your watch.

*What he gives.* A stamp on your statement. Sometimes, NO CASE.

*What not to say.* Do not tell him the last one was careless. The Gamekeeper says there was a last one, who went into the Fenwood at dusk after a light, with a book like his under his arm, and did not come out on the side he went in. He has heard this. He has written it down himself.

*Post.* A file came addressed to that desk before the desk had a man at it. Where it went is not entered here.

> *Office established for the list. — Clk.*

**The Judge — the bench under the Bell**

*Where to find him.* He comes up on the train for a sitting and goes down again at dusk. Between sittings the bench stands in the square under a tarpaulin against the fog, with the gallows behind it and the rope taken in.

*What he asks.* Nothing of you, until you are standing in front of him.

*What he gives.* One of three verdicts, painted on a board for those who cannot read: Acquittal. A Heavy Fine. Death. Death comes at an hour he sets, never during the Shift. The Company does not lose the work.

*What not to say.* Say nothing at the bench before he records the only words he is permitted to record. After *The Town has spoken*, nothing you say will be entered.

He last sat on Tuesday the twenty-eighth of November. That evening they hanged a man who mended boots. I never carried a letter for him, and so I never had his name.

*Post.* None comes to him here. A carbon of every verdict goes down by the Dusk train to the Eastern Office.

## IV. The House and the Wood

**Widow Vane — the parlour**

*Where to find her.* In the parlour, by day. She does not stand when you come in. At night she is in the bedroom, with a candle. The town says she cannot sleep; that she sleeps with it lit, to see who comes; that she lights it to be seen from the lane by someone in particular. Pick one. She will not tell you which.

*What she asks.* Who sent you.

*What she gives.* Nothing. Lord Aldous Vane's will was read in that room, and it leaves everything to whoever remains when the last lantern goes out. Horace, Butler at the manor these twenty-two years, will come down and see you out.

*What not to say.* The portrait over the letter box hangs turned to the wall. Do not turn it unless it is your work. She will let you, and she will watch whether you do. Do not speak of the island. Lord Vane is three nights dead, and he has been three nights dead for longer than three nights.

*Post.* Crepe, four yards, paid in scrip, entered at the Company Store the morning the wire came. The wire came in at the key, which the Clerk works and I do not. I carried the slip across the square to her, folded. I did not read it. Nowhere else in this guide do I say that.

> *Read it. — Clk.*

**The Gamekeeper — Gallows Wood and Hale Barn**

*Where to find him.* At the near edge of the wood, where the planks start. Or in Hale Barn with the goat, oiling his fowling piece, under a wolf pelt nailed to the door. The nails are old. The pelt is not.

*What he asks.* That you keep to the planks.

*What he gives.* He knows where the den is. He gives you that, and only by telling you not to go there.

*What not to say.* Vane paid him to keep the wood. Vane is dead, and he keeps it anyway. Do not ask him why. Do not come up behind him. Do not come for him at all: if anyone comes for him he will not go alone, and he means to take the right one.

*Post.* His wages stopped with Vane. Nothing has come for him since, and he does not come to the window to ask.

## V. Those Who Come by Train

**The Pinkertons — the platform, when the town riots**

*Where to find them.* Off the Baron's car. Mr C. Thorne travels with an escort of nine and four dogs. They came up on the fifteenth of November, the day after the riot, and again on a Saturday after. They stood at the platform all day, and went down at dusk.

*What they ask.* Nothing of you. They ask the Constable.

*What they give.* A quiet square, and the dogs.

*What not to say.* Do not speak to the dogs. Do not be on the soapbox at the Bell when the car comes in.

*Post.* They carry their own, and so does he.

---

## VI. Held at the Window

Mail does not stop because a person has. These are held, and I enter them so you will not ask me for them.

- **Elsie**, 25 Nov. A seed list from back east, sent for every autumn. She never once planted from it. She liked to read the names.
- **Mabel**, 27 Nov. A card from a niece, with a pressed flower that has gone the colour of the card.
- **Cyril**, the fisher, 29 Nov. Twine, ordered on account. Nora has the account.
- **Hattie**, the washerwoman, 2 Dec. A letter in her own hand, sent back from back east: *not known at this address*. She had written the address from memory.
- **Vera**, of the mill, 9 Dec. A paper of fifty needles, paid ahead.
- **Ada Brook**, Mill Row. One letter, which I have put in neither pile.

---

## VII. Persons Not Entered

The Gazette prints no name it has not verified, and neither do I. What follows are not persons. They are things the town says and things left lying, and I enter them as the town leaves them.

They say someone walks after dark in a face that is not their own. When the town asks loudest who did it, count the voices.

They say there is always a second, who never holds the knife, only the alibi; and that if the first falls, the second picks it up. Nobody has proved it. Nobody has stopped saying it.

A bandana. Gold left where nobody pays in gold. A match struck at the mill. A voice at the soapbox after the Shift, which the Constable has not yet called Unrest. Someone at the back of St Brannock's every dusk, who kneels and does not sing.

There is a glove in the chapel rope. I do not know whose it is.

There is a stone with tomorrow's date on it. It shows a new name every morning, and nobody has seen a name being cut. Whether it kills or only knows, I cannot tell you. I can tell you that Agnes has not missed a morning.

And this. If a letter comes for you sealed in black wax, I did not open it. I could not have told you whose it was before it came. It comes in the bag like the rest, and it comes for whoever is here.

> *Nor could I. — Clk.*

---

The train comes in at Station Rush. The train goes down at dusk. The mail goes with it.

I keep a kettle for the purpose, at the Company's expense, charged to the mails. Write what you like. Write it as if I were reading.

The envelope in my rack at dawn is who you are.

Don't miss the train.
