# Pledged and Unredeemed

*Book of Pledges. Harold's step, HollowMere. Kept since before the drain.*
*Pages for 1893, written up for the paper under the Bell.*

---

Everything in this room came over my step with someone who meant to come back for it. I lend on the meaning to come back. The thing itself is only what I hold while I wait.

## Terms of the Step

1. I lend on what can be carried over the step. Nothing that breathes. Nothing that is bread.
2. Thirty days, and a ticket in two halves. You keep one half and I keep the other. Whoever brings your half is the owner, as far as the step knows.
3. I lend in scrip and I am paid back in scrip. It was three to the dollar in October and it is five now. What it does in between is the Company's business, and has been since the drain.
4. I do not ask why. I write down what I am told.
5. At thirty days the thing is mine. I have never sold one.

---

## The Paper under the Bell

The Baron fell on the platform on Thursday. On Saturday the sixteenth the Clerk nailed a paper under the Bell in his own hand, and stood beside it while the Row read it. I have never known him stand beside a paper before.

> *Under the Articles an account survives the man. Every article held against scrip upon the Grant is an account. All such articles held at Harold's step shall be cried at the Midday Shift on Saturday, 23 December, inside their days or out of them, and the sums set against the lines of those who pledged them. A list to be posted by the holder.*

Nowhere on it is the word *forfeit*. I looked for it. A Company paper that will not use the Company's word has been written by a man who has not yet got used to his hand.

I am the holder, so I post the list. I have done it my way, which the paper did not forbid. A pawn counter is where a town puts what it cannot trust itself to keep. If the Company means to sell those things back to the town, the town can at least know what it is buying, and from whom. So under every article I have written who brought it, why it came, and why it never went home.

I list only this year. The rest of the room is from before the drain: a lake man's ring, a christening cup, four watches that stopped when their owners did, an oar with a name burned into the blade. The Company has no paper that reaches back that far, and it cannot cry what it never wrote down.

---

## The Tickets

**No. 4061. Monday, 16 October.**
*Article.* One lantern, tin, the glass whole. The wick trimmed short and hardly burned.
*Name on the ticket.* Ivy Cotter, of the mill.
*Lent.* Four scrip and some coppers, which was what she was short.

She sat in the dark of an evening to save the wick, and the whole Row knew it. That afternoon she counted her fare out on my counter from a paper twist, coin by coin, the way she had saved it, and it came out four short. The lantern was the last thing she owned that she had not been sitting in the dark beside. I would have lent on the coat. She said she needed the coat for back east.

Two boxes went down to the platform that day: Bert Haddon and the girl Nell. The next morning, the crew say, Ivy Cotter stood on the platform in her grey coat with her fare in a paper. I did not see her go. Mrs Grieves has written *Gone east* against her, and I write what Mrs Grieves wrote, and say so.

*Why it has not come back.* Nobody who has gone east comes back for a lantern. It is the only lantern on my shelf that has never been lit. Since the wolves came down to the fence at the end of November, four people have asked me for it. They hunt the edge of the light, and in a winter like this a lantern is worth more than anyone ever lent on one.

*For Saturday.* Cry it.

---

**No. 4068. Tuesday, 24 October.**
*Article.* A knife. I have written *knife, tin*, because it is tin, painted to look like steel, with the paint gone off the edge.
*Name on the ticket.* Smith.
*Lent.* One penny.

A stranger off a train, in a coat cut for a bigger town than this. They wanted the ticket to say *knife*. I wrote *knife, tin*. The stranger read it upside down, laughed, and said that would do: a paper was a paper. The penny did not interest them. What they wanted was a paper with a knife on it and a name, in a hand that was not their own, in a book the town could be shown.

*Why it has not come back.* The stranger does not want the knife. They want it found. Every dawn they pass my step going up to read the stone, and every morning on the way down they stop and ask whether anyone has asked about the knife. Nobody has. I say so, and the stranger looks sorry for me.

*For Saturday.* Cry it. On the list, under it: *It is not a knife. Whoever buys it, be told.*

---

**No. 4070. Friday, 27 October.**
*Article.* One key, brass, a study key. Worn bright at the bow where a thumb has counted it.
*Name on the ticket.* Horace, Butler to the house of Vane.
*Lent.* One coin, which he did not ask for.

He came in his mourning band and stood, because he would not sit in a shop in front of its keeper. He laid the key on the counter teeth up, so that I could see the bite, as if I could know a study key by its teeth. I could not. He could.

He asked to borrow nothing. I made him take a coin. A pledge with no sum on it is a gift, and I do not take gifts from the manor.

"The ring is no longer my count," he said. "Your book is."

He said nothing more, and I did not ask. The town says there is a key in the barn and a key under the chapel, and stops counting at two. I make it three. I do not say so on the square.

*Why it has not come back.* He passes my step every day. He writes his round in the day-book before he walks it, and has done for twenty-two years. He has never once written this step into it.

*For Saturday.* Cry it. I went up to the Bell and told the Clerk that a key to a dead man's study is not an account. He wrote down that I had said so. Then he entered the key on his own copy of the list, in his own hand, with the rest.

---

**No. 4077. Wednesday, 8 November.**
*Article.* One pocket watch, silver, a good movement. Too fine for anyone on the Grant to have bought here.
*Name on the ticket.* Nobody. I asked twice. "Nobody. Write it." I wrote it.
*Lent.* Full value, without haggling.

The voice knew a pawn counter. It had stood at one before, or at many. It set the watch down face up, with its hand flat beside it, the way you do when you want the broker to see you will not snatch it back. Nobody does not haggle. I should have known by that.

It looked longer at the shelf behind me than at the money: the brooches, the christening cup, the lake man's ring. "Somebody's grandmother's, all that," it said. "Every bit."

On Saturday the sixteenth, the Vicar saw the watch in my window. He came in, asked the price, opened the back with his thumbnail the way a man opens his own, and shut it again. I offered it to him for nothing, and my terms be hanged. He would not have it.

"Let whoever pledged it come back for it," he said. "I should like to see who."

So I lent him another, off the old shelf: a lake man's watch from before the drain that nobody had come for in thirty years. No ticket. I am told he set it by the clock in the Telegraph Room on Sunday afternoon. That evening he went up the slope.

*Why it has not come back.* The pledger and the owner were each waiting for the other. Neither is coming now, or both are, and I cannot say which. The lake man's watch has not come back either. Whoever has been preaching in those robes since Sunday has not brought it.

*For Saturday.* Not on the list. It was never a pledge, only a theft with a ticket on it. The Articles have a word for a man who lends against a thing like that, and I have entered that word against myself on the back board of this book. I wind the watch on Sundays.

---

**No. 4079. Friday, 10 November.**
*Article.* One notebook, Company paper, written to the back board. A strap.
*Name on the ticket.* Detective. He gave his office and not his name.
*Lent.* The price of a can of oil, and a wick.

He was the one before the one there is now. He kept the book shut while he stood at my counter, with his thumb inside it to hold a page. He said he would fill another by the far side and come for this one on Monday.

"I keep a copy of everything," he said. "This is the copy."

*Why it has not come back.* He went into the Fenwood at dusk after a light, with the next book under his arm, and did not come out the side he went in. That is the Gamekeeper's account, and the Gamekeeper does not say things. Lights hang low over the fen that are not lanterns. Old Fennick's is steady and the others are not. I do not know which one he followed. I have never read his book. The pages are dry. The Fenwood is not.

*For Saturday.* Cry it.

---

**No. 4090. Monday, 27 November. Station Rush.**
*Article.* One key, iron, plain, longer than a house key, no tag. Out of the sweepings.
*Name on the ticket.* Flo.
*Lent.* One penny.

A hand came under the shutter before I had it up: a child's, with chalk in the creases of the knuckles and a broom callus at the root of the thumb. I knew the hand before the voice. Flo sweeps the floor at the Lantern for a penny a night.

"Under the crew bench," she said. "Penny?"

I said it was somebody's. She said everything is somebody's. I gave her the penny, tore the ticket, and put her half into the hand, and the hand went.

While I was writing it up, the Undertaker's boy ran past my step for the cart. They had found Mabel in the lane behind the Lantern.

The town says a key like that opens any door, and tells on you afterwards. Where it lay, it left a black mark on my counter, and another on my thumb. I have scrubbed both. Neither will come off.

*Why it has not come back.* The Undertaker went round the Row in November looking for the children and could not find five. Flo was one of them. Her broom stands propped by the Lantern door every morning, and nobody sees the hand that props it. I saw it. I may be the last one who did.

*For Saturday.* Struck. I struck it late on Friday night and put the key in my own drawer. A child's penny is not an account.

---

**No. 4091. Wednesday, 29 November.**
*Article.* One mourning brooch, jet, with a plait of grey-brown hair under the glass. Her grandmother's. The hair was her grandfather's.
*Name on the ticket.* Gert, Pump Row, fourth door.
*Lent.* Six scrip, which is less than the pine.

On Tuesday the Tribunal hanged a man who mended boots. The Company billed the gallows pine to Gert's account, and at Midday it entered her Indebted. She came to me next morning with her boy of eight behind her, carrying the empty basket so the Row would think she was going for washing. She scrubs her step every morning, though the mud is back by noon. She asked whether it would keep. I said everything keeps here.

On the night of Wednesday the thirteenth it went off the shelf, and my half of its ticket went with it. Pell's chart had low water circled that night. Nothing else was touched, not even the lake man's ring beside it. One soul had stood at my counter in November and looked a long while at that shelf. I need not write more than that, and I will not.

*Why it has not come back.* It was not there to come back. Gert comes on Saturdays and stands at my window looking at the place it stood. The Saturday after, I paid her its value out of my own drawer, and her half of the ticket came back over the counter. It is the only ticket I have ever redeemed from my side.

*For Saturday.* Gone. The Clerk has entered the loss against Gert's line anyway. I have paid that too.

---

**No. 4096. Saturday, 2 December.**
*Article.* One paper, folded three times and sealed, with a name written on its face. It weighs nothing.
*Name on the ticket.* The name on the paper. I will not copy it here.
*Lent.* One penny.

It came at a quarter past five, before the lamps. The pledger asked me to write the hour. I write the hour on every ticket. This time I was also asked to say it aloud, so that we both heard it. A quarter past five. A plain coat. Clean hands, that had never pawned anything in their life: they did not know where to put themselves on my counter.

Hattie was dead by the next light. On Sunday morning the Detective, the one there is now, came to my step and asked to see Saturday's page. I showed it to him. He put his finger on the hour and the name and copied both into his own book, and thanked me. Then the Tribunal sat, and the Watchman was fined, and his lantern was taken from him on the table.

The paper was nothing. My book was the alibi.

I have never broken the seal, and I never will.

*Why it has not come back.* Once the hour was in my book, the paper had done its work.

*For Saturday.* Cry it, sealed.

---

**No. 4097. Monday, 4 December.**
*Article.* Chalk, in a twist of paper: three sticks worn to stubs.
*Name on the ticket.* Wenna.
*Lent.* One penny.

She came on the first train to meet a man who had written that he would meet it. I knew him. Jory Kitto, a tinner. He drowned at the last plug, and he lies in the first plot behind St Brannock's. She never went back east.

She told me that every night, in her ring, he asks whether the train has come. She tells him it has, and he asks again. She wanted one night without the ring, to learn whether he would ask anyway, and she asked me to keep the chalk out of her reach. Chalk is worth nothing. I lent on the reach.

At Dusk she came back to my step, though not for the chalk.

"He asked anyway," she said. Then she went down to the platform to watch the train go out.

*Why it has not come back.* She does not need chalk to hear him now.

*For Saturday.* Cry it.

---

**No. 4098. Tuesday, 5 December.**
*Article.* Irons, one pair, Office issue, with the key on a string through the chain.
*Name on the ticket.* The Watch.
*Lent.* A quarter of the fine.

I heard his heel on my step before the knock: one short scrape, then nothing. A Heavy Fine of three months' scrip, the lantern taken, stood down. The irons are not his. They are the Office's, and the Constable has not asked after them.

"I've stood by enough doors," he said. "Let them stand by yours."

A crossbow hung on his back that he had not carried on Saturday. I did not ask whose. The square says it was the Ranger's, and that she gave it to him on Monday. He does not say.

*Why it has not come back.* No wage, no lantern. He still walks the lanes after dark with nothing in his hands. At night I hear the heel go past.

*For Saturday.* Cry it.

---

**No. 4099. Friday, 15 December.**
*Article.* One crossbow, string slack. On the stock, a loop for a single bolt to ride apart in. The loop is empty, with a knot of white thread still in it.
*Name on the ticket.* The Watch.
*Lent.* The rest of the fine.

The same man, the same heel, ten days on. I asked about the loop, which breaks my fourth term, and he answered, which surprised me.

"Wednesday. At the pump, before light. It had Agnes by the arm."

That was all he said. The square told me the rest. The wolf had come up out of the dark to the pump. The bolt went through it behind the jaw. Agnes's forearm was opened in four places, two above and two below, deep enough to see what holds a hand on. The Gamekeeper hung the wolf from the gallows beam by its hind legs. It stayed there two days, its lips drawn back off its teeth and the fog beading on its fur, and the crows had the eyes by noon. Agnes lives. She has missed one morning at the stone, that one.

The Baron fell on Thursday. On Friday his escort stood on the square in their long coats, looking at anyone with anything slung on their back.

"A bow with nothing in it," he said, "is a thing a man gets looked at for."

*Why it has not come back.* He has taken his one shot. There is nothing left to redeem it with, and nothing left to redeem it for.

*For Saturday.* Cry it.

---

**No. 4100. Monday, 18 December.**
*Article.* One fowling piece, single barrel, the stock dark with years of oil.
*Name on the ticket.* Vane's man.
*Lent.* A can of oil.

On Sunday morning the stone said THE VICAR. On Monday evening, they say, the Vicar preached. The Gamekeeper did not go up to hear him. He came to me in the morning with the gun across his arms, the way a man carries a child he does not know how to hold.

"He told me to be sure," he said. "I'm not. And whoever's up there preaching hasn't told me anything."

I told him it would be cried on Saturday, by the paper under the Bell.

"Then let the Company hold it."

*Why it has not come back.* If they come for him now, he will go alone. That is better than taking the wrong face with him. Its one shot is still in it.

*For Saturday.* Cry it.

---

## Held, not pledged

**No number. Friday, 8 December.**
*Article.* One bottle, brown glass, corked.
*Name.* Mother Moll.
*Lent.* Nothing.

She brought me her second bottle, the one she keeps on the far shelf so that a hand reaching in the dark cannot take it by mistake for the first. She asked me to hold it.

I told her my first term: nothing that breathes. "This is the other way round, Moll."

"So it is, dear," she said, and set it on my counter anyway.

I would not put a sum on it, so there is no ticket. While she watched I sealed the cork in wax and put the bottle in a biscuit tin on the top shelf, where my own hand would have to fetch a chair to reach it. I asked her when she would come for it.

"Ask me after."

*Why it has not come back.* She is not yet certain. Every morning I look at the wax, and every morning it is whole.

*For Saturday.* Not on the list. The Company cannot cry what was never pledged.

---

## Saturday, 23 December. The Midday Shift.

Last night the hoods were out after Curfew, flour sacks with holes cut in them, with torches. Nobody came to the Bell in threes this morning. They came singly and stood apart. The escort stood along the bank front in their long coats.

The Clerk ran his finger down my list and said it balanced.

**Ivy's lantern** went first. Nora bought it at three times what was lent. She said it was for the dock at dusk, so that Cyril could see where she was. I wrote down what she paid.

**The tin knife** had no bid. The stranger stood at the back of the square and watched nobody bid. It came back to my shelf, and on the way past the stranger looked sorry for me again.

**The study key** went to the only bid, at the sum lent and the interest to the penny. The bid was the Clerk's. He put the key in his waistcoat pocket, and it clicked against another key in there on a brass tag. While the money was being counted, Horace came up the square with his day-book open at a page already written: *Key, bought back.* He stood a long time looking at his own line. Then he closed the book and walked his round anyway.

**The notebook** went to the Detective at the sum lent. He did not take it home. He read it where he stood, on my step, turning to the last written page. Then he asked me the hour. I opened Nobody's watch, the Vicar's, and told him. He wrote the hour down. He did not tell me what was on the page.

**The irons** went to the Constable at the sum lent, for the Office. He made the Clerk write that the Office had paid for its own irons.

**The crossbow** went to one of the long coats. "For the wolves," he said, and he did not mean the wolves.

**The sealed paper** had no bid for a while. Then a penny came up through the crowd from hand to hand, and the paper went back down the same hands. I did not see which hand it stopped in.

**The chalk** had no bid.

**The fowling piece** had no bid. Everyone on the square knew whose it was, and the Gamekeeper stood at the edge with his hands empty. I have entered: *Not sold. Nobody would.*

**The brooch:** *Gone.* Gert was there. She looked at the place on the list where it was written.

---

## Dusk, the same day

The shutter was half down. A hand came under it, and I did not look past the hand. My fourth term is that I do not ask.

It held Flo's half of the ticket, folded small, the way a child folds a thing to keep it in her fist. The paper had gone soft, with a crease of chalk dust along the fold. Beside it lay one penny.

"Redeeming," said the plain coat.

I took my half out of the drawer and laid the two halves together. The tear matched.

My second term says that whoever brings your half is the owner, as far as the step knows. I have kept that term since before the drain. I kept it tonight. I put the key in the hand. The hand took it without hurry, and the shutter came down.

I wrote it: **No. 4090. Redeemed.** It is the only redeemed thing in a book of unredeemed ones.

Then I asked the shutter where she was. I asked it once, and I asked it too late.

The black is still on my thumb. The town says a key like that tells on you afterwards. It has told on me.

---

## The last page

**Pledged:** twelve.
**Held:** one bottle, the wax whole.
**Sold:** six. The lantern, the study key, the notebook, the irons, the crossbow, the paper.
**Not sold:** three. The knife, the chalk, the fowling piece.
**Gone:** one brooch.
**Kept off the list:** one watch. And one lake man's watch, lent out without a ticket, not back.
**Redeemed:** one.

Agnes came down the Row at first light with her arm bound up in a sling, and called the stone's name to every door before anyone asked. I heard it from behind my shutter. I have not written it here.

The half-ticket in your pocket at dawn is who you are.

*Harold. His step.*
