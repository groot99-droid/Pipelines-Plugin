# The Confession Book of St Brannock's

*St Brannock's, HollowMere, Coldwater Territory. Confessions. Kept by the incumbent, from 17 October 1893.*

---

## The Rule of This Book

I came up the Line on the Tuesday train with a trunk, a candle and a watch. The man who had the cure before me is gone. Enquiries regarding my predecessor: none answered here either.

They told me at Thorne Junction that everyone in HollowMere confesses to the priest sooner or later. It was said as a warning. I have taken it as a rule.

This is the rule. Every soul who comes to me gets a page. The pages are numbered in the order the souls came, not in the order of their worth. Each night I will spend on one soul and learn where it stands. Then I will choose how much to say. Most nights the answer will be nothing. Some nights it must not be.

I keep three words for where a soul stands, and no more. A fourth would be a judgment, and judgment is not mine.

*With the parish.*

*With no one.*

*With the other.*

I will not write what the third means. Anyone who reads this far knows it. Anyone who does not know it should close the book.

Every page has the same form, so that I cannot flatter one soul with more ink than another: the look, the name as given, what they carry, what they want in their own words, what they confessed, where they stand, what I said, and whether they took the Seal.

A Parish Seal is a square of red wax on a card, pressed with the chapel's brass. It costs nothing. It is the only thing in this town that costs nothing, and I am told that is why nobody trusts it.

I keep a candle. I keep a watch. I keep the seal.

## No. 1

**Tuesday, 17 October 1893. At the pump. Not in the box.**

**The look.** A girl of about nine, in an apron cut for a bigger girl. She has never come to the box. I write her first because she was the first soul in HollowMere to tell me anything.

**Name, as given.** Not asked. Not given.

**Carries.** A slingshot through the apron string. Chalk on three fingers. In the apron pocket, a candle-end, she said. She kept one hand on that pocket the whole time we spoke, the way a child keeps a hand on a thing that is not a candle-end.

**Wants.** I asked her what she wanted to be when she was grown. "Not seen," she said. Then, after a while: "I want to see who."

**Confessed.** Nothing. That is the first thing this book holds: the first soul in HollowMere confessed nothing. I asked her where Father Bray had gone. "Father Bray went back east on the train," she said. Her mother, at their door, nodded. The woman sweeping the tavern step stopped sweeping, and nodded. I counted the nods: three. I knew it for a lie by the nodding, and I let it stand. A child should not be the one to tell a priest the truth about his church.

She counted something while I stood there. Her lips moved. I do not know what she was counting.

**Where this soul stands.** I spent my first night in HollowMere on a child. I am ashamed of it, and I write it down so that I cannot forget it. *With the parish.*

**Said.** Nothing. Not to her mother. Not to the woman with the broom.

**Seal.** Not offered. She would not have taken it.

## No. 2

**Wednesday, 18 October 1893. The Drowned Lantern, the end of the bar.**

**The look.** A platform hand, still in his crew coat at Saloon.

**Name, as given.** Len. "Just Len. Of the crew."

**Carries.** A lantern he does not need indoors and will not put down. A pocket watch that runs four minutes slow; he knows it does, and keeps it so, "so I'm never late for the train by mine." Chalk, for the platform tally. A coil of rope on one shoulder.

**Wants.** "Do my share with a man beside me. Vote on what I saw. Get the train."

**Confessed.** It is not a sin, he said. It is a sum. Dora Finch reads the tab upside down across the bar, through spectacles she mended with thread. Tonight she found a quarter too much on his line and made Mrs Grieves strike it. He owes less because of her. He was glad of the quarter first, and glad of her second, an hour later. He wanted that hour back.

I bought the Liquid Courage he could not run to. He held it a long while. He did not drink it in front of me.

**Where this soul stands.** *With the parish.*

**Said.** Nothing. I bought the drink. Mrs Grieves wrote it on my line, and wrote *Paid*.

**Seal.** Taken. He put it in the watch case, behind the glass, where he will see it every time he is four minutes late.

## No. 3

**Thursday, 19 October 1893. The bench at Mallory Apothecary.**

**The look.** Shirt-sleeves, and an apron with the strings tied twice. The shelf above the bench says NOT FOR SALE, and one slot in it stands empty.

**Name, as given.** Mallory. "Doc, if you're sick. Mallory, if you're not."

**Carries.** A medkit worn white at the corners. Bandage, which the Doc rolled on the bench all the while we talked, with hands that did not need the eyes. Salts in a stoppered bottle. I was given them once, under my nose, when I stood up too fast.

**Wants.** "Keep them alive. Read the ones I can't."

**Confessed.** The cough is in the Rows. The Company asks that it be called *miasma*. The Doc wrote on the notice that it is not catching. It is catching. The Doc has watched it go down Pump Row door by door, like a man collecting rent. It was written as the Company wanted it written because otherwise the notice would not go up, and a notice that says *shut your windows* is better than no notice. That is the sin.

The other thing, the Doc said, is not a sin but the trade. The Doc reads the dead and can tell you *when*. *When* is what breaks an alibi. *When* is the one honest thing the Doc owns.

I asked about the gravestone with tomorrow's date on it.

"It is never wrong before I am," the Doc said, and went on rolling.

**Where this soul stands.** *With the parish.*

**Said.** Nothing. The Doc did not want absolution. The Doc wanted a witness, and I was one.

**Seal.** Declined. "I'll take one when you can tell me what's in it."

## No. 4

**Saturday, 21 October 1893. In the box.**

**The look.** Flour to the elbows. It came through the grille before she did: the smell of a warm room on a cold day.

**Name, as given.** Tamsin Crumb, of Crumb's Ovens.

**Carries.** A lantern, banked. A watch on a cord at her neck, for the proving. Chalk for the slates. A length of rope for dragging the sacks, though today there was only one sack to drag.

**Wants.** "My work done in company. My vote on what I saw. Bread on every plate."

**Confessed.** Three wakes in one day: Wilf, Dora Finch, Percy. The custom she carried here is one loaf for each mourner and three for the dead, with a crumb sealed in wax at every heart, and she has never broken it. Today she broke it. She is down to one sack and the sweepings. She halved the mourners' loaves so that the dead would have their three. The living can be hungry and forgive it, she said, and the dead cannot. Then she said that is not doctrine but arithmetic.

There is more. She has been asked for a fourth wake, and she cannot account for it.

And every night she feeds the mother dough she came here with, whether there is flour for the town or not. She asked me whether that was a sin, with children hungry in the Rows. I did not answer quickly enough.

**Where this soul stands.** *With the parish.*

**Said.** That the halving was no sin. That is more than a priest should rule on bread. I ruled it anyway.

**Seal.** Taken. It went into her apron pocket with the flour.

## No. 5

**Monday, 23 October 1893. In the box.**

**The look.** A doctor's black bag, set on the shelf by the grille with the clasp undone. A coat cut for a bigger town than this one. A stranger off a train, but not off mine.

**Name, as given.** Three names. "Smith," first. "Brown," when I asked again at the end. At the door, laughing: "Anyone you like."

**Carries.** The bag was left open so that I would see into it. A knife made of tin, painted to look like steel, with the paint coming off the edge. A rag, stiff and dark. I have sat with enough dying to know the smell of blood, and this was not it. It smelled of beet, and of vinegar.

**Wants.** He used a doctor's words, borrowed, and wore them badly: "To keep them alive." Then the voice slipped the way a coat slips off one shoulder. "I want to be chosen."

**Confessed.** The three of Saturday: Wilf, Dora Finch, Percy. He said all three were by his hand. He gave me the hours, and every hour he gave was wrong. Doc Mallory had told me the hours at the wakes. His were wrong in the direction a man would choose if he wanted to be believed by people who had not asked. He said he would say it all again at the bell, if anyone would listen. Then he asked me whether the gallows here faces the pines or the square.

**Where this soul stands.** *With no one.*

**Said.** On the Tuesday I told the Constable that this soul did not do what it says. That breaks no seal: I told him what was not confessed, not what was. The Constable wrote it down.

**Seal.** Asked for, and refused. It is the first I have refused. He wanted it to show at the Tribunal.

> *In the margin, pencil, a small hand:* He wrote it down. And then?

## No. 6

**Thursday, 26 October 1893. The hall of Vane Manor.**

**The look.** Black, pressed, a mourning band. He stood the whole time, because he would not sit in his master's house in front of a guest. Behind him the clock stood at 11:47.

**Name, as given.** Horace. Butler to the house of Vane. Twenty-two years.

**Carries.** The master keyring, chained to his waistcoat. A candle, which he lit so that I could see to write, though it was afternoon. The hall is dark at any hour.

**Wants.** "To hold the doors, sir. And to say who went where."

**Confessed.** He writes the round before he walks it. He has done so for twenty-two years: first the line, then the walking that makes it true. He leaves the household day-book open on the hall table so that the ink can dry while he walks.

He showed me the ring. It holds three keys to the study. The town counts two, one in the barn and one under the chapel, and stops counting there. He has three. He does not know where the third came from. It was on the ring one morning. He has not written it in the book, and it is the only line he has ever left out. He said it the way another man would confess a woman.

Widow Vane is upstairs. Her portrait faces the wall. I asked him why. He said that was the house's business and not his.

**Where this soul stands.** *With the parish.*

**Said.** Nothing.

**Seal.** Declined, kindly. "The house has its own, sir."

## No. 7

**Saturday, 28 October 1893. The Constable's corner, under a lantern, after Curfew.**

**The look.** A lantern on two legs, in a long coat. He drags his left heel. I heard him before I saw him. That, he says, is the point of a watchman, and the trouble with one.

**Name, as given.** He gave his office and not his name. "The Watch."

**Carries.** A lantern. A pocket watch, which he opened and shut while we spoke, as though it might have something to add.

**Wants.** "One door a night. If a blade comes for that one, it fails."

**Confessed.** On the night of the twentieth he chose a door and stood by it until the Station Rush. Nobody came to it. In the morning there were three dead, behind three other doors.

He chooses his door by who looked frightened at Saloon. He has stopped trusting faces. A frightened face is cheap, he said, and anyone can buy one.

He asked me which door I would choose. He asked it as though I could tell him which door God would choose, and as though he would forgive God the answer.

**Where this soul stands.** *With the parish.*

**Said.** I would not name a door, and I called that the seal. It was not the seal. I had no door to give him. It was cowardice dressed as the seal. I write it here so that the book knows, even if he does not.

**Seal.** Taken. He hooked it through the bail of his lantern, where the light comes through the wax.

---

## No. 8

**Tuesday, 31 October 1893. In the box.**

**The look.** A tinner's coat, with wet stone in the wool. Hobnails on the boards; I counted his steps in and out by them. Through the grille I saw a jaw and a collar turned up, and nothing above them. The light was behind him. He had arranged that, or the light had. I write *he* because the coat did.

**Name, as given.** He wrote it on the card I keep on the shelf, in a careful hand: ~~KITTO, J.~~ I struck it afterwards myself. Jory Kitto is the first plot behind this chapel. A tinner, drowned at the last plug. I have stood at his stone.

**Carries.** A key on a string at his wrist, too fine for any tinner's door. I heard it tick against the grille. A rag, folded small, in his fist. About the cuffs, the smell of well water, which is not the same smell as rain. Once, as he settled, I heard a bucket being set down outside the box. He had brought a bucket to confession. His other hand I did not see.

*Added, the same hand, 12 Nov:* Hobnails.

**Wants.** In a tinner's words, which were not his own: "Work in pairs. Mark what I see. Vote on it." He said them the way a man reads a sign aloud.

**Confessed.** Nothing. He asked me who had done it: the three of the twenty-first. He asked three times, each time louder, until the boards answered. Nobody in this town has asked me that so loudly. I have come to think that the asking was the confession, and that he knew I would think so, and that he did not mind.

**Where this soul stands.** I spent the night on him. I wrote it here.

*[The lower third of the leaf is cut away. The edge is clean and straight; no scissor cut it.]*

> *In the margin, pencil, a small hand:* Not by me.

## No. 9

**Thursday, 2 November 1893. Hale Barn.**

**The look.** Oilskin, and a hat whose brim has gone soft. He sat on a stool with the goat watching us both. A wolf pelt is nailed to the outside of the door.

**Name, as given.** "Vane's man." He said it had been name enough for years and would do for one more conversation.

**Carries.** A fowling piece across his knees, which he oiled the whole time: rag down the barrel, rag back. A lantern hung from the beam.

**Wants.** "If they come for me, I'll not go alone. I'll take the one who came."

**Confessed.** Vane paid him to keep the wood. Vane is dead. Dead men pay nothing, and he keeps the wood anyway, because he does not know what else he is for. He knows the den. He goes past it at dusk and counts.

That is not his sin. His sin, he said, is that he has one shot left in him, at the end, and he is afraid of where it will go. In the dark, by a lantern, at a face. He is afraid it will be the wrong face, and that he will not be there afterwards to be sorry.

**Where this soul stands.** *With the parish.*

**Said.** "Be sure." Nothing else. He nodded as if I had told him something he had paid for.

**Seal.** Declined. "The wood's not Christian, Vicar. I'd be lying to it."

---

## No. 10

**Saturday, 4 November 1893. The steps of St Brannock's, at dusk.**

**The look.** A woman with a ladder on her shoulder and a lantern at her hip. She came to the steps because the box is off her round and the steps are on it.

**Name, as given.** She gave it, and I have not written it. She asked me not to. On the Company's books she is *Lamplighter*, paid by the lantern, and she said that is the name the town can have.

**Carries.** A lantern. A watch, for the hour at each post. Chalk, for the tally on each post. A rope, for the ladder.

**Wants.** "Light enough to keep them off the square. Somebody to work beside. A vote on what I saw."

**Confessed.** It is Saturday. Old Fennick fills his oil on Saturdays, so she was late, so she was quick. She lights the platform first, then three lanterns on the square, then the post at the Gallows Wood edge, then the manor gatepost. The fourth lantern on the square, over the chalk and the well, she leaves for last. She said she leaves it last because of what it shows. She would not say what it shows.

She does not light the dark places. That is the contract. But on her way back she stands at the mouth of the gallery for the length of a breath, because Jory Kitto paid for his own oil, and someone should.

I asked her about the lantern out in the Fenwood fog, the one that is still glowing.

"I don't go that far," she said.

**Where this soul stands.** *With the parish.*

**Said.** Nothing.

**Seal.** Taken. She asked whether it would burn. I said it would melt. She said that would do.

---

## No. 11

**Tuesday, 7 November 1893. In the box.**

**The look.** None: a dark coat in a dark box. The voice knew the box. It had been in one before, or in many.

**Name, as given.** "Nobody."

**Carries.** Picks on a ring; I heard them, a small sound like a purse of nails. A sack, empty, folded flat. Something wrapped in paper that smelled of saltpetre and match-heads.

**Wants.** "Three things that were somebody's grandmother's. And the sea door, at low water."

**Confessed.** Nothing contrite. "Nobody's hero, Vicar. Write that down." So I have.

This soul has been in HollowMere longer than I have, and longer than the talk at the pump that says the town is still waiting for one. It knows the tide in the mere better than a fisher, and said so. It said the cave goes further than the Company's maps. It laughed once, close to the grille.

**Where this soul stands.** *With no one.*

**Said.** Nothing.

**Carries,** *added the next morning, in another ink:* and, after, my watch.

**Seal.** Not given. Taken off the shelf on the way out, and the card with it.

> *In the margin, pencil, a small hand:* 5.50, low water.

## No. 12

**Thursday, 9 November 1893. The charcoal hut, at the Fenwood edge.**

**The look.** A shawl over a shawl. The clamp was smoking outside, and the smell was in everything. She called me dear before I had my hat off.

**Name, as given.** Mother Moll. "Moll will do. Mother, if you're poorly."

**Carries.** Two bottles, kept on two different shelves either side of the door, so that a hand reaching in the dark cannot pick up the wrong one by its shape. A candle in a jar.

**Wants.** "One saved and one ended, dear. And right both times."

**Confessed.** She has poured neither. She has stood at three doors with the first bottle in her apron, waiting for the knife, and the knife went to a fourth. The second she has not taken off its shelf. She is waiting to be certain, and certainty has not come up the path.

"Four sprigs of herb," she said, "and the fog is in everyone's chest. I can't sprig it out of them."

Mothers come to her door with their children, and she looks at them and thinks, *which of you*. She said the thinking is a sin, even if she never pours.

**Where this soul stands.** *With the parish.*

**Said.** I asked her whom the second bottle is for. "Ask me after," she said.

**Seal.** Taken. She set it on the shelf beside the first bottle.

## No. 13

**Saturday, 11 November 1893. On the bank of the mere channel.**

**The look.** Oilskins, and a net across his knees. His wife Nora sat beside him, mending the other end. The boats were drawn up, because the fog keeps the boats in.

**Name, as given.** Cyril, of the channel.

**Carries.** A lantern on the bank. A watch kept by the tide, not by the train. Chalk on the bow-board, for the catch. Net-rope.

**Wants.** "Mend beside a neighbour. Mark what I see. Vote on it." Nora said, "Beside his wife," and he said that was what he meant.

**Confessed.** Before he began he whistled two notes into the fog, so that anyone out on the channel would know where the bank was.

Then he told me that his people were one of the eleven fisher families. A priest stood at a door and swore them the lake's water before God. The water went into the ground in a night, and by morning the lake was a mere. He does not hold the church to the promise its last man made. He holds it to the next one. That is why he will not come to the box: in case I make one.

He fishes a tide that should not be there. It comes up the channels twice a day, where there is no sea. He takes what it brings and does not ask.

**Where this soul stands.** *With the parish.*

**Said.** That I would make no promises on God's behalf. It is the only true thing I said that week.

**Seal.** Declined. Nora took one, for the house.

---

## No. 14

**Sunday, 12 November 1893. The mouth of the Tin Gallery.**

**The look.** A tinner on a timber at the gallery mouth, with his hat on his knee. On Sundays he sits there instead of in chapel. He would not come to me, so I went to him.

**Name, as given.** Norman. "Reg is the other half of the pair."

**Carries.** A lantern, a watch, chalk and rope. The Company issues them to the hands of a shut mine, so that the hands have something to hold.

**Wants.** "Work in pairs. The shut hands together. Vote on what I saw."

**Confessed.** He was on the shift of the last plug. He was Jory Kitto's pair. A pair goes down together and comes up together. He came up.

That is all of it. He has said it to the gallery mouth every Sunday since, and now he has said it to me, and he does not think it has made any difference to either of us.

I asked him whether any other tinners come to confession.

"Eleven of us left," he said. "None come."

I went back to the vestry that night, turned to No. 8, and wrote one word on what is left of it.

**Where this soul stands.** *With the parish.*

**Said.** Nothing.

**Seal.** Taken. He put it in the hat on his knee and put the hat on.

---

## No. 15

**Tuesday, 14 November 1893. In the box, while the square rioted.**

**The look.** A plain coat, a lantern like Len's, and a voice like a dozen voices at the pump. At first I thought it was Len. It was not Len. Len's watch runs slow and ticks loud, and this one had no watch that I could hear.

**Name, as given.** None. "You don't need it."

**Carries.** Under the coat, the shape and weight of a crowbar. It knocked the wall when the coat moved. In one hand, set down on the shelf and taken up again, a small tobacco tin with a loose lid. In it were things that were not theirs: a thread of blue wool, a little nap of hat felt, a pinch of chalk dust folded in paper.

**Wants.** A townsman's words, said easily, and the ease was what was wrong with them: "Do my share in pairs. Vote on what I saw."

**Confessed.** "I said I was with him at the pump. I was at the pump."

That was the whole of the first part. It was said twice, so that I would have it right.

Then: tonight they had broken a door. The door was already broken, they said; they had only finished it. Outside, the riot was going down the square toward the bank. We could hear it through the wall: the bell going, and glass. They were very calm.

**Where this soul stands.** *Not learned.* I had spent riot night on another soul before this one knelt. God forgive me, I chose another.

**Said.** The next day, at the meeting by the bell, someone asked whether a man had been at the pump the night before. I said that he had. I had it from this box. That is more than the seal allows. I said it because I was asked, and I was asked because someone knew I could answer.

**Seal.** Taken, quickly, into the tin with the other things.

> *In the margin, pencil, a small hand:* Who asked you to?

## No. 16

**Wednesday, 15 November 1893. The post at the Gallows Wood edge.**

**The look.** A woman in a man's coat, standing under the Lamplighter's lantern. The Baron's train was in at the platform with the Pinkertons on it, and their dogs.

**Name, as given.** Not given. She said a name is a thing the Company writes on a warrant.

**Carries.** A crossbow, slung. Bolts in a quiver, and one kept apart in a loop on her belt, its fletching tied with white thread so that she cannot use it by mistake. A snare, coiled. A torch, unlit, for the den.

**Wants.** "The wolves. One arrest. And to be right."

**Confessed.** She hunts wolves for the bounty. The pelt on Hale's barn door is hers. She has been in HollowMere longer than the talk that says the town is waiting for someone like her.

She holds the right of one arrest, once, on a paper from the Territory and not from the Company. She has not used it. The bolt on her belt is the arrest. The Baron's men are on the platform today, she said. If she is right about anyone, they will be the ones who do something about it. She is more afraid of being right than of being wrong.

**Where this soul stands.** *With the parish.*

**Said.** "Wait."

**Seal.** Declined.

> *In the margin, pencil, a small hand:* Did she?

## No. 17

**Saturday, 18 November 1893. The Constable's office.**

**The look.** The window boarded where the riot broke it. A desk with a case file on it. A man who sat very straight, the way men sit who were once paid by the minute.

**Name, as given.** Not given. He said the Constable deputised a Detective, not a man, and may undeputise either.

**Carries.** A magnifier on a ribbon. A notebook. A lens for close work. A fingerprint kit in a tin box, the powder like soot. The stamp.

**Wants.** "Verified clues. The board narrowed. The village does the rest."

**Confessed.** He kept time in the railyard before this, and he still counts minutes. He counted ours while we talked, and told me at the end how many there had been.

The morning after the riot he stamped a thing *Verified* before he had verified it. The town needed a stamp more than it needed the truth, he said. A stamp on the board stops a mob in a way a truth does not. He did not tell me what he had stamped. He asked me not to ask, and I did not.

**Where this soul stands.** *With the parish.*

**Said.** Nothing. "A man who cross-references does not need a priest to agree with him." Those were his words. I write them down as his.

**Seal.** Taken. He stamped the back of the card with his own stamp before he put it away.

> *[Lower margin: a thumbprint, in ash.]*

## No. 18

**Monday, 20 November 1893. Behind the back car, as Maud went out at Dusk.**

**The look.** A woman in a thin coat, mended at the elbows more times than I could count, walking beside me behind the back car.

**Name, as given.** Wenna. She came on the first train, to meet a man who had written that he would meet it.

**Carries.** Chalk for the ring, in a twist of paper. A candle. A small wooden spoon with a rough bowl, which she keeps in her pocket. She showed it to me and did not say who had given it to her.

**Wants.** "To hear the dead. To hear one of them stop."

**Confessed.** She did not go back east. She meant to, on the next train, and the one after. She stays because he still asks about the train. Every night, in the ring, he asks whether it has come. She tells him it has. He asks again.

Tonight Maud is in the ring, she said, and has not yet said anything. Maud never said much in life either.

Wenna does not tell what the dead know, and I did not ask her to. The bell rang for the train. Bar carried one end.

**Where this soul stands.** *With the parish.*

**Said.** Nothing. At the platform I lit her candle from mine.

**Seal.** Declined. "He'd hear it crack."

## No. 19

**Tuesday, 5 December 1893. In the box.**

**The look.** A plain coat, which tonight did not pretend to be anything.

**Name, as given.** None.

I have heard this voice before, and I cannot find the page. Afterwards I went back through the book with the candle, one to eighteen. I came to No. 8 and its cut edge, and stopped there, and went on, and did not find it.

## No. 20

**Wednesday, 6 December 1893. The vestry. My own.**

**The look.** I do not know it. A coat too good for the mud, they said at the pump in October. The mud has seen to that.

**Name, as given.** —

**Carries.** A candle. ~~A watch.~~ See No. 11.

**Wants.** "To know where each of them stands. To say only as much as saves them."

**Confessed.** I have walked behind the back car for Maud. For Elsie. For Mabel. For a man from the bell whose name the Gazette would not print. Half the boots that walked behind him, he had mended. For Cyril, drowned in water I could have stood up in. For Hattie, who took in washing and gave it back folded.

I bought Liquid Courage and drank none.

On my first day I let a child's lie stand. I have let it stand every day since.

- No. 8: I learned where he stood, and wrote it, and it is gone, and I did not say.
- No. 15: I said, and should not have.
- No. 19: I learned, and I have not said.

Gold has been left at Crumb's more than once. Nobody has confessed to leaving it. I do not know that it is a sin to leave gold. I know that nobody leaves it for nothing.

Someone burns letters in the vestry stove. I find the wax in the ash, black, run like tar.

There is ash on the margins of this book, and I did not put it there.

Agnes reads the stone to the Row at dawn. I have begun to stand behind her and listen with the others. I will hear tomorrow's name tomorrow.

**Where this soul stands.** *Not learned.* No one has spent a night on me.
