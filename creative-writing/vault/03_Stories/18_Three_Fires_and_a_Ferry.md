# Three Fires and a Ferry

## 1.

At dusk the Aboyeur set a loaf on the empty platter nearest the Hearth, for a name under seal.

Then the night began.

Fanch watched it from the gallery stair with the master's tool bag on his shoulder. Every soul on the rock had seen it done every dusk of their lives, and nobody looked anymore. He looked. An Apprenti is the one who watches. That is the whole of the office. Its disgrace is speaking.

The master touched his elbow. "Come."

They went down the Counting-House steps in the last of the light, to the Guild boiler-house under them, where the mountain's steam was taken in iron and made to work the Guild's presses and warm the Guild's rooms.

The door was not locked. The master had built the lock.

Inside: heat. Iron. A hiss in the pipes like a breath let out slow. Three boilers in a row, black and sweating. A coat on a nail. A tin plate on the floor, scraped clean.

"Lamp lower," the master said. "Look at the floor."

The boy held the lamp lower and looked at the floor. He heard the master's hands on iron, then a light tap, like someone knocking politely at a door. He did not look up. He was not asked to understand. He was asked to hold the light.

"Nobody's in here at night?"

He had not meant to say it. It came out of him in the heat.

"Empty as a church on market day," the master said. "Out."

They were on the cliff path when the boilers went.

First the ground. Then the sound, one flat blow, like a door the size of the town slammed shut. Then a white column standing up out of the Counting-House steps into the dark, and every window of the Guild Hall lit at once from underneath, and then none of them.

His knees had gone. He was sitting on the path. He did not remember sitting.

He took his father's plumb-bob out of his shirt and let it hang from his fist on its cord. The ground was still shaking. The bob hung true. A line that hangs true is the one thing on the rock nobody can argue with.

The boiler-house had been empty. The master had said so. The boy believed the boiler-house was empty, and the bob hung true, and that made two things.

## 2.

At the bell they carried Pol out under sailcloth.

He knew the shape before the cloth slipped. Everyone on the Wharf knew Pol: the night stoker, no platter, no bed, Unregistered since before the boy was born. He played a tin whistle at the Tavern door for coppers. He played it badly, and people paid him to stop, and he knew it and played anyway. He slept against the boilers because the boilers were warm and nothing else on the rock was.

Nobody had told the boy that. He told himself he had not known it.

The sailcloth was too short. Pol's boots stuck out the end, and one had no lace. When the Iron-Wardens tipped the stretcher to clear the rubble, the whistle slid out of the cloth and dropped into the ash. Nobody picked it up.

The master stood at the edge of the crowd with his hat in his hands. His face was grey. He looked like a man who had lost something.

An accident, then. Nobody could have known an old man slept there. Not the master, who had said empty. Not the boy, who had held the light low and looked at the floor. An accident nobody could have known.

He waited until the stretcher was gone and the crowd had gone after it. Then he went into the ash and picked up the whistle. It was still warm.

He did not play it.

## 3.

The second night the master talked.

He did that sometimes on a long walk, warmly, like a teacher, with his hand on the back of the boy's neck. The gas-lines ran from the vents to the Hall lamps under the Hall's east wall, and it was a long way round by the dark lanes, and the master talked the whole way.

"Pol," he said. "You liked Pol."

The boy nodded.

"He slept there every night. Fifteen winters. The Guild knew. The Ledger-Clerk knew. It saved the Guild the price of a bed." The hand was warm. "Now the Guild has to bury him. In front of the Commons. And every soul on the Salt-Flats will stand at that grave and ask who let an old man sleep in a boiler-house."

The boy walked.

The accident went out of him like heat out of a loaf. The master had known. The master had counted Pol in, the way you count the eggs before you crack them.

He found something else to stand on. It came quickly; it had to. The island was asleep. It had been asleep four hundred years, eating off its silver plates. You do not wake a sleeper by asking. Pol was the price of waking the island. One old man with no bed. A price. Prices get paid.

At the east wall he did not wait to be told. He took the bag off his own shoulder and went down into the culvert first, and held the light, and handed up whatever was asked for.

When they came up, the lamps of the Hall of Platters were going out.

One. Then the next. Then the next, down the long room, until the Hall was dark but for the Hearth.

The Hearth is never let go out.

From the lane he could see it through the great south doors: the red mouth of it, the gilded platters nearest it catching the light, and on the empty platter the dusk loaf, lit from one side.

Only the Hearth, and the loaf beside it.

## 4.

In the dark, someone had taken a candle-end.

That was the charge. The Iron-Wardens found it at first light on the Salt-Flats, burned down to a stub, in the fist of a boy called Gwenaël. He had taken it off a sconce in the black Hall to see his mother home along the Wharf. He was about the Apprenti's age. He whistled through a gap in his front teeth, and the dish-pit women threw rinds at him for it, and he whistled at them too.

It was the last morning of summer.

Morning Judgment was held in Gallows Square, before the Hall's south doors, so that the whole Square could see into the Hearth. The Gallery came down for it. They always came down for it.

Earlier that month a dockhand had gone to the Hearth for a fine he could not pay. The boy had not watched that one. He watched this one.

The musicians first. Then the bell. Then the cloches, carried out on silver and set in a row on the long table, gilded nearest the Chancellor, red crust at the far end. Under one of them was Gwenaël's Wake Loaf.

The High Chancellor read the sentence from a card. It took less time than the music had.

*One candle-end, property of the Council. Taken in darkness. Trial by Fire in the Great Hearth.*

In the gallery above the doors a table had been laid. White cloth. Glasses. The supper after.

The Aboyeur lifted the cloche, took up the loaf and carried it down toward the Hearth, calling the name as it passed. The Square was quiet enough to hear the surf on the reef.

The Hearth received the loaf, and then the one the loaf named.

The boy did not look at the Hearth. He looked up at the gallery, the way you look at anything else when you cannot look at the thing. Madame de Montaury had her napkin in her lap. She was old and very upright, and she had asked for the heel; she always asked for the heel. A footman bent to her. She tasted the sauce and gave the dish back to him with two fingers on its rim.

It had gone cold. That was all. It had gone cold while they waited.

He stood in the Square with Pol's whistle in his apron and the smell of the lampless night still in his clothes, and he understood one thing very clearly. The island had not woken. It had turned over in its sleep and burned a Commons boy for the master's dark.

No. Not the master's. The master had not lit the Hearth. The master had not written the card. The candle-end was the Council's property, the sentence was the Council's, and the sauce was Madame de Montaury's. It was the Gallery's sin, not the master's.

He held on to that all the way home.

The supper was venison. He could smell it from the Square.

## 5.

The third night he went first.

He climbed the Windmill Cliffs in the wind, ahead of the master, with the bag and the coil of rope and the second bag the master would have carried. More than his share. For Gwenaël, he told himself. For the gap in Gwenaël's teeth.

At the top the sails were turning, great and slow and creaking in the dark, the way they had turned every night of his life.

The master's work took an hour. The boy's work was carrying, and holding, and keeping his mouth shut against the wind.

Then the sails stopped.

Not all at once. They slowed. They groaned. They stood.

In fog the fishermen told the hour by those sails. Now the hour had stopped.

No wind in the canvas. No grain under the stones. No flour on the rock by morning, except what the Gallery had in store.

They came down the cliff path by feel. Halfway down the boy heard wings over his head, going out to sea, and the master stopped to listen, and smiled.

"Council pigeon," he said. "That's the Council's pigeon gone for the Crown."

Three works done: the boilers, the lamps, the sails. The island crippled three ways, and the Council crying to the King across the water.

Nothing moved on the rock all night. Not a sail.

## 6.

The Morning Ferry came in on the twenty-third with the Grand Inspector-General aboard, come to put the island back in order.

And the news.

The news came up the Wharf before he did. The dockhands had it off the deckhands before the gangplank was down, and it went up through the Salt-Flats and the fish lanes faster than any coach. The boy heard it at the Wharf rail in pieces, from five mouths:

*In Paris they took the fortress.*

*The Bastille. In July.*

*The Third Estate sits as the nation.*

*The King wore their cockade.*

*They put the governor's head—*

That one was a sailor's boast, and nobody finished it.

The Inspector came off last, grey, with a hand on the rail. He had been sick on the crossing and he did not hide it. He wiped his mouth with a handkerchief and folded it away. Nobody had come down to meet him but the Iron-Wardens. The Gallery had sent a coach, and it went up the hill slower than the news.

By noon the Commons were in Gallows Square.

There was no judgment that day. They came anyway, and stood: red crust, dish-pit grey, fish scale, in the space before the south doors where the gallery's servants used to stand holding the coats. Nobody told them to go.

Someone had chalked a red line across the Square's stones, from the Hall doors to the gallows step. Nobody scrubbed it out.

## 7.

That afternoon the master sent him to the Counting-House with the plans of the boiler-house. The Crown's man had asked for them.

The boy had expected a wig, a sword and the Gallery's own face. What he found was a tired man at the Ledger-Clerk's long table with his coat off. On the table among the ledgers stood a little set of brass scales of his own, the kind the Inspecteur Sanitaire carried in the old days to weigh a loaf against its stamp.

He took the plans. He did not look at them.

"The stoker," he said to the Clerk. "The man in the boiler-house. What was his name?"

The Clerk did not know. Someone behind him said it.

"Pol."

The Inspector wrote it in his file. The boy watched the pen. A dead man's name, first, before the damage, before any rank, written down by a man who did not have to.

"And the boy. The candle." He turned a page. "Bring me the verdict. I'll review it."

"He's burned, monsieur," the Clerk said.

"Then I'll review it."

The boy went out into the lane with his hands empty. The master was waiting by the wall. The boy looked at him and made a sign with his hands, the one for *good*, the one for *kind*. The Crown's man is kind.

The master watched his hands. Then he said, without any heat, the way he said *lamp lower*:

"The Crown's man dies in the fog tonight."

The boy's hands stopped.

The one man on the rock who was reviewing the Gallery's sin. The one man who had asked Pol's name.

He had one place left to stand, and it was as narrow as a step. The master had built the lock on the boiler-house. The master had built the Hall's flue, and been called Rassis for it, and had borne it. The master could see what the boy couldn't: the whole drawing, every course of brick. The master knew better.

It was not much to stand on. He stood on it.

## 8.

The fog came in after the second bell.

It came off the water and put the harbour lamps out, nearest first. The lighthouse beam went to a smear, then to nothing, and the Witching Hour shut over the Wharf like a lid.

The master put him at the head of the Wharf steps with a lantern.

"The Gallery's giving the Crown's man his supper," he said. "He'll come down this way to his lodging. When you hear him pass, put it out."

The boy held the lantern. The fog beaded on the glass. He heard the party a long way off: boots, the Iron-Wardens' staves on the cobbles, a laugh that stopped. The Inspector's cough. Then closer, a voice saying something about Rouen, about a letter, about whether the ferry took the post.

They passed within arm's length. He could not see them. He could see the lantern.

He put the lantern out with his own hand.

The master went past him down the steps into the fog, and the fog closed behind him.

The boy stood in the dark. He heard the sea. He heard the boots go on, and stop, and someone call a name that was not his. He heard nothing he could name.

No lamps. No voices. No ferry till morning. Nothing.

Then the master came up the steps. His sleeves were wet to the elbow and he was breathing like a man who had climbed. In his right hand he had a seal, heavy and gilt, with the Crown's arms on it: the kind that hangs on a chain and sets the King's will in wax.

Then the bell.

The Iron-Wardens' alarm, three and three and three, and lanterns coming down the hill. They found the Inspector at the foot of the Wharf steps, on his back, with the tide over his shoes. His coat was buttoned. His little brass scales were still in its pocket, with the letter to his wife, folded, its last line unfinished.

The boy saw that much. Everyone did. They laid him on the stones under a lantern and left him there while they argued.

The Inquisitor came down with his powder. On the bottom step, where the fog had wetted the flour-dust from somebody's boots, he lifted a print: a builder's boot, square-toed, hobnailed at the heel.

The High Chancellor's word came down the hill at a run. An emergency tribunal at dawn. Every cloche in the Hall to be lifted and every loaf cracked until the Crown's seal was found. Whoever's loaf held it was the one.

In the lane behind the fish sheds the master took the boy's apron in one hand, opened the pocket with the other, and put the seal in.

It was heavy. It dragged the apron down on one side.

"Any gilded platter," the master said. "They'll never burn one of their own."

## 9.

The Hall was dark but for the Hearth.

The boy went in by the kitchen stair, the Commis way, up past the shaping bench, with the Crown's seal in his apron and Pol's whistle beside it. Nobody stopped him. Nobody stops an Apprenti. An Apprenti is furniture that watches.

The platters ran down the long room in their rows, cloche after cloche, each one faintly red on its Hearth side.

He went past the empty platter nearest the Hearth with the dusk loaf on it, which nobody touches.

Past the Vane platters, their silver gone gold at the rims from the heat, the feeding hand's loaf under the largest cloche of all.

Past the flour-white of the Guild, and the master's own platter among them, his loaf still stamped with the Rassis mark. Past the red crust. Past the Unregistered, the bare ones, at the far end by the door.

The Proof-Reader's scales stood dark in their alcove. With the gas-lines out, nobody had weighed a loaf since the lamps went.

*Any gilded platter*, the master had said.

He chose.

He went back up the row to Madame de Montaury's and lifted the cloche. It was heavier than he had thought, and cold inside. Her Wake Loaf sat there round and pale, the heel already scored where she liked it. He pressed the Crown's seal up into the crumb from underneath, deep, closed the crust over it with his thumb the way his mother closed a pie, and set the cloche down again without a sound.

Then he stood there and told himself what every child on the rock knows.

The Gallery never burns its own.

She would be shamed. She would pay a Guild Fine out of the snuffbox money and hate the Commons for it. The master would walk free. Nobody would burn. Nobody at all.

He took out the plumb-bob and let it hang.

It did not hang true. His hand was shaking.

## 10.

The Great Proofing Bell struck six.

The Square was full before the third stroke, and not with the gallery's servants. The Commons stood there, red crust and fish scale all the way back to the chalk line and over it, where they had stood since the ferry came in, and the news stood with them.

The gallery came down anyway. More slowly. Closer together.

The cloches were lifted to music, as they always are. The loaves were cracked one by one, platter by platter, the Aboyeur calling each name as each crust broke. Flour-white. Red crust. Unregistered. The master's own loaf, with nothing in it but bread.

At the long table the stenographer had the docket open. The boy could see it from where he stood. The verdict against the master's name was already written, *Trial by Fire*, to save time.

Then the gilded row.

Madame de Montaury's heel broke open on gold.

The Aboyeur held it up: the Crown's seal, crumb on it, catching the Hearth.

The noise from the Square was not a cheer. It was lower than that.

The High Chancellor looked at the Square. Then he looked for a long moment at his own platter, gilded, in the same row. Then he read from a card he had not had time to write.

*Trial by Fire, in the Great Hearth. The Hearth is for every platter alike.*

By his order, before the ink was dry, the master's verdict in the docket was struck through.

So Madame de Montaury was sentenced to the Hearth, and the master's verdict was struck, and the Gallery gave one gilded platter to the fire to keep the rest.

She did not look at the Square. She took a small thing out of her sleeve, a tortoiseshell snuffbox, and opened it. The boy was near enough to see the pressed violet inside. She looked at it. She closed it.

"May I keep it?"

It was not permitted.

The same cloches. The same musicians. The same bell. The Aboyeur carried her loaf down toward the Hearth and called her name as it passed, the whole of it, three titles long. The Commons stood where the gallery had always stood, and the gallery stood at the back, by the chalk, where the Commons had.

The Hearth received the loaf, and then the one the loaf named.

Then the supper. It had been laid in the gallery for the gallery, as always. The High Chancellor looked at it, and looked at the Square, and ordered it carried down.

So the footmen carried it down the stair and set it on the long table in the Square, and the Commons ate the Gallery's supper with their hands, standing up.

The boy did not speak. He was an Apprenti.

He ate.

It was venison again.

## Coda

On the twenty-fourth, at the afternoon bell, Maître Lescop was registered a Guild Citizen.

The Ledger-Clerk did it at the long table where the Inspector had sat. The Rassis mark was scraped off the master's platter and a flour-white seal set in its place, and the Council gave him a commission, under seal, to rebuild the Guild boilers to his own code. The Inspector's file went into a drawer.

Fanch was set to clean Madame de Montaury's platter before it was given out again.

He scraped the gold leaf off with a blade: the face of it, the rim, the old crest. It came away in curls. He kept at it until the silver showed through, plain, the same as anyone's.

Pol's whistle was in his apron, in the pocket where the seal had been. It did not drag the apron down. It weighed nothing at all.

The fog came in off the water again.

At dusk the Aboyeur set a loaf on the empty platter nearest the Hearth, for a name under seal.

Then the night began.
