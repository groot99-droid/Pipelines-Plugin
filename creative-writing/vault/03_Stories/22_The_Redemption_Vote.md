# The Redemption Vote

## Station Rush. Thursday, 14 December 1893.

I scrub my step every morning. The mud is back by noon. I scrub it anyway, because it is mine to scrub, and there is not much on the Grant that is.

I had done it that morning in the dark, with the brush and the last of the hot water, and the boy held the lamp for me. Then we took the empty basket down to the platform for the bunks' washing, as we do every morning but Sunday. He carries the basket. He is eight, and he carries it in front of him in both arms, so that it walks ahead of him down the Row.

The train was in. Behind the engine there was a car I had never seen on the Line, with curtains in the windows. Dogs came out of it first, two and then two more, on chains. Then men in long coats.

The boy wanted the engine. He always wants the engine. He stood at the edge of the boards with the basket on his hip and looked up at the wheels and the steam, and I looked at him, because the edge of the boards is the edge of the boards.

Then a sound. Not loud.

The whole platform turned at once, the way a room turns when a plate goes.

When I looked, there was a man down on the boards by the curtained car, in a good coat, and the dogs were out.

I did not see it. If anyone ever writes this down, I want that in. I had my eyes on my boy. I always have my eyes on my boy.

## Station Rush, after.

The dogs went through us.

Through the crew, through the mailbags, through the Rows standing about with their crates. They pulled their men along on the chains, noses down, and stopped at a boot here and a hem there, and went on, and settled on nobody. The men in long coats turned the cars out one by one. The mail car. The passenger car. Car 3 at the back, where the boxes ride. They found the boxes. They found nobody.

By then everyone was saying who the man on the boards was. The Baron. I had never seen his face. Now I had seen the back of his coat.

"Who did it?" the boy said.

"Somebody off the train," I said. "Somebody who came up with him. He'll be in the pines by now."

"The dogs'd find him."

"The dogs don't go past the fence."

I said it to him as a fact, and he took it as one, and so did I. A stranger off the train. That was where I put it, and it fit there. Everything bad in HollowMere comes up the Line sooner or later, and goes back down it in a box.

Nobody had thought to stop the washing. We took up the bunks' bundle and carried the basket home full, a handle each.

## The Shift.

She came to my door on the Monday after they hanged him, with a bundle and a shawl, and asked after the lean-to. A bed and her supper. She had been turned out of somewhere, she said, and did not say where. She hauls in the railyard. I said yes before she had finished asking, because an Indebted house takes what knocks.

She has paid every Saturday since. She folds her scrip into small hard squares, like letters she means to send, and sets them by my plate without being asked. I do not count them until she has gone back out.

I know her name. She told it me on the step that Monday. I will come to that.

That morning at breakfast she had said she would be in the yard until the Shift. She said it the way she says everything, as if she had thought about it first.

When the boy and I came up Pump Row with the basket she was at my table with her sleeves up, washing her hands in my basin.

You can hear the yard from Pump Row. The hammers on the workbench. The cars being shunted. I could hear them then, through the open door, over the water.

"You're early," I said.

"They stopped us."

The hammers went on.

The water in the basin was black. Not grey, the way a mill hand's goes. Black from the bottom up.

She dried her hands on her own skirt, not my cloth, and went out to the lean-to. She shut the door the way he used to, with the latch held up so it would not knock.

I stood and looked at the basin.

No stranger. No pines. No train.

My table. My basin. The black water in my own basin.

## The Shift, still.

Then I thought about it, which I should not have done, because thinking is how I got the pine.

Powder comes up on the Train every morning. The yard unloads it with their hands, and it goes under the nails and stays there. I wash for the bunks. Every cuff in that basket had black on it, and I have scrubbed black out of a hundred cuffs since Michaelmas. A yard hand washes at noon and the water goes black. That is what water does in this town.

So I put it there instead. Every yard hand's powder. It was a smaller shelf than the first one, and I had to push to make it hold.

I took the basin to the step and tipped it into the mud. I scrubbed the basin out with sand and set it upside down on the table to dry, the way I have set every basin of my life.

The mud takes everything by noon. I had only brought this a little forward.

## The Bell.

Somebody paid the ten scrip and rang it in the last of the afternoon.

By the Articles I keep curfew twice over: from Dusk, and from the hour of my entry, which was a Tuesday noon. Nobody has ever come to see whether I keep it. I went down to the square with the boy.

The square was full. The men in long coats stood along the bank side with their dogs sitting at their boots. They looked up at the bank windows, where the Baron had been carried, and waited to be told something. Nobody told them anything.

Clem got up on the kit chest by the pump. He had the town's copy of the Articles, the one with the corners gone soft. He reads them aloud at the Lantern, the parts nobody asks for. Nobody had asked now either.

"Article Ten," he said. "Section four. Restoration."

He read it slowly, with his finger under it.

"Any person who spoke against the Company during Public Unrest, and who remains within the Grant upon the President's departure, by whatever means, shall be restored to the rolls as Townsfolk."

Somebody at the back laughed once. "He's departed, all right."

"By whatever means," Clem said, and read the rest. "Their speech shall be struck from the record, and their account shall not."

The square took it a word at a time, the way the Row takes the stone's name off Agnes at dawn. Whoever she was, the woman off the soapbox in November, the Company's own book said she was forgiven if she was still on the Grant when the President left it. He had left it. If nobody stood her up before the Dusk train went out, the Articles would do the rest.

She was at my shoulder. She had come out of the lean-to behind us, and she stood with her hand on my boy's cap, the way you steady a child in a crowd. I looked at her only because she was close.

Her lips were moving.

Not much. A woman at her prayers in a back pew. But it was not a prayer. It was the clause. She had it a word ahead of Clem. "Remains" before he got to remains. "Whatever" before whatever. He stumbled on "Restoration" and she did not.

Every yard hand has the powder. Not every yard hand has the clause by heart.

"We'll have a vote," somebody said. "Before the train."

That meant the last of the light. The Dusk train goes when it goes.

## The sum.

I do every sum in washing. A sheet is so much. A shirt is so much. A week of the bunks' cuffs is so much, and Saturday's rent is so many weeks of cuffs.

The reward on the November notice never would come to washing. There were too many sheets in it. It would clear my line. It would clear his pine, which the Company had billed to my household and was still billing. It would buy the boy a winter.

On the other side was Article Eleven. I do not need Clem for that one. I learned it the Tuesday they hanged him. The account survives the man. It passes first to the household. A man who sleeps in your lean-to is your household when the pine comes. If they stood her up and hanged her, the pine for her would come up Pump Row to the fourth door, the same as his had. A second rope on a line that had not finished paying for the first.

And at noon, before the Bell, she had come in from the lean-to and set two weeks by my plate. Small hard squares. On a Thursday. She pays on Saturdays.

I did not know if it was rent or something else. I did not know what else there was in this town but rent.

I did the sum twice, standing in the square with my hands under my shawl. It came out near even. It always does here.

## Hands.

The names went round.

A dozen voices, the way they go round the pump: a Row name, a mill name, a yard name, one I might have said myself that morning if anyone had asked me. For each one a few hands went up. Then the one named would say where they had been at the Rush, and a voice would say, "With me," and the hands would come down. None carried.

The light was going. Down at the platform the engine let off steam and stood.

Then a voice from the back. I never saw whose.

"Who's in Gert's lean-to now?"

The square turned round to me.

They were the same faces that stood under this Bell the Tuesday the Judge struck my word. Not one of them had asked me anything at the Bell since. I had not been let answer anything. The Indebted may not bear witness. But this was not the Tribunal, and no Judge was sitting, and at a vote anyone may say anything.

Her hand stayed on the boy's cap. It did not tighten. That was the worst of it: it did not have to.

I had not finished the sum. I want that in, too.

"Nobody," I said. "Not since him."

I said it loud, louder than I had ever said anything under that Bell. Louder than I had said eleven, and twelve, and one, to the Judge, before they struck it.

I thought of him then, at my table on that Sunday night, with the boy's boot and his last, by my one candle. How he held the boot up to the light when it was done and turned it over, and whistled two notes, up and down, the way you call a dog you are fond of. The boy wore that boot to the Rush this morning. He was wearing it in the square.

## Hands, the last round.

The square looked away from me.

All at once, the way it had turned. I knew why. They had hanged the last man who slept in that lean-to, after my word was struck, and they had stood here and watched it, and his pine was on my line. Not one of them was going to ask me twice what slept in my lean-to. They owed me that much. It was the first thing anybody in HollowMere ever owed me that got paid.

There was one more round. Nobody's name. No hands.

A man by the pump said, "The Town has spoken," the way the Judge says it, though there was no Judge. Nobody laughed this time.

The Dusk whistle went. The train pulled out. The men in long coats watched it go and stayed where they were.

That was all a vote is. By Clem's book she was restored. She was Townsfolk now, the same as me.

She walked home behind me with the boy's hand in hers. I had soup and the heel of a wake loaf. She ate my bread at my table and said it was good bread, and thanked me for it, and the boy talked about the engine until he was asleep on his arms.

After curfew I sat up with the one candle.

Through the window I could see the lean-to. The door was open. She was standing in it.

A long while. Not moving. A darker piece of the dark against the wash-house wall. I could not see her face, or whether she was looking at my window or at the square, or at nothing.

She did not go in.

I put out the candle first.

## Midday Rent. Friday, 15 December.

The Clerk comes up Pump Row in person every day at the Shift, with a man behind him carrying the roll. Two years I have watched him do it. He has never once spoken at a door. He holds out his hand. You put the scrip in it. The man with the roll writes.

On Friday he spoke.

"Two beds," he said.

He had a voice. I had not known he had one.

"One," I said. "Mine and the boy's. The lean-to's empty. I said so at the Bell."

He looked at the lean-to. Not for long.

"Rent is due on every bed slept in," he said, "and on every bed that should have been."

The Articles' words. He did not need the book for them.

He did not ask who slept there. He did not need to know. The Company does not count people. It counts beds, and a bed that should have been slept in costs the same as one that was.

I paid him in her squares. He took them folded and did not open them. Then he took the pen from the man with the roll and entered it himself, on my line, in a narrow hand, small and leaning, which I had never seen him write in before.

I cannot read much. I know a hand the way I know a stain, by the shape of it and where it sits.

What I said at the Bell, I said to the square, and the square let it stand. On the roll it stood nowhere. Two beds, paid. It changed nothing on the roll at all.

Her debt was paid. Clem had read it out. Her account was not. Clem had read that out too.

## Station Rush. Saturday, 16 December.

The Constable had pasted a new one up on the board by the pump, face out, four tacks. Clem read it to the square over the water.

"Wanted. The Activist. Reissued."

He read the Baron's name and the platform and the Thursday. He read the mill loft, and the four sticks under the last mile, and the soapbox. Then he slowed.

"If she is taken, or gives herself up, the Town will vote at the Bell on her Redemption."

He stopped there. Somebody said, "We've voted."

Nobody told the Company. Nobody is going to.

"Reward," Clem said. "Five hundred scrip. Payable at the Company Bank, at the Midday Rent, by order of the Heir to the Baron."

"What heir?" somebody said, and nobody answered.

There was writing below the stamp. Clem put his finger on it and said it was not the Constable's. I went up close, as if to see the print. It was narrow, small and leaning. It sat where it sat on my line the day before.

Across the square, under the Bell, the Clerk was nailing up a paper of his own. When it was up he stood beside it, which I had never known him do.

Five hundred. I did the sum again, in washing, and it would not come to washing at all. I did it against a household that has already paid for one rope. I kept my hand under my shawl.

She was on the boards with the yard hands, going down to the crates. Her shawl was up and her nails were black already, and nobody looked at her, because nobody looks at a yard hand at the Rush. The boy waved. She waved back.

I know her name. She told it me on my step. I have not said it at the Bell or at the pump or to the Clerk's hand, and I will not write it here, on the back of anything.

I scrubbed my step this morning, before the Rush, in the dark.

The mud will be back by noon.
