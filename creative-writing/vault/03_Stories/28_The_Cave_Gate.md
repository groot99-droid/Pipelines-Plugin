# The Cave Gate

## 1.

Monday, 18 December 1893. Midday.

The slip was on her nail, under the hook where the oil can hung. It was Company paper in the Clerk's hand, pencil, with no fold in it.

*Post 7. The Cave Gate, below the fisher cottage. One lantern. Lit with the rest. Half again.*

The slip gave no reason, and she did not ask for one. She had never asked why a window was lit and never reported one, and she did not ask why a lantern was wanted. A lantern lit was scrip. A lantern dark was docked. That was the whole of her contract, and she understood it better than most people in the town understood theirs.

She read it twice. Then she counted on her fingers, because the slip had done a thing to her round without saying so. Platform, one. The square, two, three, four. The Gallows Wood edge, five. The manor gatepost, six. Then the Gate, seven. Then up the hill again to the fourth lantern of the square, over the chalk and the well, the one she always left for last.

The last lantern was the eighth now. The slip had not said that either.

At dusk the train stood at the platform with steam up. She filled the can at the barrel in the Lantern's back room, to the seam and no further, took the wick-pole and the short ladder, and went out to see the train go. It went, the way it always went, into the pines. *One.* Touch. Light. Touch. The square, the wood's edge, the manor gate. Then, for the first time, she went down past the tavern alley onto the shingle.

The town called it the sea cave. There was no sea. There was the mere, which kept a tide with no sea to keep it, and the hill, and the hole where the mere went into the hill. Everybody called the stones in front of it the shingle, as if a sea had left them there.

The Gate stood across the mouth.

It was iron, salt-bloomed, though there was no salt. The bars were set into the rock top and bottom. VANE TIN CO was cast into the crossbar in letters the size of a hand, and an old chain was wound twice through the bars and the post. A hook was already set at lantern height. Somebody had put it there before anyone wrote the slip.

Behind the bars the cave ran back into the dark. Cold air came out of it. It smelled of stone that has had water on the far side of it for a long time.

She hung the lantern on the hook. *Seven.* Touch. Light. Touch.

Across the water, low on the far edge of the mere, the new light came on, the Company's. She had heard at the pump that it would be lit tonight. There were two of them now. Neither was hers.

Up on the bank the fisher cottage had its shutters across. Nora's girl kept a book in there. Every morning she wrote down where the water stood. The whole shore knew it.

She went back up the hill to the eighth.

Seven posts. Half again for the seventh.

## 2.

Tuesday, 19 December. Dawn.

The storm had held the water up all night. It was over the shingle to the foot of the Gate and in through the bars. At the pump they were saying the coach road was shut, which meant snow on the pass, which meant the road would stay shut.

She came down for her dawn round to trim the wicks and count.

The hook was empty.

The lantern was inside the bars. It lay on its side on a ledge of stone above the water, a body's length back from the Gate. It was whole. The glass had not cracked.

The glass was black.

Not soot. Something on it. Something dark and thick that lay on the glass and ran down it, and where it had run into the water below the ledge it sat on top of the water and would not mix, the way lamp oil lies on a puddle. It was not oil. She leaned in to the bars. It smelled of tin.

The bars were wide enough to let a lantern through.

She did not finish that thought.

She put the wick-pole through and hooked the bail, and drew it back along the ledge and through the bars. It came heavy and slow, and left a line in the dark stuff as it came.

She turned it in her hands. The wick was burned to the collar. The well was dry.

It had not spilled. It had burned down where it lay.

## 3.

Tuesday, 19 December. Dusk.

The mere keeps a tide with no sea. Everybody in HollowMere knows that. There is a chart on the wall by the Lantern's door, an island chart, with the low water circled in ink. Some say the mere keeps to it and some say it only seems to, and she read the circle every dusk with her thumb under it either way.

Last night it had not been low water. Last night the storm had held the water up over the shingle and in through the bars.

So the water had come in. And the water had brought the leak with it. Everybody knew about the leak, too. The flooded gallery under the hill bled mere-rot into the marsh, and the mere-rot made the wolves bold, and it was black, everybody said so, black and stinking. The water had come up, and lifted the lantern off its hook, and floated it in, and set it down on the ledge, and the black had come up with the water and stuck to the glass.

The water moved it. The water blacked it.

She said it twice, under her breath, the way she said the posts.

She cleaned a second lantern at the barrel. At the Gate she found the wrack line from the storm, a line of weed and rot along the stones, the highest the water had come. She took the wire from her coat and hung the lantern from the crossbar on it, a hand above the wrack. Higher than any water in the town's memory had come.

*Seven.* Touch. Light. Touch.

The water, then.

## 4.

Thursday, 21 December. Dawn. Low water.

The escort had a man on the Gate that night. She did not know whose idea it was. The Baron's long coats had been standing about the town since the Baron fell, nine of them and four dogs, waiting at the Clerk's counter to be told something. Somebody had told one of them something, or he had told himself. When she came down at dusk on Wednesday he was on the shingle with his own lantern and a dog, a big man in the long coat, standing where he could see both the Gate and the alley.

He nodded to her. She nodded back. She lit the seventh and went up.

At dawn the water was out. The shingle ran clean to the bars and past them, wet stones and pools.

The wire on the crossbar was wrung round on itself like a rag, and the lantern was gone off it. It was inside again. She could see it farther back this time, past the ledge, black.

He was against the bars.

On the outside. On his back, with his arms at his sides. Not fallen. A fallen man ends on his face. He had been put down, the way you put down a thing you mean to come back for. His own lantern stood upright beside his hand. Its glass was black. The black was on his coat, on the long coat, and on the stones round him.

From the bars to where he lay, and back, the shingle was scored. Drag marks. Long, in pairs.

The shingle there was dry. It was above the wrack line. She had stood on that spot on Tuesday to hang the wire.

The dog was at the top of the shingle where the planks began. It lay flat and would not come down. Another of the long coats came along the alley at a run, and stopped, and called to the dog, and the dog did not come. He called it by name.

It was a woman's name. Annie.

He went down to the man at the bars, and knelt, and said "Kearney," once, and nothing after it.

The water had never come up that far. She had hung the wire there herself.

## 5.

Thursday, 21 December.

The boy brought the cart down with his shoulders in the shafts, and the Undertaker walked beside it with his hat off. They lifted Kearney. The long coat walked behind. The dog came as far as the top of the planks and then it came the rest of the way, close to the wheel.

She followed them up to the shed. She did not know why. Then she did: she wanted the word. Every thing in HollowMere had a word. Somebody would give her the word for this, and she would go home with it.

Doris had the water warm. She washed him. She always told the dead the news while she worked, the rate and the weather and who was barred. She did not tell him anything. She washed his face and his hands and said nothing at all.

The Undertaker unlocked the box under the cart seat and opened his book on the lid. She looked over his arm. The last column had a word at the head of it with a line ruled through. Under it, for the dead above, there was nothing.

"Two wounds," the Undertaker said, to nobody. That was all he said.

He did not write it.

At the pump by noon the town had its word without him. Wolves. The wolves had been bold all December. The mere-rot made them bold. A man alone on the shingle at night. Wolves.

She knew wolves better than anyone at that pump. She had walked among them every dusk for years. They came to the edge of the light and held there, and did not cross. She had stood still with her lantern lit and watched them go round her like water round a stone. A flame near at hand. That was the whole of the rule, and she had never seen it broken.

Kearney had had his lantern. It had been standing upright beside his hand.

She said nothing at the pump. She filled her bucket and went home.

It was the town's word. The town had no other, and neither did she. She let it stand.

## 6.

Saturday, 23 December.

The last train went out at dusk. There were several people on it, and the town stood on the boards and watched them go. She stood with the pole up and lit the platform post as the last car cleared. Then there was only the sound of it going into the pines. Then not that.

The square would not open again until a train came back.

Low water was circled on the chart that night. The long coats knew it. They had been in the Lantern all afternoon, standing, the way they drank. When she came for the oil they were waiting by the barrel.

There were eight of them. They were going in after the wolf. They would go in at low water, when the floor was bare, and they would have it out of there or know why. They wanted light, a lantern each, and only hers were lawful after the train. They paid her for eight on top of her own, out of their own pockets, coin and not scrip.

Nine lanterns. She had never lit so many at once in one place.

On the square they stopped Norman, who was sitting on the pump step with his arm bound to his chest. Somebody said he was a tinner, he knew the hill, he could take them under.

Norman looked at the lanterns. He looked at her.

"Lost in the workings," he said.

He did not get up.

On the shingle the eight stood round the Gate with their lanterns raised, and one of them put a bar through the old chain and twisted it until a link gave. The chain came down off the bars in a heap. The Gate swung in a little way on its own weight and stopped, and they pushed it back to the rock.

They went in. She went in with them, because the lanterns were hers. A lantern lit was scrip, and nobody had said a word about a lantern carried.

At low water the floor of the cave was bare, wet rock and pools, running back and down. The light went before them on the walls. The dogs had not come. The long coats had left them at the planks.

They looked for fur. There was none. They looked for claw marks on the rock, the scoring a wolf leaves where it scrambles. There were none. There were the drag marks, long, in pairs, in the grit between the pools, and the black stuff in them, all of them going the same way. Back.

At the back of the cave was the sea door. The town called it that, though there was no sea for three hundred miles. It was boards, old, heavy, nailed across where the cave went on, and the water went out under them at low water through a gap at the foot.

The drag marks went to the boards and stopped at the foot of them.

The long coats stood there with their lanterns up and argued, low, about whether to take a bar to the boards.

One of them went off to the side, round a rock where the wall bellied out, to look. She heard him counting under his breath as he went. Cartridges. He did it with his thumb on the loops of his belt. Six, seven, eight. She knew that kind of count. She had one of her own.

The count stopped.

Two knocks. Dull, heavy, the way a man strikes a door with the heel of his hand. Two, and then nothing.

They went round the rock with nine lanterns and he was not there. His lantern was there, on its side, black.

They found him at the foot of the boards. They could not have missed him going round. He was on his back, arms at his sides, laid down. The black was on him.

Nobody said wolf.

They picked him up between four of them and ran with him, and she ran with them, ahead, with the light, because they could not run in the dark and the light was hers. Out through the Gate. Up the shingle. The Gate hung open behind them with its chain in a heap, and nobody went back to close it.

There had been nine lanterns in that cave, and no wolf.

## 7.

Monday, 25 December.

No whistle at dawn. There was no train to whistle.

The Clerk went up the Row at Midday with his box all the same, because rent falls on a Monday and the Company does not keep Christmas. She heard about it at the pump. She did not see it.

By Sunday noon a new chain had come down from the Company stores with a new padlock on it, and the Gate was shut on the outfall again. The slip on her nail had not changed. *Post 7.*

She had thought about it all of Sunday, with her hands round a cup, and she had got it to lie down.

He had gone round the rock. He had stepped out of the others' light. One man with one lantern, alone, past the bulge of the wall, where the eight could not see him and he could not see them. And the eight who had stayed together, in the light of each other's lanterns, had come out. She had come out.

A flame near at hand. Not one flame. All of them, together.

It was the rule. It was only the rule, said bigger. The light kept the others. It had kept her.

At dusk she lit the platform with no train to light it for. On the square she turned every wick up as far as it would go, and stood at each post longer than she needed, with her hand on the iron after the second touch. The Gallows Wood edge, the manor gate. She went down to the Gate and turned the seventh up so high the flame stood in the glass like a finger. She did not look past it into the cave. She did not look at the back of the cave at all. She hung it and touched the post and went up.

The light kept the eight.

## 8.

Tuesday, 26 December. Dawn.

She did her dawn count the way she always did, post by post, and wrote nothing down, because there was nothing to write on.

The Gate post. Lantern on its hook, glass clean, burned down. At the foot of the post, black. Smeared up the iron a hand's height.

The shingle. Drag marks, in pairs, out of the Gate and up the stones.

The planks at the top of the shingle. The black on the planks, in a line, going up.

The platform post, at the east end, the one nearest the water. Black at its foot.

The tavern alley. Doors dark all night. Nothing.

Pell's dock, dark all night. Nothing.

The square. Nothing. The fence. Nothing.

She stood at the platform post with her hand on the iron for a long time.

Two posts lit nearest the water, and the black at the foot of both. Not one dark door touched, not one dark step. Whatever came up off the shingle in the night had not wandered. It had gone from light to light.

She remembered then. It had been at the Lantern, weeks back, one of those things the town says that she had let go by her like the rest. That something sleeps in the cave. That it comes to a light and not away from one. She had heard it and thought nothing of it, because it was said the way the town said everything.

The wolves' rule, turned over.

Kearney with his lantern. The man round the rock with his lantern. Herself, with the wire.

She took her hand off the post.

Her posts were the light.

## 9.

Tuesday, 26 December. Midday.

The Clerk's counter. The long coats were not at it. Seven of them stood out on the square now, in pairs, with the dogs.

She said she wanted the Gate post dark.

He did not ask why. He took the slip out of a drawer, the copy, the same hand as hers, and laid it on the counter, and laid the Schedule beside it.

"Only the Lamplighter's lanterns may be lit," he said. "Schedule B." He put his finger on the slip. "Post seven is on the schedule. A lantern left dark is docked."

"Then dock it."

"Docked, at the rate. Half again." He wrote the sum in pencil on the edge of the slip and turned it round so she could read it. "Or you may resign the round. Anyone may."

The road was shut. There was no train. There was no work on the Grant that paid in anything but scrip, and her scrip went on oil, and on the rent, and on the line in his book with her name on it.

She looked at the sum.

He put the slip back in the drawer. That was all there was to it.

At dusk she lit the round, the Gate with it.

Lit or docked.

## 10.

Wednesday, 27 December. Dusk. Low water, circled.

The fourth night since the last train. She had counted them without meaning to. Saturday, Sunday, Monday, Tuesday, and this was the fourth dark since, coming on.

She went down the shingle with the can and the pole and the lantern.

The new chain hung straight down from the bars.

The padlock was open. It hung on the last link with its shackle up. She put her hand on it. Not broken. Not forced. Not cut. Turned. A key had turned it, or something had that did what a key does.

The Gate stood a hand open.

Inside, the pools were still. Nothing on them. Nothing moved on the walls. The cold came out of the cave the way it always did, and the smell of tin with it, stronger.

She stood with the lantern unlit in her hand.

It did not matter now what the Clerk would dock. There was nothing between the cave and the town but whatever she chose to light. Whatever she lit tonight, the Gate or the square or the platform, it would come to.

## 11.

Wednesday, 27 December. Night.

She thought it through once, standing there, and then she did not think it again.

If she lit the town, it would come up to the town. Up the planks. To the platform. To the square. To the fourth lantern, over the chalk where the children played hopscotch, and over the well.

If she lit the Gate, it would come here.

She hung the lantern on its hook. She turned the wick all the way up, higher than the schedule asked, higher than she had ever turned a wick, until the flame stood tall and white in the glass and the light ran off the bars and out over the shingle and the water.

*Seven.* Touch. Light. Touch.

Then she went up. The platform, the square, the wood's edge, the manor gate, in their order. Up the hill to the fourth lantern, over the chalk and the well.

She stood under it with the pole.

She did not light it.

She had always come back to it last. She had come back to it every night she had ever kept the round. She stood under it with the pole up and the wick dark, and then she lowered the pole, and put her hand on the post once, and took it off.

She went back down to look at the Gate from the top of the shingle. She did not mean to go further than that.

Someone was coming down.

No lantern. Just walking, in the dark, down the planks from the alley and onto the stones, unhurried, the way you walk to a place you have been asked to. Toward the Gate. Toward the light.

They passed her close enough to touch. Neither of them said anything.

She did not turn.

She went up the planks, and up the alley, and she did not turn, and the light at her back threw her shadow up the alley ahead of her, long and thin.

Behind her, after a while: two knocks.

She did not turn.

## 12.

Thursday, 28 December.

At dawn the Undertaker's cart was on the shingle. The boy was in the shafts. The Undertaker had his hat off. There was a box on the cart, nailed, and Doris walking beside it with her hand on the lid, telling it the news. The rate. The weather. The road still shut.

She did not look into the box. She did not ask the name. She went past on the planks with the can and did her count and came back up.

At Midday the Clerk counted her scrip out on the counter, to the penny. The platform, the square, the wood's edge, the manor gate. The Gate post, lit, half again. The fourth lantern of the square, dark, docked.

She counted it twice.

At dusk she filled the can at the barrel, to the seam and no further, for eight posts. The chart was on the wall by the door. She found the next circle with her thumb under it.

The square's lamps went on that night, one after another, in their order. The last of them was the fourth, over the chalk and the well.

Then she went down to the shingle.

The chain hung straight down. The Gate stood a hand open. She did not touch it. She hung the lantern on the hook and turned the wick up.

*Seven.*
