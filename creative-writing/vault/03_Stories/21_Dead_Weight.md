# Dead Weight

A dragged crate is a docked crate.

That is the first thing you learn carrying off the morning train in HollowMere, and the only thing anybody bothers to tell you. The Company pays by the crate. The Company docks by the scuff. A sack that leaves a furrow on the boards is a sack that cost you, so you take it up on the shoulder, the whole weight of it, and you walk. You never drag. Not a crate, not a sack, not your own heel.

I know every door in this town by its count from the platform. Crumb's back step is two hundred and twelve. The Lantern's yard door, a hundred and eighty. The Bell is three hundred and some from the Lantern, depending on the mud. I had never needed to count to the Bell.

---

## 1. Monday, 18 December

In his first week a crate of tonics went over on the platform steps, mine, and the Vicar took the other end of it before I could swear. Nobody of rank in this town has ever taken the other end of anything. He carried it up to the Morgue door with me, two hundred and forty steps, and halfway he said my name. I don't know where he got it. Nobody up here uses it.

After that I walked with the threes. I was entered L. I took my tea black at the Lantern because he did.

Sunday the stone said he was dead. Monday at dusk the robes preached.

I went in after. The box was warm from the one before me. Through the grille the voice was his voice, the cadence, the open hands, and it said my name. The second time anybody in HollowMere had said it. The same voice. I knelt there and could not think of one thing I had done.

I went in with the parish. I came out with nothing in me at all, except one thing, set down the way you set a crate on a shelf.

*There's a weight in the loft. Keep it off the floor.*

The hands came through the grille to bless me. I have carried for those hands. I could not have told you when.

The loft ladder is behind the vestry. Twenty-one rungs. I climbed them with no lamp.

He was on the boards under the eaves with his hands in his coat. As if he were cold, or waiting for somebody. A watch was going in his waistcoat, loud in that little room. Not the one on the chain he came up with in October. An older one. Brass gone brown.

I sat down beside him because my legs did.

I had heard the end of Sunday's sermon, or the pump tells me I did. The strength of ten in the next life. I worked it out sitting there, the way you work out a load: what's heavy, what isn't, where it goes. A man who goes on to ten lives steps out of the one. What's left on the boards is what he stepped out of. A shell. And you keep a shell for the one who left it, the way you'd hold a man's coat while he's in the river.

So I would keep it. Not in the loft, where the parish might trip on it. Off the Grant. Down the coach road before light, into the ground somewhere past the last Company post, where nobody's rent was owed.

I got my shoulder under him.

He was lighter than a sack of flour, and he wouldn't split, and I lifted him as if he might.

---

## 2. Tuesday, 19 December, before light

Down the slope, past the old plots, onto the road. I counted. Three hundred. Four hundred. Past the last count I know, out where I keep no counts because nothing of mine is that far.

His heels did not touch. I made sure.

At something over a mile the road wasn't there.

Wind. Water where the ruts should be. The dark, and the noise of the dark moving. The storm had come over the ridge in the night and taken the coach road in its mouth. I stood at the edge of it with a dead man on my shoulder and the water going past my boots, carrying off bits of the road I'd walked on.

Nothing off the Grant now but the Train.

The Train counts the crate, not the contents. It's on the board on the platform, Rule 2; I read it every morning. But a crate goes through the Clerk's book, and a box goes through the Undertaker's, and I had neither.

So I carried him back. Up the slope, past the plots, in by the vestry door, up the twenty-one rungs. Laid him under the eaves where he'd been. Found the chapel's spare sacking, folded on a pew-end against the floods, and drew it over him to the chin. Not the face. I couldn't do the face.

Then I went down to the platform for the Station Rush, as on any morning, because a man who isn't at his crates gets asked why. The loft would have to hold. Lofts do. Nobody climbs a ladder in a chapel.

I had carried a dead man two miles for nothing, and I was not tired.

---

## 3. Tuesday, Station Rush

The parish bag comes off the train with the post. It's mine to carry to the Telegraph Room counter, a hundred and four steps, and it was the Vicar's to come and take.

The robes came. With the voice, thanking the Clerk. They stood where he always stood, at the rail, and took the bag.

And didn't ask the time.

Every morning since October he had asked the Clerk the time by the telegraph clock, and set his watch to it, and said thank you as if he'd been given something. The whole platform knew it. You could set the platform by it.

The Clerk stood with his hand on the clock-case. Waiting. I stood in the door with the crate strap in my fist. Waiting.

The robes went out with the bag.

Up in the loft, under the sacking, an old brown watch was going.

I turned that over, standing there, the way you turn a crate to find the stencil. A man of ten lives has no use for one hour. That's all it was. Why would he ask. He was past asking.

I put it on the shelf with the shell, and carried the next crate.

The clock on the wall kept going. So did the one in the loft.

---

## 4. Tuesday, dusk, and after Curfew

I sat at the back for the dusk sermon. I always sat at the back. The threes don't sit together inside.

It was a good sermon. Debts, and the weight of them. I didn't hear much of it.

Halfway, up in the roof, a sound. Bone on bone. Once. A small dry knock like chimes in a wind, and no wind, and nothing hung up there to make it. I hadn't hung anything. In the box I'd been told I would hear when. I hadn't known what that meant.

Three pews in front of me the Detective lifted his head off his case file and looked up at the loft door.

He did not get up. He sat through the hymn with the file shut on his knee and his eyes on that door. The robes led the hymn. I knew every word of it. I did not open my mouth.

The Detective had looked up. He would go up. Not tonight, with the parish all round him, but by light.

After Curfew I came back with a whiskey case, empty, one I'd carried up to the Lantern full that morning. It is the right length if a man's knees are bent. I did not think about that while I did it. I thought about the stencil, which side up.

Down the twenty-one rungs. Across the square in the dark, the case on my shoulder, eighty steps from the chapel to the Lantern yard, every one of them lifted clean. In by the yard door. The back room: the oil barrel on its chock with the funnel hung on its string, the whiskey cases stacked, two empty barrels against the wall waiting for the dray. I put him in the far one and set the lid on.

In the morning the pump had it. The Detective went up the loft ladder at first light with a lamp. He found the place, and nothing in it but the place.

No smear on the rungs. No furrow in the mud. Nothing follows a carry. A drag is what talks.

The mud had nothing to say about me.

---

## 5. Wednesday, 20 December, Midday

The escort drink at the Lantern now. They have since the Baron, standing, in their long coats, waiting to be told something by somebody.

They brought the dogs in.

One of the dogs left them. It walked across the boards between the tables, past the bar, and lay down across the back-room door. Put its nose to the gap at the bottom. Breathed.

They whistled. It didn't come.

It didn't bark. Those dogs bark at a man who runs, or a man who's armed. I was neither. I was at the end of the bar with tea, black. It whined, a thin sound through its nose, at the gap.

One of the escort took it by the collar, and it lay flat and let itself be pulled six inches, and crawled back.

Bar looked at the dog. Then at me. He did not reach under the counter. He wiped a glass that was already dry.

Nobody in the Lantern would look at the back-room door except the dog.

---

## 6. Wednesday, dusk

I was still there at dusk. Leaving would have been a thing a man does.

Bar set a Liquid Courage down in front of me. I hadn't asked. I hadn't the scrip for it.

"Paid," he said.

He didn't say by whom. The Vicar used to stand them for men who couldn't run to one, and sit with each of them a while. That was his line in Mrs Grieves's book, every time. Paid.

I drank it.

They say at the Lantern it keeps a man proof against any prayer for about two minutes. I can tell you what it is like inside the two minutes. It's like setting down a load you didn't know you were carrying, and standing up straight, and hearing your back go.

The man in the barrel was dead because somebody had killed him.

Not stepped out. Not risen. Killed, on Sunday, at dusk, and carried up a ladder by somebody before ever I went near it.

And the robes were not him. A risen man has no use for the hour, I'd told myself. He'd have asked. Risen or dead or any way you like, he'd have asked. It was the one thing he could not have stopped himself doing.

I was out the door before the glass was down.

Two minutes is about two hundred of my steps. The Bell is three hundred and some from the Lantern. I counted. I always count. Fifty. Ninety. A hundred and forty, past the pump trough, the square opening out ahead, the Bell up on its frame against the last of the light. A hundred and eighty.

At the pump the count ran out.

Not the steps. The other thing. The load came back down on me the way a sack comes down when the man on the other end lets go. I stood on the stones by the pump with my hand out at nothing, reaching, at the height a bell rope would be if the Bell were twenty yards nearer.

I knew. That stayed. The drink went, and the knowing didn't go with it.

I walked back.

I had known for two minutes. Knowing didn't need the drink, it turned out. Only the walking did.

---

## 7. Wednesday, night

The dog was still across the back-room door when they closed. They had to carry it out in the end, two of them, and it didn't fight, only looked back over the escort's arm all the way to the street.

It would be there tomorrow. And the Bell was behind me. I had walked back from it. You can't carry a thing back to a place you've already walked away from.

So it was the yard door again. No lamp. A lamp is a man saying *here I am* to every window in the Row. I got the barrel up on the shoulder, which is a bad carry, and the Vicar in it, which made it a worse one, and I went out the back of town.

Past Crumb's. Past where the lane goes to planks. The Lamplighter had been round: her post at the Gallows Wood edge was lit, the last lantern on the Grant. I went by it on the dark side, over the planks, tarred and warped, and into the pines.

A thicket, twenty yards in. I tipped the barrel and took him out as gently as I have ever taken anything out of anything, and laid him under the low branches with the needles under his head.

This was the first carry I had made knowing what it was. I noticed I'd made it just as well.

Past the last lamp there's no count. So I stopped counting.

---

## 8. The same night

I heard them before anything. Not a howl. Feet, a lot of them, soft, and breath.

A wolf doesn't run at the dead. It doesn't run at what's hidden. Every child on the Row can tell you. It runs at a living man it can see.

A living man, unlit, alone in its wood, kneeling over something.

I found it in his coat pocket while I was looking for nothing: a stub of candle. Pink. The one for the third Sunday. He said once, at the service, that it was the one for joy and he had always thought it the wrong colour for it. He must have pocketed the end of it. I had the Lantern's matches in my vest, as I always do, for the back-room lamp.

It took three. My hands.

Then it was lit, small and pink and the wrong colour, and I held it out at the length of my arm.

They came their six seconds. You can count them. Everybody does. Eyes, low down, the shine. Then they stopped, all of them, wherever they were, like men who hear their names, and went back into the dark. Home, the Row says. As if called.

I stood there with it burning.

A light at the Gallows Wood edge, past the last post, where no lamp is meant to be. The whole town can see that edge from its windows.

It was the wrong colour for it, he said. It was what I had.

---

## 9. The same night, and Thursday, 21 December, Station Rush

The chimes went in the pines. Bone. Once. Nothing hung.

Then a lantern on the planks. Low, swinging on its pole. A cart lantern. The boy at the shafts, and the Undertaker walking beside him with his hat down.

I didn't run. I didn't put the candle out. I don't know why. I think I wanted somebody to see it, and then I think that's a lie.

He stopped at the edge of the thicket. He looked at me. He looked at the candle. Then he looked down under the branches at the length of the man there, a long while, the way he must look at every one of them before the box. The boy kept his eyes on the shafts.

The Undertaker has seen the body, I thought. It's done.

"Not yet," he said.

It's the word his book gives the living. Everybody on the Grant knows it; he carries the book to the Midday Rent on Saturdays. He turned the cart on the planks, the boy hauling, and went back toward the lamps.

He rang nothing. Nobody came.

In the morning, the incoming manifest was on the platform board, as on any morning. Flour, tonics, whiskey. And in a hand I knew, a requisition. Lead, sheet. Officer of the Dead.

I'd carried his first one. On the ninth, off that same train, a roll he unrolled the end of right there on the boards and stood looking at. Longer than any of his forty, the crew said. He signed for it, and I took it up on my shoulder to his shed and leaned it on the back wall where he'd cleared the trestles. Heavy as a man. Heavier.

He had one. He'd asked for more.

I didn't try to know why. I've carried coffins and I know nothing about what goes into their walls. What I knew was this: when the lead came, the Undertaker would take him. Sealed. And whatever I was keeping him for would be finished, and nobody would need a man with a shoulder. I would wait on the lead.

I'd carried his lining. I would carry the rest of it.

---

## 10. Thursday, the pump

The pump says things the way it says the weather. Nobody's mouth. Just said.

That morning it said where the Vicar's body had been.

The chapel loft. A barrel in a saloon back room. A pine thicket at the Gallows Wood edge.

The cave.

I had my bucket under the spout. The water ran over the lip and onto my boots.

Nobody saw the loft. Nobody saw the barrel. The Undertaker saw the thicket and nobody else did, and the Undertaker doesn't come to the pump. Three true. Nothing follows a carry, I'd said, and the pump had followed every one.

And the fourth. I hadn't been to the cave. I hadn't thought of the cave.

"Who says?" I said.

Nobody said. It was only said. A woman from Mill Row looked at me as if I'd asked who says it's raining.

On the Constable's corner of the board in the square the notices were up, the four of them, reissued Saturday. Before Sunday. Before any of it. I'd walked past them every day. I read one now.

> **The Minion.** Keeps a body from being found.

Before Sunday. Before Monday's box. Somebody had written the work down and pinned it up, and then come and found the man for it.

The town was a carry ahead of me.

---

## 11. Thursday, night

I went to the box first. I hadn't been back since Monday. I knelt and said, the cave?

"Yes," the voice said.

Nothing else. I waited for something else. I could hear breathing on the other side of the grille, slow, the way you breathe when you've been carrying.

So I went out past the post in the dark and got him up out of the thicket, and the wolves did not come, because I wasn't hiding him now, only taking him somewhere. Down through the pines to where the forest goes into the hill. In, a long way, with my free hand on the wall.

There's a passage in there that runs down toward the Gallery, the way the Entranced go. Off it, in the dark, a pocket. A room the size of a bunk, dry, with a lip of stone you have to step over. I laid him there.

The air is bad. It catches in your chest and you want to cough and you mustn't, because a cough goes on and on in the stone. And there are feet in it. Steps behind you, ahead of you, that stop when you stop. Mere-rot makes false footsteps. Everybody knows it. I knew it, and I stood with my back to the wall a long time anyway, till they stopped.

At dawn, if it was dawn, I couldn't see, I took the old brown watch out of his waistcoat by feel. It had stopped. I don't know when. Sometime in the barrel, or the pines. I wound it, and it went.

The first of the mornings.

Down here there was nobody. Not the dog, not the Detective, not the Undertaker with his not yet. Down here the keeping was mine alone, and the pump could say what it liked.

I wound it full. A man should have the hour, whether he wants it or not.

---

## 12. Saturday, 23 December

Friday at dawn Agnes called a name up the Row, the way she calls them all. Somebody who paid at the Company Store in exact coppers, counted twice. I heard the name. I won't write it. It isn't mine to carry.

Saturday the last train in came in the morning, out of the weather. I unloaded it. Flour. Whiskey. Tonics, a crate. Post.

No lead.

I looked twice. I went through it a third time, every crate and every sack, the way you look for a thing you've already been told isn't there. The requisition went unfilled. Nobody entered it so. Nobody enters anything now.

At dusk the train stood at the platform with steam up to go out, the last one, and after it nothing till Friday the twenty-ninth, the storm willing. Everyone on the platform knew it. You could see it in how they held their bags.

I had my crate money in my boot. Enough for a one-way fare. I had counted it on Tuesday night, after the road. I had counted it every night since.

Several were leaving. I carried their things aboard: a trunk with a broken strap, two carpet bags, a hatbox, a crate of somebody's whole kitchen, tin by tin. I am good at it. Nothing scuffed. One passenger had a heavy case and would not let me take it, would not even let it go up on the rack. I know the carry of a weight a man wants to look lighter than it is. I said nothing. I took the next bag.

The back car went past me at the rail when she pulled out. Car 3. The coffins ride in Car 3, and nobody opens them in transit, for any reason. It was empty of what I could have put in it.

I stood on the boards with the money in my boot.

The train went at dusk and did not wait. It never has.

---

## 13. Monday, 25 December

Christmas. The Company doesn't keep it. Rent was due, as on any Monday.

I had taken to being in the pocket at Dusk as well as at dawn. Sitting by him. Not winding, only sitting. I had a lantern by then, a small one, turned so low it was hardly more than a coal, for the step over the lip.

That night they came down the passage.

The Entranced. No lanterns. No sound but their feet, and their feet in step, one behind another, going down toward the Gallery. Shapes at the edge of what light I had. Twenty, maybe. I knew some of them. I've carried for most of them. A woman from Pump Row who used to give me the heel of a loaf. A man off the crew. Not one of them turned a head toward the pocket.

I put my hand over the coal and waited, and they went past, and down.

When I took my hand away there was a candle at his feet.

It hadn't been there at dawn. A long one, white, a church candle, unlit, standing upright on the stone between his boots. I knew it. It had stood in its stick by the pulpit at St Brannock's since October, and he had lit it every evening before he spoke.

I hadn't set it there. I hadn't heard anybody step over the lip.

Someone had come into the pocket and stood at his feet in the dark, a yard from me, and set it down, and gone on with the others.

I was not keeping him. I was only one of the ones keeping him.

---

## 14. Tuesday, 26 December

The chimes went in the passage, at what I had for Midday. Bone. Once.

Then light. A lantern, properly lit, coming down from the forest mouth. Boots. One man, careful, a case file under one arm and the lantern held out ahead of him at the end of the other.

Going past. Down toward the Gallery, where, the pump said after, black candles stood on the old tin crate like an altar. He had come for that. Not for me.

But he would pass the pocket going down, and pass it again coming up.

The candle at the Vicar's feet was white. White wax catches a lantern. I had watched it catch my own coal.

I lay down on it. On the stone between the lip and his boots, my body between the passage and him, my hand closed round the candle so not a thread of it showed. Face down. The air sat in my chest and wanted out of me as a cough, and I held it, and held it, until it wasn't air any more, only a stone I had swallowed.

The light came along the passage wall. Into the mouth of the pocket. Across the lip, the length of my arm from my head.

It went down.

I lay. I don't know how long. The feet in the stone came and went, false ones and maybe true ones. Then the real light again, coming up, slower, the way a man walks who has seen something he must tell somebody. Across the lip. Within reach.

It went up.

The dead man didn't need to hold his breath. I held it for the both of us.

---

## 15. Friday, 29 December, Station Rush

Five days nothing had come. Not a train, not a wire, not a sack of flour. The pump got thin on it.

Friday the first train through came in out of the fog, as though it had been waiting at the edge of the weather to be let in.

The freight first. That's my work. Flour. Post. And down the length of the car, a roll of sheet lead, long, tied at three places with cord. A tag: *Officer of the Dead*. No sender.

I got it up on my shoulder. I know a man's weight. This was more.

Then the private car. Conductor Pruett in the door of it with his punch out and ready, and then not using it, his hand coming down slowly to his side.

A man stepped down into the fog.

He had the dead man's face.

Light stood on him. Not lamplight. The way low sun stands on still water, too level, too bright. The fog drew off it all round in a ring, a clean edge on the boards. People inside the ring went still. People outside it stepped in, some of them, and I watched their faces change.

I stood outside the ring by the length of the roll on my shoulder.

He had a crosier, black iron, plain, as tall as he was. He crossed the platform to the Telegraph Room with it, and the crowd went back from him, and he put one hand on the rail inside, and I heard him through the glass. He asked the Clerk the time.

Then he took out a watch. Opened it, set it to the clock on the wall, shut it, and thanked the Clerk as though he'd been given something.

The same watch, or its twin. From where I stood I couldn't tell you. I can't now.

I had wound the one in the cave at dawn. By feel, in the dark, the old brown one, full. It would be going now in his waistcoat, down there under the hill.

There were two watches in HollowMere, and I had wound one of them.

---

## 16. Friday, Midday and dusk

I carried the lead to the Undertaker's shed. Lifted every step.

The first roll was where I'd leaned it on the ninth, against the back wall. I laid the second beside it. Two lengths. Nobody on his forty that long.

He came out of the back and signed the tag where I held it. *Officer of the Dead.* His hand was steady.

"Is it for him?" I said.

"Not yet," he said.

He didn't say for whom. He didn't look at me when he said it, or away from me. He looked at the lead.

At dusk the robes preached on the chapel step, as they had every evening, the open hands, the voice. And the man with the light was somewhere in the lanes. You could tell where by the people coming over, a whole lane at a time, and the lamps not needed. Two of him, in the one town.

I went down to the cave, as on every evening. Past the post. Into the hill. Over the lip.

The candle stood at his feet where I'd set it back after the Detective. The old brown watch was going. I knelt and took it out by feel and wound it, though I'd wound it at dawn, and counted the turns. Four. It had hardly run.

The pump hasn't said where he goes next. It will. The town is a carry ahead of me, and I mean to be ready when it says.

I got my shoulder under him.

On the shoulder. Never dragged.

A dragged crate is a docked crate.
