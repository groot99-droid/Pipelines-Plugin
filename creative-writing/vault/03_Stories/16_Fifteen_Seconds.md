# Fifteen Seconds

## 1

The whistle at the Foundry did not begin. It was simply there, all at once, the way the cold is there when the blanket goes.

Fifteen seconds.

I was on my feet by two. Boots by four. By six I had the button in my cheek, the brown one off my good coat, the coat they took at the gate. You hold a button in your cheek and your teeth can't chatter. A man whose teeth chatter is a man who is saying something.

Slot four. My slot. A number painted on the post at the foot of my bunk, and the same number burned into a pine billet on our crew's rack by the door. Every crew keeps its own rack of ten. The bunkhouse crew's hangs where we can see it while we eat.

Nine. Ten.

Three was on my left, breathing through his nose. On my right, slot five.

Slot five was empty.

Twelve. Thirteen.

I didn't look. You don't look. I counted his blanket instead, folded the way he folded it, corners in. His hymn card on the shelf. His boots not under the bunk.

Fifteen.

The whistle stopped and the arc-lights came up through the one window. White. Flat. No shadow anywhere in the room except under us. Then the door. Then the masks.

Two of them. Rubber faces, glass eyes, the canister snouts. They walk the row with a clipboard and a lamp. They don't say your number. They look at it.

At five, one of them stopped.

"Five," the mask said. It came out wet and far off, a man talking from the bottom of a well.

Nobody answered. That is the answer.

Outside, the alert. The big light on the Foundry roof swung once across the yard and once across the pines. The dogs went out. You can hear dogs go out: the chains first, then nothing, then a long way off.

Slot five was called missing on the roll, and the pencil wrote it down.

I lay back down with my boots on and told myself the plain thing. Five was late at the kilns. They'd had him on clamp work all week. An old man, a slow path, a bad knee. The dogs would find him on the skid road and walk him back, and tomorrow he'd lose his sugar for it and hum about it, flat, all day.

Fifteen seconds is long enough to see who isn't there.

## 2

The dogs came back at two.

I know because I was counting. Not sleeping. Counting the slats over my face, eleven, and the nails in the slats, thirty-four.

Chains. Boots. The handler said something to the mask on the porch, and the mask said nothing back. No argument. No second team. No lamps going out again along the woods trail.

No scent. Fine. The rain, the kiln smoke. Dogs lose things.

Then the mask came in alone, sat at the end of the mess table with the roll sheet and the lamp turned low, and wrote.

I can read upside down. Eleven years at a repair bench in Dayton, reading the dial plates of radios across a counter. I read it from my bunk through the gap in the half wall.

TRANSFERRED.

One word. Block capitals. Beside five.

Nobody had looked for him. The dogs had gone out and come back, and in between nobody had looked anywhere, and the word was already in the pencil. Waiting. As if it had been written in the morning and only needed the night to catch up to it.

Five was entered as transferred.

All right, I thought. All right. Transferred means a train. The payroll train comes Fridays, and tomorrow was Friday. Men go out on it sometimes, men with papers, men somebody higher up has made a decision about. He was in the Office with a blanket and a cup of something. He was going somewhere else.

The sugar twist was still under his pillow. I could see the newspaper edge of it.

Eight weeks of sugar, for a grandson in Scranton he'd never met.

You do not leave the sugar.

## 3

At morning tally a mask laid a form on my blanket and a pencil on the form.

FEDERAL ███████ OF ██████████████ — GROUNDS 99
PRODUCTION IS OBLIGATION.
EFFECTS OF TRANSFERRED PERSONNEL
(NEIGHBOUR TO SIGN)

Five was entered as transferred, and the form for transferred men wants the man in the next slot. So I was the one who emptied his bunk.

Two shirts, grey. One hymn card, soft as cloth from his thumb. One comb, three teeth gone. One twist of sugar, newspaper.

Boots: none. I wrote *none*.

One lunchbox, tin, dented on the lid where somebody's heel had been.

I knew that box. He kept his bread in it and his tonic ticket and nothing else, and it was too heavy for that. I tipped it. The bottom moved an eighth of an inch. Someone had set a second floor into it and pressed it down tight.

Under it, one slip of paper.

Not camp paper. Camp paper is grey and furry and drinks pencil like a blotter. This was thin and white and crackled, and someone had covered it in typed letters in groups of five, five, five, none of them words.

I wrote: *One lunchbox, tin.*

I didn't write the slip.

I put it in my boot, under my heel, and signed. The mask took the form without looking at it, and the bunk was just a bunk again, a board and a ticking, waiting for a number.

Escape money, I told myself, walking out to the yard. That's what it was. A price. Some arrangement with somebody for a key, or a gap in the wire, and the arrangement came due and he went. Good for him. Good for Five.

I had signed for a man. That's what the line said. Neighbour to sign.

## 4

Friday night the arc-light came through the cracks between the boards in stripes. Under the blanket I laid the slip on my knee in one of them.

Groups of five. I tried them every way a radio man knows: backward, every second letter, a shift of three, a shift of four. Nothing. It wasn't written for me to read.

Except for three things.

At the top, typed and then struck through: DEPT. and a black bar where the department should be. Somebody had sat down with a ribbon and inked out whose paper this was.

Bottom corner, in pencil, not typed: a canteen. A child's drawing of one, a circle with a neck and a strap.

And beside it, a number.

4.

Not a group, not part of the cipher. A four on its own, the way they paint it on the post at the foot of my bunk.

So it wasn't escape money. Nobody buys his way out with a department and a picture of my canteen.

I sat with the stripe of light across my knee and my number in it, and I did what I do. I counted what I knew.

One. Somebody with a typewriter and a department had put this in an old man's lunchbox. Nobody in this bunkhouse owns paper that white.

Two. It was about slot four. About the water in slot four's canteen. That was how I read it. That is how anyone would read it.

Three. Five had held it. One night, two, a week. I didn't know.

Four. He hadn't done it. I was alive. I'd drunk from that canteen every day.

Five. He was gone.

So he'd said no. That was the shelf I put it on, and it was a narrow one. He had said no, in whatever way you say no to a slip of paper, and been taken for it, and the word was in the pencil before the dogs got back.

I folded the slip into my boot again.

Five had been keeping me alive. With a lunchbox.

## 5

Because the slip named slot four, I stopped drinking from the canteen.

On Saturday I drank at the pump from my hands, when the line was long and nobody watched hands. Sunday the same. It tastes of the pipe. Everything here tastes of something else.

I stopped sleeping, too. Not on purpose. I lay down and my eyes stayed open, and after a while I sat up with my back to the half wall, where I could see the door, the window and the hatch.

Old men talk in the dark. Eight said there was a lamp down in the Deep Level, in the refuge, a miner's lamp from before the camp, still burning, and nobody fills it. Two said that was a story for new men. Eight said go down and look, then. Nobody said anything after that.

Sunday's roll came and went. I stood. I was counted. I sat back against the wall.

Somewhere after one, under the floor, something knocked.

The root-cellar hatch is between the end bunks, a square of planks with an iron ring. Turnips once. Nothing now, the cook says.

Knock. Knock. Knock.

I counted. Of course I counted.

Fifteen. Then quiet, about as long as two breaths. Then fifteen.

Fifteen is the whistle. Fifteen is the one number every man in this room has in his bones.

Five, I thought. Five in the cellar. Not on a train, not in the woods, nowhere the dogs go. Under the boards, alive, hiding from whatever he'd said no to, and knocking the only count we all share, so that whoever heard would know it was one of us.

I got down on the floor by the ring and put my knuckle to the plank.

I knocked back. Fifteen.

## 6

It knocked fifteen again.

The bunk room was asleep the way men sleep here, shallow and all at once. Three was turned to the wall. The window was white.

I put two fingers through the ring and lifted.

Cold came up. Earth. Roots. Something sour under it, a sickroom smell.

The arc-light fell in behind me, one square of it down the ladder and across the floor.

At the edge of the square, against the joist, someone was sitting up.

Not lying. Sitting straight, the way you sit when you've been told to. Hands in the lap. One knuckle lifted an inch from the wood.

Eyes open.

Not open the way mine were, sore and red after two nights. Open past that. The lids gone thin as cigarette paper, raw at the rims, and they did not close. Not once while I looked. I don't think they knew how any more.

The rest came at me in pieces, the way a thing comes when you can't take it whole.

Skin gone tight over the skull, a drumhead over a frame. Temples sunk in. Lips drawn back off the teeth, split, dried black in the splits.

No shirt. Ribs standing out through the skin, every one. I counted them. God help me, I counted them.

A tube taped into the crook of one arm, the tape gone brown. It ran down into nothing, its end knotted off.

Fingertips black at the ends, the cellar cold had them. The knocking knuckle was worn through to something white.

A jaw that never stopped moving. Under its breath, all the time, it was counting.

And the boots. Laced and double-knotted, the ends tucked down the sides because he couldn't stand them flapping. I knew those boots. I had looked at them every night for four months, under the bunk on my right.

The knuckle came down on the joist once and stopped, because of the light.

It flinched from the square like a dog flinches from a raised hand. A sound came out of it, high in the nose. Not a word. Then the face came up into the light anyway. Thin. Not Five's. Nobody's I knew. Somebody's, once. Somebody who had been made to stay awake past where a body stops. And then past that. And then past that.

It said, "Four."

Not loud. The way the mask says it at roll. Exactly that way, with the same weight on it. Someone had taught it how a number is said.

I dropped the hatch and sat on it.

Below me, after a while, very gently: one. Two. Three.

Five wasn't under the boards. His boots were, and whatever was in them had my number.

It wasn't signalling. It was practising.

## 7

There was a tribunal in the yard at dawn on Monday. Scrip Forfeiture, nobody from our crew. I stood at the back and learned how it goes. They read. They weigh. They decide. A man who goes to them first is a witness. A man they come for is a case.

I had no shelves left. The thing under the floor had my number, the slip in my boot had my number, and the only door in Grounds 99 that opens from the inside is the one you report through.

So I chose.

After roll the mess hall stays lit. One mask keeps the night desk by the iron range, and a man who has something to say goes then, counted and accounted for, with the whole room watching him walk.

A dozen men were at the long table. Not our crew; men from all over, men I'd seen at the pump and never heard speak. They sat with their hands flat on the boards. When the last echo of the whistle died in the rafters, they turned their heads together, the twelve of them, as smooth as a door on a hinge.

Then they hummed.

One note. Low, and all of it at once, the way the whistle is all at once.

I walked to the desk with the slip in my hand.

"There's a contract—"

The note rose. Not louder, fuller. It filled the hall to the rafters, and my words went into it the way a stone goes into the Blackwater. I could feel my mouth working. I could not hear it. I said *lunchbox*. I said *Five*. I said *canteen*. Nothing came out of the hum.

The mask didn't hear any of it. The mask looked at the paper.

White paper. Typed groups. A black bar where a department should be. A pencilled canteen, a pencilled four, in the hand of the man standing in front of it.

The glass eyes stayed on it a long time. Then the mask wrote something down on the night sheet, waved me off with the pencil, and didn't take the slip. It didn't need to.

The humming stopped when I sat down. Not before.

Nobody heard me. Everybody saw it.

## 8

On Tuesday the seam in the Workings burned. A dust fire, the men said, and nobody said who. Smoke lay over the yard all morning, and the Workings crew came up coughing black and were sent to the tonic line.

At noon the work sheet went up on the board.

*4 — KILNS — LATE DETAIL — RELIEVE 23:50.*

The kilns are out on the woods trail, past the skid road. On a clear path, running, a man is ten minutes from the bunkhouse door. Fifteen seconds is fifteen seconds.

I read it twice, and then I understood about Five.

He hadn't been late at the kilns. They had put him at the kilns. The mask had seen the slip in my hand and written it down, and now the sheet put me where the whistle would end with me outside. A man missing at roll is a man the dogs are for. Nobody has to decide anything. The count decides.

A man searched with that slip in his boot was finished. So I did the one thing left to me.

Three was on his bunk mending a sock, badly, the needle going in and out of the same hole. When he went to the pump I opened his lunchbox. I pressed the slip flat under his bread, under the tonic ticket, and closed the lid soft so the tin wouldn't speak.

I am not going to say why. I know why. Fifteen seconds is not enough time to be a better man.

He came back and picked up the sock again.

Three's sock had a hole in the heel. I didn't tell him.

## 9

The Collier gave me the torch at the clamp and looked at the sheet in my hand and didn't say anything about it.

"Keep that torch up."

I kept it up. The kilns ticked. The morning's smoke still hung in the pines. 23:50 came on the Collier's watch and nobody came to relieve me. I hadn't thought anybody would.

Then the whistle.

It didn't begin. It was there.

I ran. Torch in one hand, the button in my cheek, counting. Five. Six. My wife's kitchen clock ran four minutes fast so we would never be late, and I thought of it, four minutes, I'd have taken four minutes. Nine. The skid road under my boots. Ten. Eleven. The lantern posts in the yard, very small, very far.

Fifteen.

The whistle stopped, and I was a long way from anywhere.

And in the quiet where it had been, far off, past the kilns, past the ridge, somewhere I have never been, something sounded. Low. Long. Like breath drawn across the mouth of a bottle. Almost a flute.

Once.

I stood in the road and for one breath I was not afraid. I don't know why. I will not know why.

Then the arc-light swung off the Foundry roof and out across the yard. It went over the woods trail, over the pines, over me, and kept going.

Carried thin across the yard, a mask's voice through the bunkhouse window.

"Four."

Nobody answered. That is the answer.

I know where Three was standing. Slot three, boots on, his breath through his nose, beside a board and a ticking and a blanket folded corners-in. Not looking. Counting it instead.

The chains went first. Then nothing.

And under the bunkhouse floor, I know, something in an old man's boots had lifted one knuckle an inch from the wood.

Three's lunchbox. Tomorrow.

Fifteen seconds is long enough to see who isn't there.
