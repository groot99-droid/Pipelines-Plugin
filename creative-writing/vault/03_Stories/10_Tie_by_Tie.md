# Tie by Tie

> HOLLOWMERE — EST. BY THE RAILROAD EXPANSE
>
> *Station sign, east platform. Painted twice since. Never corrected.*

The sign tells the truth, as far as it goes. The town was laid down tie by wooden tie, spike by iron spike, and the Expanse laid it. What the sign leaves out is the water, the four roads that came to it, and what the last mile was laid on.

Before there was a mere there was a lake, and before there was a town there was a line on a map that would not bend.

## I. Four Roads to the Water

### The Survey

Silas Rook counted his steps out loud. He had done it since he was a chainman of fifteen, and twenty years later he still did it. The numbers came off his tongue like beads off a broken string: four hundred and twelve, four hundred and thirteen. The crew stopped hearing him after the first week. The pines never stopped hearing him. They stood on the north side of the grade like a congregation in black, so close and so tall that the light came down between them thin as milk through cloth.

On the south side were the caves. They were hollow in a way that rock should not be, and they breathed cold out of their mouths in the mornings like cattle.

Between the pines and the caves lay the lake.

It was long and grey and so still it seemed to be thinking. At dawn the fog lay on it like a sheet over furniture in a shut house. At noon it held the sky so exactly that a man could lose his footing just by looking down. Rook walked its shore three times with his level and his chain, looking for a way round, and three times the ground told him what the map had told him. There was no way round. The pines would not be cleared by first frost. The caves would not take a grade. The only line that could be laid ran straight across the water.

Cornelius Thorne's charter said first frost. Rook had never met Thorne. He had met the charter, which was the same thing, folded.

So he did what the Company paid him for. He waded out to his thighs and then to his chest and drove painted poles into the bottom where there was bottom. Past the poles he took sightings to the far shore, and he wrote in the field book, in his neat clerk's hand, *grade sound*. The Company wanted those two words, and he had learned long ago to give a company what it wanted in the shortest form.

In twenty years of survey Rook had never once seen the end of a line he had staked. Other men laid the track and drove the last spike, and other men stood on the platforms when the first trains came in. He was always three valleys on by then, counting.

That night he sat on the shore and watched the poles stand in the water in a row. The last mile was staked across the lake. The poles went out into the dark like the spine of something that had lain down to drink and never got up.

### The Island Man

Tamsin Crumb came inland with the cup held against her body.

That was how she thought of it afterwards: not *with Aldous Vane* but *with the cup*, as if he were a road she had taken. He had come off the island with tin money sewn into the lining of his coat and a stoneware cup with a waxed-cloth lid, and the cup held a measure of the Mother of Mothers. She did not ask how he came to have it. He did not say. On Port-au-Levain the Mother lived in her glass vat under the Great Hearth, fed by the mountain's own breath, and nobody carried her anywhere. That was all Tamsin let herself think about it. Whatever the island thought, it thought behind the fog.

The cup was Vane's. He kept it in a strapped box that rode on his own mule, and he kept the key on his own watch-chain.

Every night at the same hour Tamsin fed the bread on the island's clock, not the mainland's: the hour the Hall of Platters had kept for four hundred years. Each night Vane unlocked the box, and each night she took one spoon from the cup. She did not take it for herself. She fed it into a crock of her own, flour and water and the spoon, until the crock had a life in it that was the cup's life and not the cup. Her daughter culture. It rose in her crock the way a tide comes up a beach, slowly, and then all at once it was there.

The cup went where Vane went. The daughter went where she went.

Afterwards people would ask whether she came inland out of loyalty to him or to stay near the cup. She never answered, because she did not know which. She knew the hour, the spoon and the crock. Some faiths are no bigger than that, and hold.

They came by sea and then by mule through the black pines, and at the end of the pines was the lake, grey and thinking. Vane looked at it for a long time. Then he looked at the caves on its south shore. Tamsin watched him do arithmetic with his face, the way island bakers do it over a proving loaf, judging by the rise and not by the clock.

He bought the caves outright from a land office three days' ride east, with tin money, in a single afternoon. The deed gave the caves and everything beneath them to their furthest extent. The clerk did not ask how far the caves ran. Nobody yet knew they ran under the water. Vane owned what lay under the lake, and only he had thought to buy it.

### The Tinners

Jory Kitto did not like the dark. He would have told you so if you asked, and laughed at himself for it, a tinner afraid of the dark like a fisherman afraid of being wet. He kept his fear in the same place he kept his wages, a tobacco tin in the breast pocket of his shirt, where he could feel its corners against his ribs when he breathed.

The tin was for Wenna. She was eleven years younger than he was and still back east, in a room over a laundry. She ironed other people's collars and wrote to him in pencil, because ink cost money. When the train comes, she wrote. When the train comes, he wrote back, and they both knew it meant the fare, and the fare meant the tin.

The gang came up from diggings that had failed back east, eight men who had signed on together because none of them could read a contract alone. Vane's man read it to them at a trestle table under the pines. The words went over Jory like weather. He signed where the others signed.

They opened the Tin Gallery mouth that autumn. The seam was good. It ran into the hill like a vein in a wrist, bright when the lamp caught it, and they followed it down and then sideways and then down again. The deeper they went, the colder and wetter the air grew, and the gallery began to smell of something Jory had not smelled underground before. Rain. Not real rain. The ghost of it: the smell of stone that has had water on the far side of it for a very long time.

In the evenings he whittled spoons from the offcuts of the props, small ones, with a notch in the handle for a thumb. He had made a dozen and given most away. He kept the best one unfinished in his pocket beside the tin, because a finished thing is a thing you can lose.

In the fourth week the roof began to weep. It was not a leak, only a sweat on the stone, slow and patient and cold, and it beaded and ran like the inside of a window in winter. The gang foreman held his lamp to it and said nothing. Then he took out a pencil stub and the plan, and drew a line on the plan. Jory, who could not read, could read a line. It went under the blue part. The blue part was the lake. The gallery roof ran under the lake.

### The Priest

Father Bray came on foot, which was the only way a man of his means came anywhere. He arrived at the lake in rain so fine it did not fall so much as hang in the air.

There were eleven fisher families on the water. They lived in plank houses on stilts at the north shore, where the pines came down to the reeds. They took perch and something they called lake-cod, which was not cod, and they had been there longer than anyone could say. They had no church. They had the lake-light.

The light hung from a great oak at the waterline on the edge of the Fenwood. It was a ship's lantern in a cage of iron, at the height of a tall man's reach, and a young man called Fennick kept it. He was nineteen and solemn, his father's son and his father's lamp. When the fog came down, and it came down most nights, Fennick climbed the oak's lower limbs and trimmed the wick, and the light swung out over the water like a slow, held note. The boats came home to it. Nobody in that place had drowned in Fennick's memory, and he took this personally, the way a young man takes a thing that is his.

Bray stayed. He raised St Brannock's on the shore with fish money and his own two hands, a chapel of rough planks no bigger than a boathouse, with a door that faced the water and a frame for a bell he did not have.

On the first Sunday he stood at that door and said what the fishers needed said. A priest makes promises on God's behalf, and Bray had never been cautious with them. He swore the lake's water to the fishers. He told them God had given it to them before any land office was built, and no deed written by a clerk three days east could take what God had given first.

The fishers believed him. That was the trouble, later.

## II. The Lake That Would Not Be Crossed

### The Trestle

Since the line was staked across the lake, it had to be built across the lake, and Rook built it.

The crew felled the near edge of Black Pine for the timber, a long bite out of the forest's hem. They left a field of stumps that stood afterwards like the stubs of burnt candles on an altar. Nobody named it then. The name came later, and the stumps would earn it.

They drove piles where Rook's poles had stood and laid a trestle out from the north shore, tie by tie. The ties went down pale and new and smelling of sap, and the lake took the reflection of each one so exactly that from the shore the trestle seemed to go out over a second trestle hung upside down beneath it. Every morning Rook walked to the end of it, counting. Every morning the count was longer.

The fishers came out in their boats to watch, and stayed well back. They did not say anything. Fishermen in that place did not waste speech on water.

At the end of the third week Rook stood at the trestle's head and took a bearing on the far shore. He found himself counting the far shore's distance and not the near's. That was how he knew the trestle reached the middle of the lake.

### The Swallowing

Rook woke in the dark that night and did not know what had woken him. Then he did. Nothing had. The lake had made no sound at all.

At dawn the trestle was gone. It had not fallen, broken or burned. It was gone the way a word goes from the tongue: piles and stringers and a thousand ties, and the two horses that had been left tethered at its end to draw the next day's load. The water lay as flat and grey as it had the day Rook first saw it, holding the sky exactly. There was not a plank floating on it. Not a bubble.

He took a boat out with a lead and line. He let out sixty fathoms at the place where the piles had stood. There was no bottom. He tied on more line and let out more, and still found none.

The oldest of the fishers rowed out to him and drifted alongside. The man was white-whiskered, and the fishers called him Uncle, though he was nobody's uncle. He looked at the line going down and down into the grey, and he said the one thing he said that autumn: "It has no floor, that one. It never had." Then he rowed away.

Rook sat in the boat a long while with the line in his hands. Below him, somewhere, lay a thousand ties he had counted onto the water one at a time. He thought of the field book with its two neat words, *grade sound*, and of the wire in his pocket with its *no exception*, and of the frost, which was already whitening the reeds at the margin in the mornings like salt on a rim.

He had been given a line he could not cross and a date he could not move. Sixty fathoms down, past the end of his rope, a thousand ties lay under the lake with two horses in the dark.

## III. The Breach

### The Bargain

Vane sent for him.

Rook went to the tent on the south shore, below the cave mouths. Inside there was a lamp, a table, a strapped box Rook did not ask about, and a man with the island still in his voice, who listened more than he spoke and listened as if he were weighing something on a scale that only he could see.

Vane laid it out without hurry, the way he did everything. He owned what lay under the lake: the deed said so, to its furthest extent. His men's gallery roof already ran under the lake: his foreman's plan said so, in pencil. And Rook's ties lay under the lake too, a thousand of them, sixty fathoms down and further. So, Vane said, everything the two of them wanted was already down there. The only thing in the way was the water.

He would break the gallery roof. The lake would go down into the mine the way a bath goes down a drain, and in a week or a month the bed would be dry enough to bear a sleeper. The Expanse would have its mile. In return Vane wanted the freight contract on the new line, for tin and everything else, and he would leave the naming of the station to the Company. He did not care what it was called. That, he said, was the difference between him and Mr Thorne.

Rook said he would have to wire. Vane said of course.

The answer came back in a day. It was a single line, all capitals, and it was the shortest wire Rook ever received from the Expanse, because it did not need to be longer.

Rook stood outside the telegraph tent with it in his hand and looked at the lake. It lay grey and still in the evening, holding the last of the light the way a held breath holds the last of a word, and he understood that the lake's water was sold to the earth. He did not mean the shore or the bed. He meant the water itself, the oldest thing in the valley, sold to the stone underneath it at a price fixed between a man with a cup and a man with a charter, and the stone would take delivery.

### The Vigil

Bray heard of it from a tinner at confession. The man was not confessing anything of his own. He only wanted someone else to know.

Bray had sworn the lake's water to the fishers before God. Now the lake's water had been sold to the earth by a clerk's agreement, and between those two sentences stood Bray himself and nothing else. He did not sleep that night. In the morning he went to the stilt houses and told the eleven families what he had heard. He did not tell them what to do. He told them where he was going, and they came.

They stood at the Tin Gallery mouth with lanterns: men, women, children too young to know what the lanterns were for. Bray stood in front of them in his one good coat with his hands open at his sides. The tinners came up the path for the morning shift and stopped.

There was no speech. There was a long silence like the one before a bell. Then the tinners turned back down the path, because to go into the gallery they would have had to lay hands on a priest. Most of them were men who would sooner go into a flooded shaft than do that.

The lanterns burned at the gallery mouth all that day and all that night, a ring of small lights at the black arch like coins laid on a dead man's eyes. The fishers sang a little near dawn, not hymns, which they did not know, but the songs they sang hauling nets, and the cold came out of the cave mouth and moved among them like a tide. The tinners did not come back. The gallery stood unworked.

On the second morning Bray looked at the faces around him in the grey light and saw that they had held. The fishers stood between the tinners and the gallery. For one day the lake was kept by people standing in the way.

### The Bell

Vane did not come with men or guns. He sent bread.

It came up the path on the second evening in baskets, carried by Tamsin Crumb and a boy. It was round loaves with a dark crust that cracked when you broke it, and an inside that smelled of something nobody at that lake had smelled before: sour and deep and alive, like the floor of a forest after rain. The fishers ate. They had been standing two days. Bray ate too, and the bread was good, and he hated that it was good.

Then Vane came himself, alone, and stood just outside the ring of lanterns. He did not argue the water. He spoke about winter. He said the train was the only flour that would ever come over those pines. Without the line the fishers' children would winter on lake-cod and bark, as they had the winter before, when three of them had died. Bray had buried none of them, because Bray had not been there. Vane knew that. He knew the number and he knew the names.

And he said St Brannock's had a frame for a bell and no bell.

Bray stood with the bread still in his mouth and understood that he was being bought. He understood the price, and he understood that it was fair. That was the worst part. It was not a lie. The children would starve without the train. A chapel with a bell could call the boats in through fog when Fennick's lamp was not enough. A priest is a man who makes promises on God's behalf, and here was a second promise, a better one, set next to the first: *I swore them their water; I can swear them their bread.*

He chose. If anyone had ever written the life of Bray, that is the word it would have used: he chose. He went into the gallery mouth with a lantern, raised his hand, and blessed the stone, the seam and the roof that ran under the water. Bray blessed the breach in the same words he would have used to bless a boat. The fishers watched him do it, and one by one they put out their lanterns and went home.

The bell came on a wagon a fortnight later, cast back east with the Company's mark stamped into its shoulder, and St Brannock's hung a Company bell.

### The Last Plug

The tinners had waited for the blessing. They would not have gone down without it. Now that Bray had blessed the breach, they went down.

The work was slow and close. They cut up into the roof under the lake in a long rising drift, bracing as they went, and they left a plug of stone at the top. It was a door they would not open until the end, a cork in a bottle the size of a valley. The plug sweated. In the last days it more than sweated: it ticked, a sound like a clock in another room, as the water found the hairline seams. Then the foreman called the gang together in the lamplight and said someone would have to stay below and strike it.

It paid double wage.

Jory did the sum before he meant to. Double wage was the rest of Wenna's fare and a little over: enough for a coat for the train, because it would be cold on the platform, and she had written that her coat had gone thin at the elbows. He felt the tin against his ribs. He said he would.

Nobody argued. Nobody argues with a man who volunteers for a thing nobody else will do.

On the last night he went up the drift alone with a lamp, a hammer and a bar. The others went up and out into the air, and the gallery closed behind them like a mouth. He sat on a timber under the plug and listened to it tick. He took out the unfinished spoon and turned it in the lamplight. The handle was done and the notch was done. The bowl was still rough, waiting for a knife he had left at the bunkhouse. He thought he would finish it on the platform while he waited for her train. He thought he would give it to her.

He could smell rain. Not the ghost of it now. Rain itself, near and heavy, as if the whole sky had come down and lay on the other side of the stone, waiting to be let in.

Jory Kitto stood alone under the lake at the last plug. Two fathoms of rock and a valley of water lay over his head, and he sat beneath it with a hammer, a spoon and a tin, and he was so afraid of the dark that he left the lamp turned all the way up, though it would cost him oil.

### The Going-Down

He had no watch. Afterwards it was said to have been 11:47. That was said by a great many people who had not been there, which is how most things in HollowMere come to be said.

He struck once, and the plug held. He struck again, and the plug held, and the ticking stopped. He understood what the silence meant and was turning away, toward the drift and the long way down and out, when the stone opened.

That is all of it that belongs to him, and all of it that will be told. The lamp, the turn, and the opening. Whatever was under the lake came down to whatever was under that, and he was between them.

Silas Rook stood on the north shore at dawn with his level and did not need it.

The lake had gone into the earth in the night without a cry, the way it had taken the trestle. Where it had lain there was a bed of bare grey mud as far as the far shore, steaming faintly in the cold like the flank of a horse that has been ridden hard. Down at the Tin Gallery mouth the water stood level with the arch, black and still. The gallery was flooded to its lips, and the great pump beam the tinners had rigged at the mouth hung over it uselessly, like a heron over a pond with no fish in it. No pump in the Territory could have drawn a lake back up out of the stone.

Out on the mud lay the ties.

The thousand ties the lake had swallowed under his trestle lay everywhere across the bed. Some were scattered, some still pinned in their stringers, and all of them were pale and whole, as if the water had only been keeping them for him. They lay in long ribbed rows across the grey like the bones of something enormous that had died lying down, and Rook stood and looked at them and laughed once, a sound he did not recognise as his own. The lake had taken the first attempt to cross it. Now it gave the first attempt back as the means. The ties that had failed to bridge the water would lay the line across the place where the water had been.

Jory Kitto did not come up. He was lost below.

## IV. The End of the Line

### The First Body

They laid the last mile in eleven days, on the swallowed ties.

Rook walked it as it went down, counting. He had never walked a line as it was laid, and he found he did not know how. His numbers kept getting ahead of the rails. The mud took the ties' weight like a held breath, and held, and the iron came down on them in lengths. The spike mauls rang out across the empty bed with nothing to stop the sound, so that every blow came back off the far pines a half-beat late, like a congregation answering.

On the eleventh day they reached the south shore, below the caves, where the station would stand. The foreman set the last spike and handed Rook the maul, because it was the custom, and because for once in twenty years the surveyor was there.

Rook did not drive it. One of the gang, crouching to set the last tie square, had stopped with his hands on it and gone still. Rook came and looked.

Jory Kitto lay face-up in the mud at the end of the line.

He lay as a man lies who has been put down carefully. His eyes were closed. The mud had not taken him; it held him, the way the lake had held the ties. His right hand was closed on something against his chest, and when the foreman knelt, he did not open the fingers. Everyone could see what it was by its corners: a tobacco tin. A small wooden spoon with a rough bowl lay on the mud by his shoulder, as if it had slipped from a pocket a moment before.

He was a mile from the gallery mouth. The water had gone down into the stone, and he had come up out of it, at the very place where the rails stopped.

Nobody could say how. Some of the gang said the channels under the lake ran further than anyone knew. The fishers said nothing, and their silence said it was the lake: that it had kept what it held, all of it, and given it back where it chose. Rook did not say anything. He stood with the maul in his hand at the end of a line, the first end of a line he had ever seen, and the first thing at the end of it was the man who had opened the way.

That night the gang foreman wrote up the week in the Company's book.

> VANE TIN CO. — GALLERY LEDGER — WEEK ENDING
> *Kitto, J. — tinner — lost in the workings. Double wage forfeit (shift not completed). No next of kin on file.*

It is set down here as it was written. Jory had not been lost in the workings. He had been found at the end of the line, by eleven men who knew it. The ledger said otherwise, and the ledger was the Company's, and the Company was the law.

### The Wake

Tamsin Crumb had never baked for a death off the island. She found she knew how.

On Port-au-Levain the dead were mourned in bread. Every soul had a platter in the Hall, and when a soul went out, the bakers made the wake: one loaf for each mourner and three for the dead. Each loaf held a crumb token sealed in wax, so that every mouth that ate carried away a small hard piece of the one who was gone. She had watched it done a hundred times. She had never done it herself, because there it was done by the guild.

This was the first body. There was no guild here. There was her.

She baked through the night in the clay oven Vane's men had built her behind the tents, the oven that would later stand at the back of Crumb's Ovens. She baked from her daughter culture, not from the cup. The cup stayed in its strapped box on Vane's side of the camp, and she did not ask for it. What went into the dough for Jory Kitto was the crock she had fed one spoon at a time every night since the island, on the island's hour. It was the cup's life but not the cup. It was hers to give.

Eleven tinners, and the foreman, and Rook, who came though no one asked him. Eleven fisher families. The loaves came out of the oven dark and cracked, and the smell went out over the bare lakebed like a lantern going out over water. She pressed a crumb into each one and sealed it with candle wax, and the seal set white on the dark crust like frost on a stone.

Three loaves for the dead she set on a plank by the grave.

Bray buried him at St Brannock's, in the first plot on the slope behind the chapel, under the new bell. He said the words. If his voice shook, nobody there knew him well enough to say whether it usually did. The tinners stood bareheaded and ate their loaves one by one, and each of them closed his teeth on the hard crumb in the wax and kept it.

Not one of them went back into the gallery. There was no gallery to go back into. It was full of a lake. But they would not have gone in any case, and everyone at that grave knew it.

The island had buried its dead in bread for four hundred years. Now, by one woman and one crock, the island's wake loaf was HollowMere's mourning.

### The Mere

The bed never dried.

The Company had promised a dry bed. Vane had promised it, Rook had written it down, and the rails ran across it on the drowned ties. But the bare mud they had laid the last mile on stayed mud. The rains came and did not drain. Reeds came up through it like hair through a scalp. The grey went green and black, and the green and black went soft, and by midwinter what had been a lake was not a lake and not land. It was a fen that lay under the rails like a held grudge.

Fennick watched it happen from the oak.

The cough came first. It came up off the fen in the fog, and the fog came every night now, thicker than the lake had ever made. It got into the stilt houses, then the bunkhouse, then the chapel. Everyone in the valley carried it, a wet rattle deep in the chest like a stone turning over in a stream. The fishers called the thing in the fog *mere-rot*. The Company's doctor, when one came, wrote *miasma*. They meant the same thing, and neither word helped.

Then came the footsteps: on the trestle boards over the channels, on the duckboards to the stilt houses, on the planking of St Brannock's porch. They were steps with nobody walking them, steady as a man counting.

Then the wolves. They had always been in Black Pine, and they had always kept to it. Now they came down to the fen's edge and stood there at dusk, a dozen grey shapes in the reeds, and did not leave when shouted at. They watched the lights. They stayed out of the light, every one of them, as if it were a fence. They pressed right up against the edge of it.

On the worst night of that first winter the lake-light fell.

The oak had stood at the waterline for longer than anyone remembered. Now the water was gone from its roots, and the mud under it shifted, and a limb let go. The old ship's lantern in its iron cage went down into the fen, and Fennick, coming out at the sound, watched it sink. He saw it go under and stood waiting for it to go dark.

It did not go dark. It burned on under the black water, green-gold and steady, deep in the fen beneath the oak, like an eye that had closed and was still seeing. It burned the next night, and the next. Fennick never trimmed it again, and it never needed trimming. Nobody in HollowMere has ever said who keeps it lit. Nobody has ever said that anyone does.

Fennick did not ask. He did what a keeper of a light does. He took a new lantern up a new pole at the new edge of the marsh, at the lip of the town that was beginning, and hung it, and lit it, and he climbed up to it every fog-bound night afterwards for the rest of his life. He was young then. In time they would call him Old Fennick.

Vane, for his part, paid a man to keep the wood. The wolves were bold and the pines were Company timber now, and a man with a rifle and a wage was cheaper than wolves. That was how the town came to have a gamekeeper before it had a constable.

The stumps of the near edge of Black Pine, where Rook's crew had cut the timber for the trestle, got their first gallows that winter. It was built of drowned ties: the ones that had come up out of the mud too warped to bear the line. The town had not hanged anyone on it yet. It would. A gallows built is a promise made. After that the stumps had a name, and the name was Gallows Wood.

And the mere had tides.

That was the last strange thing, and the one the Company liked least. It was a fen with no sea for three hundred miles, yet twice a day the water in the channels rose and fell, a hand's breadth, as regular as breathing. Vane had brought a tide chart out from the island, an old chart for the harbour at Port-au-Levain. Someone pinned it to the wall of the tavern that was not yet a tavern, and someone else circled the next low tide on it, and the mere kept to it. Or seemed to. Whether it was the drained lake's channels running under the stone toward some water nobody had mapped, or something else, nobody could say. People in HollowMere still say a great many things about it. None of them has ever been proved.

The lake was a mere now, and the mere-rot was in every chest.

### The First Train

The station was a platform of drowned ties at the end of the line, on a spur along the east edge of the new town. It had one lantern on an iron post and a sign, fresh-painted, that said what the Company wanted said. Bray stood at the end of the platform where Jory Kitto had been found, and waited with everyone else for the first train.

It came out of the pines at midmorning like a black thought arriving, steam going up white into the grey, and the whole town came out to see it. The town was not much yet: tents and plank houses, a chapel with a bell, an oven, a fen. The train docked at the platform where the rails stopped and stood breathing.

It brought flour, and the town wept a little at the flour. It brought whiskey, and mailbags. Out of the second car, last of all, after the crates were down, came a young woman in a thin travelling coat. The coat was gone at the elbows. She stood on the platform with a carpetbag and looked around at the fen and the pines and the strangers, and asked in a voice too careful to be steady where she might find Jory Kitto. He had written that he would meet the train.

Bray went to her. Whatever he said, he said it in a low voice at the end of the platform, at the end of the line, and nobody heard it, and it is not recorded here. Afterwards Tamsin Crumb brought her a loaf from the oven with a crumb sealed in wax at its heart, and the foreman, who had written *no next of kin on file*, did not look at her. Someone gave her a small wooden spoon with a rough bowl. The story does not say who.

Up on the rise, where the south shore had been, Vane's men were raising the frame of a manor. In time it would have a hall, and in the hall a clock. It is said that clock stopped at 11:47 and never ran past it again. It is said a great many ways, by people who were not there. Nobody has ever said who stopped it, and the hands still stand where they stand.

Bray went back up to St Brannock's and rang the Company bell for the train. The rope was new hemp and rough. He rang it as long as the train stood at the platform, and it burned his palms through his gloves. He did not stop.

The bell rang out over the mere, the drowned lantern and the end of the line, and came back off the black pines a half-beat late, like an answer. HollowMere had been founded: on water sold to the earth, a promise broken for bread, and a man who opened the way and was given back at the end of it. Whatever it owed, it owed from that day. In time the debt would pass to people who never knew they had inherited it.

The sign does not say any of this. The sign says the Expanse built the town, tie by tie. It is not wrong.

*The tie under your boot at dawn is who you are.*
