# What the Well Remembers

Father Bray went back east on the train.

My mother said it at supper, the week he went, the way you say the pump is frozen. She said it to the bread. Nobody at the table asked her anything else.

I remember him in three things.

He set the hymn numbers on the board every Sunday, and while he set them he made me read them back to him, and that is how I learned numbers. After Mass he read the Gazette on the chapel step and let me sound out the big letters at the top. That is how I learned letters.

He let me stand inside his arms when he rang the Company bell. The rope went up past my face and came down past it again, and the floor hummed.

His gloves were worn through at the palm from that rope. He darned them himself in red wool, badly. The darn sat right in front of my eyes when I stood inside his arms. I used to count the stitches. Nine on the left hand. I never saw the right one close enough.

I count things. Planks on the platform: forty-two. Lanterns on the square: four. Plates on Crumb's wake table: ten, every time, whether there are ten mourners or two. Counting is how I keep things. If you have counted it, it happened.

So I believed it. Trains go east. Priests go with them.

## 1.

Saloon was over. The Curfew bell rang. The wolves came down to the fence the way they do, and the Watchman walked the lanes knocking on shutters and sending children in.

I did not go in.

There is a ladder in the well on the square. Iron rungs, set into the shaft wall, going down to the shortcut under the town. Grown men use it sometimes in the day, sideways, cursing, because the rungs sit too close to the stone for a man's shoulders. They don't fit me badly at all. I have hidden on it on curfew nights since I was seven. You go down three rungs, where the light from the square still reaches, and you sit with your knees against the wall, and when the Station Rush starts you climb out and walk home like you've been to the pump.

Three rungs down. Knees on the stone. Four lanterns on the square. I counted them lit, one, two, three, and I was still waiting on the fourth when I stopped watching for it.

Up was always the way home. Up was the only way home I knew.

## 2.

Boots on the well-head.

Not the Watchman's. The Watchman drags his left heel. These didn't drag.

The bucket came down past my face on its chain and went into the water below me and came up again full, close enough that it brushed my hair. Then washing. Hands, by the sound. Rubbing. Rinsing. Rubbing. A long time. Longer than flour takes. Longer than mud.

Water spilled over the lip and came down the shaft onto me. It was colder than well water. In the little light it was darker than well water, too. I did not look at my sleeve after. I am telling you that so you know I didn't.

Then the washing stopped, and he did not leave.

He stood at the top of the well with his hands held out, maybe, waiting for something to dry. I could not see a face. I could only see that something was standing between me and the lantern, because the light on the rungs above me had gone.

You cannot climb past someone at the top of the only ladder. You can't even breathe loudly. The ladder up was held by whoever had washed there, and I was small, and quiet, and I stayed still the way you stay still.

He did not go.

So I went down.

## 3.

Below the rungs I knew there were more rungs, and below those there was a passage, low and wet, and at the end of the passage there was the Undercroft.

I had never gone that far. I knew where it came out, the way you know what is behind a door you've been told not to open. The Undercroft lies under St Brannock's. There is a stair from the Undercroft up into the chapel. The chapel door is never barred, because Father Bray said God's house has no bar, and nobody since has thought to fit one.

That was my plan. Down. Across. Up the stair. Out the chapel door. Home by the back lanes before anyone at the well thought to look down it.

And nobody would be in the Undercroft. I was sure of that. Nobody comes down there at night. It is a cellar. Cellars are for the dead and the potatoes.

Dark. Cold. A smell of gas.

## 4.

The Undercroft has two alcoves for the gas, one north and one south. The north one was dark.

The south one was lit.

The lamp was turned down to a bead, a blue drop on the end of the pipe, but it was burning. Under it was a pallet made of sacking, pressed flat in the shape of a body that had lain there more than once. A tin cup. A candle stub, burned to a puddle. On the shelf over the pallet, a key with a brass tag. I could read the tag. STUDY, and under it, smaller, VANE.

Someone sleeps here.

I knew it the way you know a bed has been slept in. You don't need to touch it.

So I made it into something. A stowaway off the train. The Gazette says they come in the freight crates, under the flour, and they have to sleep somewhere when they get out. Somewhere dry. Somewhere nobody comes down to at night. A stowaway had found the Undercroft before I did, and that was all.

I took the key. I don't know why. Children keep things. It went into my pocket and it stayed there.

A stowaway. That was all.

## 5.

There was chalk on the wall by the pallet. Low down, where a man lying on his side would write if he had a piece of chalk and nothing to do with the night.

Four numbers.

23. 140. 8. 271.

I knew them. I knew them the way you know your own name said in another room. They were the hymn numbers from Father Bray's last Sunday. I read them back to him that morning while he slid the cards into the board, and he said *good*, and he said *again*, and I said them again. They are still on the board upstairs. Nobody has changed them since he went. Nobody has set any numbers at all.

A stowaway off the train would not know those numbers. Nobody off the train would.

So that wasn't it.

So it was him. It was Father Bray who slept here, and it was before his train. A night, maybe two, before he went east. He came down under his own chapel to say goodbye to it from underneath, and he wrote his last hymns on the wall so he'd have them. That is a thing a priest might do. It is.

Before his train. Before.

## 6.

If Father Bray slept here before his train, then something of his might still be here. I wanted something of his. I had kept his hymn numbers in my head all that time and nobody had told me I could keep anything else.

I lifted the head of the pallet to look underneath it.

A Gazette, folded flat. Folded the way he folded it on the chapel step, in half and in half again, so it would fit in a coat.

Mine now, I thought. His or not.

I unfolded it to see what he had been reading.

## 7.

You read the date first. He taught me that. *Before you believe a paper, find out what day it thinks it is.*

I read it the way he taught me. Letter by letter, under the blue bead of the lamp. The day. The month. The number.

The number was after his last Sunday. Not much after. Enough.

The Gazette comes in on the Daily Train. That is the only way it comes. So this one came in on a train after the train that took him east.

Someone else brought it down, then. That's what I said. Anyone could have. Anyone could fold a paper in half and in half again. I said it in my head twice, the way he made me say the hymns twice, and the second time it was thinner than the first, and I did not believe it either time.

And the lamp.

The lamp in the south alcove was turned low. Not out. Turned down to a bead, the way you leave a lamp you are coming back to. You turn a lamp out when you go away. You turn it down when you are going up to do something and you will want it again before morning.

Whoever slept here was out, and the alcove's sleeper was coming back tonight.

The lamp was turned down, not out.

## 8.

Up the well was the washer. Down here was a bed someone was coming back to. The chapel was the only other way out I knew.

I took the stair.

St Brannock's at night. Plank walls. Pews. The smell of cold wax. The hymn board, white cards in black slots, *23 140 8 271*, still. The windows grey. The door at the far end. And between me and the door, the place under the bell, where the rope comes down.

The chapel was empty. I told myself that. It was empty, and the door was ten steps away, and the way to it ran under the bell and past the rope, the way it always had.

Ten steps. I counted them.

## 9.

The Company bell hangs over that spot with the Company's mark stamped on its shoulder. I have looked up at it every Sunday of my life.

The rope was moving.

Only a little. A slow sway, side to side, getting smaller. The way a rope does after. After someone has let go of it. After something has come off it. So the chapel was not empty. Or it had not been, a moment before I came up the stair.

There was something caught in the rope. Twisted into the lay of it, about where a grown man's hands would be if he reached up to ring.

A glove.

Not a whole one. The cuff, and half the palm, the rest torn away. The palm worn through from rope. Darned over the worn place in red wool. Badly.

I knew it.

I have stood inside those arms with that darn in front of my eyes. I have counted those stitches.

I started to tell myself something. Other men darn their gloves. Other men ring bells. Red wool is cheap. Everybody has red wool. I got that far. I could not get any further. I could not get to the end of it, and I have never got to the end of it since.

I did not count the stitches. I did not want to know if there were nine.

What the glove did not tell me: whether he rang that bell until the rope tore his hand out of it. Whether he climbed. Whether the rope was for something else. Whether someone took it off him. Whether he pulled his hand out himself and left the glove behind and walked down to the platform and got on the train like they said.

The bell did not ring. Nothing rang.

I did not take the glove. I had the key.

I knew whose it was.

I could not unknow it. I have tried. It does not go back.

## 10.

I went out by the chapel door, which has no bar, and home by the back lanes, and I was in my bed with my wet sleeve under the blanket before the Station Rush.

The whistle out of the pines. The platform lantern going pale. The well-head stones, when I went out for water, dark and wet, and nobody standing at them.

The train had brought a Vicar. He came up the square in a coat too good for the mud, with his bag in one hand, looking at everything the way you look at a house you have been told is yours. He stopped at the pump, where I was. He bent down, because grown men do that to children, and smiled, and asked me.

Where did Father Bray go?

The key was in my pocket. My mother was at our door. Mrs Grieves was sweeping the tavern step, and she had stopped sweeping.

"Father Bray went back east on the train," I said.

The Vicar nodded. My mother nodded. Mrs Grieves nodded, and went back to her sweeping.

Three.

I counted them.
