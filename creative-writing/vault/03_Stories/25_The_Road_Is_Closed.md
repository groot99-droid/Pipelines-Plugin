# The Road Is Closed

## Tuesday, 19 December

Keep to the light, and don't miss the train.

I say it to the boy every morning on the step while I scrub it, and he says it back with the basket on his arm. Every mother in HollowMere says it. None of us made it up.

That morning Clem said something else. He had the paper from the Telegraph Room in his hand at the pump, and he read it twice, the way he reads everything: once for us, and once for the parts nobody asked for. The storm had come down off the hills in the night. Trees were down across the coach road at the Cut, and past them the drifts stood as high as a horse's chest. There would be no coach till the thaw.

The tin sits on the shelf behind the copper. In it is my washing money, saved a penny at a time. In it is Harold's money for my grandmother's brooch, paid out of his own drawer when the brooch went off his shelf. And in it is what was left of the lodger's rent, in the small hard squares she folds it into, like letters she means to send.

That tin was the boy's seat. The New Year coach goes out over the road to the junction with the carrier's mail, the mail that does not go by the Company. I was to pay the carrier on the day. A coach is not the Company's, and a seat on it is not a ticket. The Articles say that whoever leaves the Grant by any way but the Train stays on the Grant in law, and their rent goes on falling due. I had done that sum at the copper more nights than one. Let it fall. Let it fall on me.

Now the road was under the snow. The Train was the only way out, and the Train sells no ticket to the Indebted. I have been Indebted since the pine.

"Roads come back," I said.

The boy asked when.

Roads come back.

## Wednesday, 20 December

No ticket shall be sold to the Indebted. Clem has read us that line often enough. The Articles never say who may ride on one.

I took the tin to Harold's step with the boy behind me, carrying the empty basket so the Row would think we were going for washing. Harold looked at the tin and then at the boy, and he did not ask. He has four terms. The fourth is that he does not ask.

He put on his coat, walked down to the platform window, and bought it with my money: one way, HollowMere to the junction, for a boy of eight. He brought it back and laid it on his counter face up, with his hand flat beside it, the way he lays down everything.

"Keeps," he said.

I would keep to the coach's day, after the New Year. That gave us one more Christmas at the fourth door. I had earned one.

The sale was his. The boy was mine.

## Thursday, 21 December

At the Station Rush there was a wire pasted on the platform window. The flour paste was already curling in the wet. Clem read it, then read it again, slower.

NO SERVICE AFTER SATURDAY DUSK STOP UNTIL WEATHER ALLOWS STOP

The crowd read it over my shoulder: the ones who can read, out loud, for the ones who can't. *Weather allows.* Nobody on that platform could have told you how many days that was.

So there was no after the New Year any more. There was Saturday.

I changed my mind right there on the boards, with my hand round the boy's wrist and the engine breathing at my back. I did not tell him. He knew by my hand.

Saturday, then.

## Friday, 22 December

At the pump Agnes called the stone, her arm still bound up from the wolf. Behind me someone said who it was: the one who paid at the Company Store in exact coppers, and counted them twice before letting go. I will not say the name. It is not mine to say.

The Mill takes the Indebted at Midday and gives us back at Dusk; that is the Schedule. So I could not sit with him in a car through the afternoon. And I would not put him in one alone, a boy of eight on a cold seat, with the long coats standing along the bank front watching the square and nobody to stand between.

So I sat up with his coat. I unpicked a hand's width of the lining at the hem. In went the ticket, and with it a paper in my own hand, which does not write much: his name, where he came from, and *Please*. I had nothing else to put. I sewed it shut with the small stitch, the one that does not show on a collar.

He was to wait at the Mill door with the basket, as he always has. If you lose me, I have always told him, wait at the Mill door. I would leave the wheel at the Dusk bell. I would see the crescent go through his ticket with my own eyes and put his hand into the Conductor's.

That night torches went past the gap under the door. Hoods, flour sacks with holes cut in them. I put my hand over the boy's mouth, though he was not making a sound.

The bell, then the door, then the train.

## Saturday, 23 December

The sale was at Midday, at Harold's step. The list was on a board, and I read down it till I found my grandmother's brooch.

*Gone.*

I looked at the word. I had known it would be there, and it was there.

Then the Mill. The wheel goes round on the mere's tide, and we go round with it. By the middle of the afternoon the storm had taken the light. The windows went brown, then grey, then nothing, and the lamps were lit at three. I kept my eyes on the door. The bell would ring Dusk at its hour, and I would go.

The whistle went.

Not the bell. The whistle: one long, two short. Pruett takes his train out by the dark and not by anybody's bell, and the storm had brought the dark early.

I let go of the bar and ran. Somebody shouted my line after me. The boy was at the Mill door with the basket, where I had told him to be. I pulled the basket off him and dropped it, and took his hand, and we ran the square with the snow coming sideways and every lamp a smear.

The platform was bare boards. Down the line, going away, were the lit windows, four, five, yellow in the snow, and the red lamp behind them. In one window a passenger sat with a case held on the knees in both arms, the way you hold a thing that is heavy. Then the snow took the windows, and then it took the red lamp.

The Rules of Carriage say it does not wait. It does not.

Then the Dusk bell rang at its proper hour, over nobody.

The bell rang Dusk for a train that had gone.

## Sunday, 24 December

There is no Sunday service. There never has been. So nothing was wrong yet, and I told him so, and I told myself. Monday.

At first light Agnes came down the Row with her arm in its sling and called the stone at every door before anyone could ask. She called it at ours too. I heard it through the wood. It was not ours. I will not put it down here.

I kept him in his coat all day, buttoned over the lining, and he slept in it. I sat with my hand on the hem, over the stitches.

Monday.

## Monday, 25 December

No whistle at dawn.

I stood behind the door with the boy and listened. The only thing I heard was Clem at the pump, reading Schedule B to whoever came out, though nobody was meant to come out.

*Station Rush. Dawn, on the Train's whistle. The square opens. The platform opens.*

"No whistle," Clem said. "So."

He did not finish it. He read it again.

So the square had not opened, not since Saturday's Dusk. In law it was still Saturday night out there.

And still the Clerk came up the Row at Midday with his box, because rent is due on a Monday and the Company does not keep Christmas. He did not knock at the doors where the stoves were cold. He stood at them with his pencil and wrote something against each one. The boy said afterwards it was *forfeit*. He had seen the shape of the word.

Then the Mill took me. The Shift keeps its hour even when the Station Rush keeps none.

Our Christmas was a heel of bread. Peg had it off a loaf at Crumb's back door when Tamsin turned round, and the boy brought it to the Mill in the basket. Between turns I came out, and we ate it on the wheel-house step, half each, his half bigger. He asked if it was Christmas at the junction. I said it was Christmas everywhere. I did not know if that was true.

The square had not opened, and still the Mill took me.

## Tuesday, 26 December

The rule keeps the door. That much I had. If the square has not opened, the Indebted keep in, and I keep in twice over. It is the Company's rule, and it had kept me this far.

So I stayed behind the door until the Mill. And I did not scrub my step. I have scrubbed it every morning since I have had a step, through the cough, through the pine. But it is outside the door, and outside had not opened.

The lean-to door stayed shut all that week. I did not knock on it.

At Dusk I watched through the gap. They came past the pump without lanterns, one and then another a hand's length behind, and nobody spoke. They went off the end of the Row toward the Fenwood and the Gallery mouth. Next door's stove went out, and nobody lit it again.

The Row, by morning:

Third door, step not swept.
Fifth door, step not swept.
Seventh door, open, and the stove cold.
Mine.

The mud came up over my step, and I let it.

## Wednesday, 27 December

Agnes told it at the pump that morning: what the sermons on the chapel step had turned into. Debts were not forgiven any more. They were called in.

At Dusk something in the Vicar's robes came down Pump Row. I watched it through the gap: a black hem, wet to the knee, going door to door. It knocked, stood, and knocked again.

It knocked at mine.

I put my back to the wood. The boy held the basket in both arms, the empty basket, as if I had told him we were going for washing.

Through the door it told me my debt. The pine, so many feet at the rate, with the rope separate. It gave the sum to the penny, the Company's own sum, the one on my line in the Clerk's hand, the one I work in my head at the copper every night because I can't afford the paper. It said it kindly, the way you would give out a hymn number.

I did not answer. The hem stayed in the gap under the door a long time. Then it went on to the fifth door.

It knew the sum. Only the Clerk knows the sum.

## Wednesday, 27 December, late

The rule did not keep the door. The law was never going to open this square for me, not ever, not for the Indebted.

So it would be the first whistle, at whatever hour it came, Mill or no Mill, curfew or none. At the first whistle we would go. If the long coats wanted me, they could have me on the platform once he was aboard.

We lay down in our coats against the door, the boy inside my arm. I told him: at the first whistle you get up, and you go.

Before I lay down, I went one step's width out into the dark with the brush and the cold water and scrubbed the step. Just once, but properly. I wanted it clean when we left it.

I left it clean.

## Thursday, 28 December

The whistle came in the night.

It was half a step flat, long and flat, the way Arthur used to whistle the Dusk out every night on the platform until his brother told him to quit. Arthur was buried last winter.

I was asleep. I had told the boy the first whistle. He was up and gone before my eyes were open, and the door stood wide on the black.

I went after him across the square, the square that had not opened. There were no lamps but the Lamplighter's. Snow lay on everything, and his prints went through it, small and straight, to the platform.

He stood at the edge of the boards under the one lit post, with the basket on his arm. There was no train. The rails went off into the dark, filling up with snow.

Past where the light reached, along the line, something was standing. It was as tall as a man, or taller, and as still as a post that is not a post. I did not look at it long enough to say what it was. I have never been sorry for that.

I picked him up. He is too big to carry. I carried him.

A whistle can lie. I had told him it could not.

## Friday, 29 December

The real whistle came at first light, far off and then not far: one long, two short, and in tune.

I had his hand before I had my shawl, and I did not let go of it from my door to the platform, not once. Behind us the Row came out, the ones who were left.

The train pulls into the station. That is what you say here, and it did. The engine came in, then the cars, then the private car at the back, with the snow sliding off its roof in sheets.

I had it worked out. I would put him aboard now, at the Rush, with the ticket in his lining, and sit with him in the cold car with my hand on his until the Midday bell. Then the Mill. I would not see it go, because the Indebted never do. But I would have seen him on it.

I had his hand.

## The same morning

Pruett stood in the door of the private car with his punch in his hand. He did not use it. He stood aside.

A man stepped down into the fog. He had the Vicar's face, the dead Vicar's; everyone in HollowMere knows that face. He carried a staff of black iron, crooked at the top like a shepherd's and taller than he was.

The light on him lay flat, like the last of the sun lying on a pail of water, and there was too much of it. The fog went back off him all round, the way steam goes back off a pan when you lift the lid.

I do not know what my hand did. I know what the boy's did. It went out of mine. He did not pull or tug. It was in mine, and then it was not, the way water goes out of a cloth.

He went up the square after the light. They all did: the crew, the Row, the ones who had sat with their hands in their laps since the Monday before, all walking after it in the snow. The ticket was still sewn in his coat.

I called his name, once.

I went after him and could not get through. It was all backs and shoulders, everyone facing the one way. The light went in at the Telegraph Room door, to the Clerk's counter, and the crowd pushed in behind it as far as it could and stood. I lost him there. I saw a cap, and then I didn't.

He did not look back. He was looking at the light.

## Midday to Dusk

He knows it: if you lose me, the Mill door.

So at the Midday bell I went where the Mill takes the Indebted, because that was where he would be.

He was not at the door. There was no basket.

I went in and took the bar, and I watched the door all afternoon. Each time it opened, it was someone else.

Dusk came at its hour. There was no storm. For the first time in ten days the sky was clear at the edges, a strip of green-white over the pines.

The whistle went: one long, two short, in tune.

From the wheel, for the second time, I heard the train go out. I do not know if he was on it. The ticket is in his coat, and the coat is on him, or on a peg somewhere in the light. Nobody punched it that I saw. Nobody would tell me if they had.

The bell rang Dusk.

Keep to the light, I told him, every morning of his life. And don't miss the train.
