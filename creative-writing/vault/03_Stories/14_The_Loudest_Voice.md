# The Loudest Voice

Somebody has to ask first.

## Walk in a borrowed character.

The stone is up before the train.

It stands at the edge of the square where the lanes give out. Grey. About the height of a child. Tomorrow's date cut in it, the way it always is, and under the date a name. Nobody has ever seen a name being cut. There is a new one every morning anyway.

Agnes reads it to the Row at dawn, because half the Row can't, and you hear her from the pump. One word. Then the Row goes quiet, the way a kitchen does when a cup breaks.

HATTIE.

You chose her last night. You lay on your back in the dark and went through the town door by door, and you stopped at hers, and you slept. Then the stone. You have never settled which of you goes first, you or the stone, which of you is following the other. You don't try to settle it this morning either. You get up.

Coat. Hat. Scarf.

They go on the way weather does. It is not your coat. It has never been your coat. The square says good morning to it every day of the week, and that is the whole trick: nobody looks twice at a thing they already say good morning to.

Hattie is at the pump with her two buckets. Her hands are red to the wrist from the copper, the way a washerwoman's are once the cold sets in. She has heard the stone. Everyone has. She fills the first bucket, looks up at the borrowed face, and nods.

"Morning."

"Morning."

"Stone's got me today," she says. "Wash still wants doing." She works the handle. "I've asked the Watchman. He'll stand at my door tonight. Paid him in sheets. First bed he's had with no holes in it."

She says it the way you'd mention a debt you've settled. Then she lifts both buckets and goes down the Rise to the wash-house with the water slopping. She doesn't look back at the stone. You understand that she has decided not to.

So the whole town knows the Watchman will stand at her door tonight.

So do you.

## Curfew.

The Lamplighter comes round at dusk. Platform first, then the square, and the fourth lantern, the one over the chalk and the well, last of all. She always leaves that one till last. The Saloon empties. The Curfew bell rings. The wolves come down to the fence and walk the length of it.

By then the Watchman is at the wash-house door: lantern on the step, collar up. When he settles his weight his left heel drags on the stone, one short scrape, and stops.

You are across the lane at the foot of the Rise, in the borrowed coat, doing nothing a neighbour wouldn't do at curfew. Walking home slowly. Looking at nothing.

Then a voice from the pines side.

"Wolves! Wolves in the square!"

High. Pitched to carry. Pitched, exactly, to carry to the bottom of the Rise and no further.

You know that voice. You do not turn your head toward it. That is the one rule between you and the other one, older than any other: you never look at the other one where anyone can see. *You never hold the knife, only the alibi.* That is their card, not yours, and they keep it better than you keep yours.

The Watchman looks at Hattie's door. He looks at the dark toward the fence. He looks at the door again.

He goes, lantern swinging, heel scraping, scrape and scrape up the lane, out of hearing.

Her door is unwatched. You didn't even have to ask.

## Strike after dark when the room is empty.

The room is empty. The card promised it would be, and the card is right.

Sheets, rows of them, pegged on lines across the room so that the room is all corridors. The copper, banked low. The mangle. Lye. A jar on the sill.

Some things go into you whether you want them or not.

Over the copper, chalked big in careful capitals: HATTIE. It is the one word she can write, and she has written it where she'll see it every morning. On the shelf there is a letter half done in somebody else's hand. *Dear son, the work is steady and the* — and nothing after. The Postmaster writes it for her at a scrip a week, a line at a time, and this week's scrip hasn't been paid.

The jar is buttons. You tip it to the window and they slide: horn, bone, brass, one shell. One from every dead man's shirt she washed for the box. Somebody ought to keep something. You don't know that she ever said that. You think she must have.

You put the jar back where the ring in the dust says it goes.

On the nail by the door hangs her own shawl, washed thinner than anything she takes in. Everyone's washing comes before hers. Under the copper there's a pair of boots, the soles stuffed with Gazette against the wet, set side by side, toes to the wall, the way you'd set them if somebody had once told you to and you'd never stopped.

Then you stand between two sheets and wait.

She comes in with the last basket on her hip, humming. It isn't a hymn, just a tune with no words left in it. An hour ago she heard the Watchman settle at her door, and she believes he is still there. You can see her believe it: her shoulders down, the latch left up behind her.

"Night, then," she says, over her shoulder, to the empty step.

She sets the basket down. She stands a moment with her hands in the small of her back, looking at her rows, counting them the way she must count them every night. She has had all day to run, and she spent it on the town's sheets. There is a second where you think she knows. Then she reaches up to unpeg the nearest sheet, and the nearest sheet is the one you are behind.

A cold edge, and the lantern goes out.

Her hand comes up. It finds the end of the borrowed scarf and closes on it, and it does not open again.

You work at her fingers. A thread comes away into them. One thread, the weave of every scarf on the square. You could get it out. You stop, because the card says one clue, and you haven't seen what else there is yet.

Nobody saw. You believe that all the way to the door.

## Clean one clue.

The doorframe. At the height of a child's shoulder.

Four strokes and a bar across them, in white chalk, the dust still loose on the wood. It was not there when you went in. You came in by this door. You would know.

Children chalk every door in this town. There is hopscotch under the fourth lantern every morning, and nobody ever seen drawing it. There are marks on the shutters. Townsfolk carry chalk and mark what they see; it's written on their cards. So it's a wash tally. Five sheets owed. A game.

Nothing.

That is three explanations, each one shorter than the last. You stand in the doorway with the shelf half built and you decide: children's chalk. That is what it is.

But the card says one.

A thread is a weave, and half the square wears it. A tally is somebody who stood here and counted.

You take the rag from your pocket and wipe the frame until the wood there is darker than the wood around it. You leave the thread where it is, in her fist.

The chalk is gone. Your hands are wet to the wrist.

## Wash at the well.

The square is empty, and the fourth lantern burns over the chalk and the well.

The bucket goes down on its chain, past the rungs in the shaft, into the water, and comes up full.

Hands first. Rub. Rinse. Rub. Longer than flour takes, longer than mud. What you tip back over the lip looks darker than well water in that light. You don't look at it twice.

Then, below you, a breath.

Three rungs down. Maybe four. One breath, held too long and let go. Then nothing. Nothing at all, which is worse than the breath.

You stand at the lip with your hands out as if you are waiting for them to dry. You are between the lantern and the shaft, so whatever is down there has no light now except what gets past you. You don't call down. You don't leave. You stand, and the wet goes cold on your hands, and you listen.

Then iron. A small sound on the rungs, going down. Slow. Careful. Away.

Mere-rot. The fen puts feet where there aren't any, and everyone in HollowMere knows it. The Lamplighter has heard steps on these rungs and not gone to look. That's all it is.

"Mere-rot," you say out loud, to nobody. You dry your hands on the borrowed coat and walk home across the square in boots that don't drag.

## Then be the loudest voice asking who did it.

She is found at first light by somebody coming for their sheets. The Bell goes. The square fills the way it always does: coats over nightshirts, the platform crew straight off the Rush, Mrs Grieves on the tavern step holding a broom she hasn't put down.

"Who did it?"

You say it first, and loudest, before the Bell has finished. It is the borrowed character's voice, the part of the borrowed character that has always fitted best.

The Detective has a table set up at the Bell: magnifier, notebook, the case file. And on a square of clean paper, the thread, out of her hand.

The Detective holds it up to the light. The square looks at it. Then the square looks at scarves.

It isn't slow. It runs along the crowd like wind along wheat. Eyes drop to the throat of the man alongside, the woman alongside, their own. Half the square wears that weave, and yours is in that half. One pair of eyes rests on your scarf and stays there for the length of a breath. Then another pair.

You don't touch it. You don't look at anybody else's either. You say it again, louder: who did it? You say her name with it this time. You say what she was. A good woman, you say. Did the town's wash. The crowd murmurs yes, and the yes comes back to you warm, and you hold on to it.

There's a girl in the crowd by the pump, about nine. The one who counts things. Everyone knows she counts things. She isn't looking at faces. She is looking at feet, the whole front row of them, and her lips are moving.

The Detective says, "Anyone with something to say, step up."

You step up. You have something to say.

Her eyes come with your boots. One. Two. Three.

You stop.

You stand still the way you stood still at the well. She goes on watching your boots, lips going, then moves to the next pair and the next. It's nothing. She counts everything. She counts the planks on the platform. But now you can hear your own steps, every one of them. You couldn't cross the square without hearing each one land, and you know you'll still be hearing them tomorrow.

The Detective has a second find, and it isn't on the paper. It's in the mud: a trail from the fence line down the lane to the wash-house door. Boot prints, and behind each one a long gouge where a heel was dragged.

The square turns its head. Nobody has to tell it whose heel drags.

The Watchman tells them the truth. He stood at her door. There was a cry at the fence, wolves in the square, and he went, because that is what the lantern is for.

"There were no wolves in the square last night," you say.

You heard the cry. You were at the foot of the Rise when it came. You say it louder than he told the truth, and your voice goes over the heads of the crowd and comes down on him.

Somebody near the table turns to you. Where were you, then, at curfew?

Before you can open your mouth, a voice at the back says, "At the pump. With me."

You don't turn round. You never turn round.

The square's eyes come off your scarf. They drop, all together, to the Watchman's heel.

## The town has spoken.

The Tribunal sits at the Bell. It doesn't take long; it never does. It can say three things: Acquittal, a Heavy Fine, or Death.

"Rope," you say.

Just the one word, loud. It leaves you and goes into the crowd, and the crowd takes it up, mouth to mouth, *rope, rope*, until it isn't your word any more. It belongs to the square, and nobody remembers who said it first.

The Tribunal gives a Heavy Fine: three months' scrip. Then it takes his lantern off him, there on the table beside the thread, and stands him down.

Nobody will stand at a door tonight. No one stands at any door in HollowMere now.

The girl is there for the vote. She watches the Watchman's heel all the way off the square, and if she knows anything about heels, she doesn't say it. She counts the hands that went up for the rope. You watch her count them. You know you are one of the hands.

## The basin.

Home. The door barred. Basin, jug, water.

Hat. Scarf. Coat.

You look for your face.

Not in a glass. You don't need a glass. You look from the inside, the way you know your own hand in the dark. You go looking for the face under the borrowed one, the one that was there before the coat, and there is nothing where it ought to be. Not a blank. Not a dark. Just the coat on the chair, and the voice in your throat still asking who did it. It is the coat's voice, and it is the only one you have.

The square says good morning to the coat. Hattie said good morning to the coat. The coat is the one with the voice.

You put it back on. It goes on easier than it ever has. You know it will not come off again.

The girl counted your steps. One day she will say a number out loud to somebody who writes things down. So you take out the knife and lay it on the sill, under the sacking, in the place the other one knows to look if you don't come home. If they come for you, the other one finishes it. It is the one thing you have done all day that was not for the square, and even that is for whoever comes after you.

Dawn. The stone. A new name. You read it, and you already know the door.

The square is filling. The Bell hasn't finished.

Somebody has to ask first.
