# What the Wolves Were Blamed For

## Six seconds

Thursday, the thirtieth of November. Twilight. In HollowMere twilight lasts a quarter of an hour, and then it is gone.

I was coming back from the mill's timber stack at the wood's edge with no light. A lantern costs scrip, and scrip is rent. I had counted the stack, forty-one lengths, on the hand that has five. I was thinking of nothing but the number.

Then the footsteps.

They were behind me on the planks. Boot on board, boot on board, on the tarred planks that give a little under a man. I stopped, and they stopped. I went on, and they came on.

I turned round. There was nobody on the planks.

Mere-rot does that. Everybody knows it does. You hear footsteps where nobody walks. I knew it, and I ran anyway, and I ran the wrong way: off the planks, into the pines, into the mud that takes your boot and keeps it.

I had been gored at the wood's edge in October, by a boar, in daylight. I walked to Mallory's step on it, counting my own drips. Boars wound, and I had thought that was the worst the wood did.

I lost the planks. I lost the edge post. I lost the town. I found the den.

I did not know it was the den until they stood up.

Their eyes came first, then the rest of them. Grey in the grey. Four. Five. I counted them, because I count.

They say in town that the wolves hunt the edge of the light and never cross it. There was no light, so the whole wood was edge.

I had my back to a pine. One of them came in low with its head down, as if it were ashamed of what it was about to do.

Then a light came up from the clamp side, swinging through the trees. A man with a sack on his shoulder held a lantern out in front of him at arm's length, the way you hold a lantern when you mean it.

They broke like water off a hot plate. Every one of them went back from the flame, back past where the light reached, into the dark.

Except one. It had my smell. It came round the rim of the light and ran at me, and I ran, and I counted.

One, on the thumb. Two. Three. Four. Five, and the good hand was a fist. The sixth went on the other hand, the hand with three, on the stub where a finger used to be.

On six it stopped. It was not tired. It stopped the way a dog stops when it is called in. It turned round and trotted home to the den with its tail down, and did not look back.

The man with the lantern came up beside me. I knew him by his hands. He was the smith from the railyard bench, the one who rings every spike against the anvil and throws back the ones that sound wrong. He laughed once, not unkindly. He put the lantern in my hand to carry and took the sack back up on his shoulder.

"Six," he said. "They give you six."

## The planks

I owed him my life, and I had nothing else he could use.

So I gave him what a sawyer has: my back, and my evenings.

From the next dusk I met him at the edge post as the Lamplighter lit it. She would touch the wick, the glass would go gold, and she would be off toward the manor gate before we had taken the first plank. I carried the sack and he carried the lantern. Every dusk we walked the planks out to Mother Moll's clamp in the Fenwood and back. The Company coal came up short for his forge, and the clamp's charcoal ran hotter. He said so, and I didn't know enough to argue.

He told me about the spikes. That was his talk. Not the weather, not the rent: how many he had rung that day and how many he had thrown back. Three. None. Seven, on a Tuesday, and he was cross about the seven all the way to the clamp. Under his breath he hummed the note a good spike gives. I never caught it, but he could hum it on a dark plank with his mouth shut.

His lantern was always full, and he topped it up before we turned for home. A man who listens to iron does not trust the dark to tell him anything.

I said it on the second night, and on most nights after: if it ever came to the Bell, I would stand for him.

He said nobody would ever need to.

I said it anyway.

Around us the town went on dying in its order. The Undertaker's cart went up and down. Vera went on the Saturday. On Monday the eleventh the Undertaker stood his cart at Rose's door on Pump Row in the middle of the day, where the Row could see it. Her geranium was out on the sill. By the morning they said she had gone.

I walked the planks with him that Monday night with my scarf up over my mouth.

Eleven dusks. I ran out of fingers on the fourth of them, and started again.

## The scream

Tuesday, the twelfth of December. I didn't go.

I will say why, plainly, and I will not dress it. I was afraid. There was the cart at Rose's door. There was the stone up at the churchyard, which carries a name every morning; I did not ask Agnes whose it was, because I did not want to have it. There were my two fingers, already gone to the Panic. And the dark came down at four.

I barred my door. I told myself that he had the lantern. He was the one with the lantern. It was me that needed him, not him me.

At Curfew, when the lanes go empty, one scream came out of the wood.

It was the long one. Everybody in HollowMere knows that sound. It is the sound the whole town goes to its door for and does not open it. After it, the wolves.

I lay on my back and counted it out. I did not get up.

No bell. No boots in the lane. No light in the window across. Nothing.

## One. The lantern

I went at Station Rush, before the whistle and before anyone. I took my own lantern, bought on the first of the month with rent and never lit, because I had always walked inside his.

I went because of the scream. I had heard it and lain there.

He was at the den mouth.

I will say it once and plainly, because he would have. They had been at him.

The left side of his face was gone to the bone. His throat was open. His coat was open, and what was under the coat was opened too, and there were his ribs. His right hand was gone at the wrist: the hammer hand, the hand that rang the spikes. I looked for it and did not find it.

There was fur round him in the mud, grey, in tufts. Down the near pine were claw marks, fresh and pale under the bark. There were no footprints. Wolves leave none, and the mud round the den was all paw, and the drag of bellies.

The sack lay beside him, still tied, and the charcoal inside it was dry.

His lantern lay in the mud a yard off. It was dark, and the glass was whole.

I said it to myself the way you would say it at the Bell: the lantern went out, and they took him, the way they nearly took me. A lantern goes out, and then the whole wood is edge.

I put my thumb on the first finger.

One.

## Two. The wick

I picked the lantern up. It was heavy. It was full to the brim, because he always filled it before he turned for home.

The wick was turned down, all the way down into the brass, to nothing.

A lantern that goes out by itself is empty, or it is broken. This one was neither. A hand had put it out.

I had that one on a shelf before I set the lantern down. He turned it down himself, to pass the den quiet. Dark is quieter; they say it at the Lantern. The Gamekeeper says it too, going past the den at dusk to count them. A man might do it.

He wouldn't. I knew he wouldn't. I put it on the shelf anyway.

Two.

## Three. The smear

I walked back from the den toward the planks, the way a wolf would never have brought anyone. A wolf doesn't bring you anywhere. It leaves you where you fall.

There was a smear in the mud as wide as a man's shoulders, with two grooves in it where the heels had gone, all the way from the end of the planks to the den mouth. Forty yards. I counted them.

Somebody had brought him.

He had not fallen at the den. He had fallen at the planks and been carried home: their home, where they would be.

My third shelf was the oldest thing the town says about them: mere-rot makes them bold. The fog gets into them the way it gets into us, and they do what they don't do. They drag a man. Why not?

It was thin. I could see through it.

I ran back over the planks to the square and rang the Bell. The town came out past me and down the planks at a run: half of Mill Row, the platform crew, and the Constable last of all, buttoning his coat. By the time he reached the den the smear was under all of them, boot on boot, worked back into mud.

Three. And nobody had a finger on it but me.

## The town's entry

The Gamekeeper crouched by him a long while. He rubbed the fur between his fingers and laid his hand flat on the claw marks on the pine, as if he were measuring them.

"Wolves," he said. There was no question in it.

He was right about the teeth.

The Constable wrote it in his book, standing up. Somebody behind me said it the way you would say the fog was in: blame the wolves. Nobody laughed, because the wolves do it. Everybody knew of a box that had gone down the Line for the wolves.

The Undertaker came with the cart and his boy in the shafts. He unlocked the box under the seat and opened his book on the lid, and over his arm I saw the page. At the head of the last column a word had a line ruled through it: ~~Cause~~. There was nowhere in that book to put what I knew.

I said nothing about the wick and nothing about the smear. When the Gamekeeper said wolves, I said yes. It was easy, because it was half true.

In the Constable's book it said wolves, and you can't hang a wolf.

## Four. The knife

Nobody lifts a body until the Doc has been. The Undertaker waited with his cap off.

Mallory came at noon and knelt in the mud longer than anyone kneels in mud. Then the Doc spoke to the Constable, low. I heard it because I was standing where I should not have been: one wound, under the ribs, made before. The wolves had come to a dead man.

By the time the cart was on the planks there was a paper on the notice board by the Bell, in Mallory's hand, under the word *Verified*:

*Knife. Before Curfew. The teeth after.*

The Constable's word came down, and so did my last shelf, because mere-rot doesn't carry a knife.

I stood at the board with my hands in my pockets and counted without moving my fingers.

Four. The fourth finger is the one that shakes.

## Five. The thread

Late in the afternoon, before her round, I went back out with my lantern lit and held up high in the last of the daylight. A knife means a man, and a man walks on planks.

On the third plank from the end, where the smear had begun, a nail head stood proud of the wood. Something was caught on it: a Thread of wool, a few inches long, Prussian blue.

The Company Store had that blue in October, two scarves of it. I bought one. I had not been wearing it that morning; I left it on its peg when I ran out at Station Rush.

I went home at a run and took it off the peg. It was whole. I turned it end to end twice and found no pull.

Then I put the Thread in my pocket, and not on the board. That was the first thing I hid.

"There's more than one blue in the world," I said, to the peg.

Five. The good hand was full.

## The edge post

At dusk the Lamplighter was at the pump with her ladder, starting her round. She stopped beside me and spoke low, so the pump would not hear.

"I lit the post last night, and you went by me."

I said nothing.

"Your scarf. Your walk. Into the wood, a bit after him. I thought it was the two of you, the same as always."

Last night I had been behind my barred door, on my back, counting a scream.

So it was not just more than one blue. It was my blue, my walk, near enough my height, past her post at dusk on the one night I wasn't there. Somebody had worn me into the wood, and she had seen it. Or something had. The threes walk the lanes unlit now and say they see well enough together. The fog makes footsteps where nobody walks; it might make a man where nobody walks. I didn't know, and she didn't ask me which.

I counted what the town would have if the Thread went up on the board: my scarf, her word, Mallory's hour. The gallows is at the Bell.

She lit the post and went on. She did not look back to see whether I followed.

## Six. The Bell

They met at the Bell after her round, the whole square in the cold, with the four lanterns lit and the fourth one last.

The Detective kept time at the railyard before the Constable made him what he is. He knew the smith's anvil by its sound; he told me once he could have set the yard clock by it. He stood the Constable's book open beside Mallory's paper on the board, *wolves* beside *knife*, and asked it plainly.

"Who walked the planks with him at dusk?"

Everybody knew. Eleven dusks, at the edge post, where the Lamplighter lights it.

I had said I would stand for him at the Bell, and I stood.

"Albert," I said. It was the first time I had said his name out loud in the open air, and I said it to the whole square.

"I walked with him," I said. "Not last night. Last night he went alone, and I heard it. It was the wolves."

The square was quiet.

"A wolf gives you six seconds," I said. "Then it goes home. I've been run. I counted. Last night they didn't go home."

I said it well. I believed it while I was saying it. The Thread was in my pocket, against the hand with three.

The Lamplighter looked at me across the square and said nothing.

Mallory's paper stayed on the board. Nobody took it down, and nobody read it again.

The vote went the way the town had wanted it to go before it started: wolves. The town has spoken.

No suspect. No Tribunal. No rope. No blue.

## Station Rush

In the morning I did not go to the Company Store to ask who had bought the other blue. I knew the Clerk would have it in a book. I didn't go.

I fed my scarf to the stove. Wool stinks when it burns, and it burns slowly. I kept the Thread.

The morning train came in with a private car behind it and dogs, and down on the boards there was a bigger death than ours. The crowd on the platform turned all at once, like a field in a wind, and the word went round the square. By noon nobody in HollowMere was asking about the smith.

At the pump a child, one of the Row's, small, with a pail, looked up at me.

"What took the smith?"

"Wolves," I said, before she had finished asking.

I had it ready. I will have it ready for the next one.

I counted it out for her on my fingers, the way he did for me: five on the one hand, and the sixth on the stub.

"They give you six," I said.
