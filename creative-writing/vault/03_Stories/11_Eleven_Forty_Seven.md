# Eleven Forty-Seven

In the hall of Vane Manor it was eleven forty-seven, and it always had been.

## I

*Front door, barred. Hall, dark. Clock, as ever.*

Horace wrote the line before he walked it. He had kept the day-book that way for twenty-two years: write the round down, then go and make it true. He saw no reason for a will to change that.

The last mourner had gone at half past nine, with her wake loaf wrapped in her shawl. Horace held the door for her and bowed, and she did not look at him. Then he barred it. He left the day-book open on the hall table, as he always did, so the ink could dry while he walked.

He carried the lamp in his left hand and the keyring in his right. He counted the keys the way he did in the dark, by their teeth against his thumb. Cellar. Pantry. Linen. Wine. Safe. Study. Thirty-one keys, and he knew each one by its bite.

The hall looked the way a hall looks after a crowd. Chairs out of line. Crumbs on the runner. A cold smell of wet wool.

The clock stood at the foot of the stair. Eleven forty-seven. It had said so on the day he came to be interviewed, when he was a young man with his hair still black, and nobody had ever told him why. On Sundays he drew the weight up its chain, always stopping two links short of the top so the chain would not strain. The clock never ran. Nobody had ever told him to stop.

He had let himself think about the will once, at the reading. *Whoever remains when the last lantern goes out.* He had looked down at his own lamp, and then he was ashamed, and he put the thought away like a spoon in a drawer.

Then the clock ticked once.

One tick, from a clock that had none left.

## II

*Clock. Cold in the brass.*

"Cold in the brass," he said aloud, because a thing said aloud in an empty house is half true already.

Nobody had lit a fire since the reading. Brass shrinks in the cold, and old wood complains. And he was tired, and a tired ear will hear what it is waiting for.

He found the clock key by its teeth and opened the case to prove it to himself. He put his palm flat on the pendulum. It hung still and cold, and it had been still for as long as he had served here.

Good.

Then his knuckles brushed the chain.

The weight had been drawn all the way to the top, not two links short. Someone had drawn it tonight, and not the way he did it.

The chain was wet. He brought his fingers to the lamp and smelled them: reed, rot and standing water. The mere.

Cold does not wind a clock.

Somebody had wound it tonight with mere-wet hands. Somebody with the fen on them.

## III

*Safe, locked.*

So it was a mourner, then. He knew how it happened. They had come in from the fen for the reading: fishers whom the fog kept off the boats, tinners whose mine was shut and whose hands were not. Each of them hoped to hear their own name read out and did not. One of them had stayed behind when the rest went out.

He did not blame them. He only wanted to know where they were.

There was a stranger in the house, then. And a stranger would go to the safe.

He crossed the hall with the lamp held low. The safe stood in its alcove beside the clock, and it was locked. He tried the handle twice. Only one key opened it, and Vane had put it on his ring the year the house was finished. It had never come off.

He opened the safe anyway, to be sure.

The will lay inside, just where it had lain before the reading. It was folded in thirds, the way he folded letters for the post, with the crease pressed down hard under a thumbnail. At four o'clock that afternoon he had watched the Company clerk slide that will into a satchel and walk it down to the Bank.

No draught. No mourner. No key but his.

Only his key opens it.

## IV

*Boots, blacked.*

Grief, then, and habit.

Every night since the wire came, he had laid out the master's slippers by the bed: left, then right, toes to the wall. A man who could do that could walk to a safe in his sleep. He could fetch back a paper he had seen leave, fold it his own way, and put it to bed, all without waking.

His own hands, then. Nobody else's.

He looked down at his boots to prove it. He had blacked them at six. They were dry, black and clean.

Then he lowered the lamp.

There were bootprints on the carpet, in fen mud still wet enough to shine. They came from the barred front door and went to the clock, then to the safe, right past where he stood, and on to the stair.

He set his own foot beside one of them. It did not fit. He went through the mourners: the Widow's small boot, the clerk's town shoes, the tinners in their hobnails. It was none of theirs.

The size matched no one in the house.

They went up the stair.

## V

*Stair. Study door, locked.*

He followed them up, lamp in one hand and ring in the other. He had stopped asking whose they were. He had run out of people.

The prints climbed the stair steadily and without hurry, the way a man walks up his own stairs. On the landing they turned left and stopped at the study.

He had locked the study door himself, at three o'clock before the reading, and tried it twice. It was locked now. He put his hand on the knob and it did not give.

Black crepe hung on the door.

He knew that crepe. It had hung on the front door for three nights, and he had taken it down at noon because the Widow said a will is business, and business does not come in through crepe. He had folded it and laid it in the linen press. Now it was tied across the study door in his own knot: a reef with the tail tucked under, the knot his mother taught him.

Crepe goes on the front door of a house with a death in it, where the street can see it.

This door was inside.

The house was mourning someone behind the study door. Not Vane.

## VI

*Study.*

He did not knock.

Opening doors was what he was for. He knew every door in Hollowmere: which ones stuck in wet weather, which ones a woman could lean against in the dark and weep, and which ones he must never open without being asked.

The ring was in his hand. He counted for the study key by its teeth, as he had counted all his life.

Study.

Study.

Study.

There were three. One was his. The second had barn straw caught in its bow and a pale twist of goat hair. The third was so cold it ached, and when he raised it to the lamp it smelled of the Undercroft's gas.

He had never hung either of them. He had never cut a copy of a key in his life.

The ring was no longer his count.

He stopped counting.

## VII

*11:47.*

He fitted a key without choosing which one, and it turned.

The study was as it had always been. The desk. The master's chair, with its back to the window. The curtains drawn, as he had drawn them.

He put his hand on the chair. It was warm.

The day-book lay open on the desk.

He had left it downstairs, on the hall table, to dry. He knew that as surely as he knew the teeth of the keys. Now it lay open on the desk with tonight's page written all the way down. The ink was still wet, and every word was in his hand.

*Front door, barred. Hall, dark. Clock, as ever.*
*Clock. Cold in the brass.*
*Safe, locked.*
*Boots, blacked.*
*Stair. Study door, locked.*
*Study.*

Then the lines he had not walked yet.

*Crepe, front door.*
*Key, Hale Barn.*
*Key, Undercroft.*
*Clock, wound.*
*Hall. One clock, standing.*
*11:47.*

He read it twice. It was a good round. He would have written it the same way himself. He had.

The night was already written, and it ended at 11:47.

So he walked it.

He untied the crepe from the study door and carried it down, and he tied it across the front door in a reef with the tail tucked under.

He drew back the bar and went out across the wet ground to Hale Barn, where the wolf pelt hung on the door. The goat watched him, as goats watch everyone, and he hung the key with the straw in its bow where it would be found.

He crossed the square. Every window was dark. He went down the Well Ladder rung by rung, into the cold and the smell of gas, and laid the third key in the Undercroft where it would be found.

He came back in by the front door. He had barred it. He did not wonder about that.

His boots were not dry any more.

In the hall he opened the case and took the chain in his wet hands and drew the weight all the way to the top.

The hands did not move.

He did not put out the lamp. He did not ask himself why.

In the hall of Vane Manor it was eleven forty-seven, and it always had been.
