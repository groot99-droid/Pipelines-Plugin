# The Dead Man's Lamp

## 1.

There's a lamp burning down there nobody fills.

The Collier said it on Tuesday, the twenty-eighth of October, with his back to the clamp and his arms black to the elbow. He said it the way a man tells you it will rain.

Six was carrying stone. The Workings crew had been lent to the kilns for the week, and the stone was for the clamp, and the Collier did not look up when the detail officer came down the skid road with a clipboard under his arm.

"Six," the officer said.

Six put the stone down.

The officer read from the top half of a form. *Form 9-D. Detail: Recovery, Deep Level. Item: one crate, stencilled VANE TIN CO. Light issue: one charge. Return: by whistle.* He held the clipboard so the bottom half folded under his thumb.

"Yes," Six said.

In Grounds 99 you said yes or you said nothing, and nothing was written down as no.

They gave him a rope and a camp lamp, a carbide cap lamp gone green at the seams. He shook it by his ear. The charge was light. Half, maybe. He thought: they ration everything. He thought: it's a crate, not a shift.

The Collier spoke as he passed, still not looking up.

"Down the winze?"

"Down the winze."

"Lamp's still burning down there, then. Nobody fills it."

"Whose lamp?"

"Him off the board. The German, or the Swede." The Collier spat into the ash. "Depends who tells it."

His name was Arvo Lahti. He had been twenty-six years underground in Butte before the leaflet, and the leaflet was a single sheet about the strike in 1948 that he had not written and had handed out at a mine gate, to men he knew, in the rain. His classification said *enemy of the state*. He had always thought the state would be bigger than that.

He knew lamps. That was the use of him.

The tram rails took him up through the adit into the ore chamber, past the last shift's tally board. Names went on that board in chalk when men went down and came off when they came up. One at the bottom had been rubbed so many times it had gone to ghosts, a smear the shape of a name the way a cloud is the shape of a horse. He did not try to read it. Nobody on the crew tried.

At the winze gallery the water stood black round the ladder shaft. Two guards in gas masks pried the boards up with a bar and stacked them against the wall.

Six took off his helmet to settle the liner. Inside it, in pencil, was *Aili*. He wrote it there every morning because pencil fades by night, and because the camp used numbers, and he would not let a number be the only thing written on him. He put the helmet back on.

He went over the edge and counted the rungs the way his father had, in Finnish, under his breath. *Yksi. Kaksi. Kolme.*

Above him the boards stayed on the stack, against the wall. Not on the hole. He noticed that, and was glad of it.

He was below the boards with one charge.

## 2.

The winze landing was a shelf of rock over water. The flooded stope opened beyond it, wide and black, crossed by two plank walkways on trestles. His lamp reached a few yards and gave up.

Across the water, low in the southwest corner, a light was burning.

Yellow. Small. Steady.

Six stood with his hand on the ladder and knew who it was.

No. 3 had slept in the bunk above his. Nineteen, classed *other*, a boy who wore a red bootlace round his wrist, double-knotted, cut from his sister's skates. On his first night he had told Six about the skates: a pond at home, his sister faster than him every winter and never letting him forget it. He went on this same detail on Saturday. On Sunday his billet came off the crew's rack, and the roll said *not recovered* against his slot.

Six had thought then: *recovered* means a body. *Not recovered* means they did not look.

And here was a light. The boy had got down, found the refuge, found oil, and lived on something, and the camp had written him off because writing was cheaper than coming down.

It went through Six like heat off a stove. He had not been glad of anything since intake.

A ledge ran round the west wall to the refuge, clear of the water. He took it fast, one hand on the rock, expecting a living man.

"Three!" he called.

He had never asked the boy his name.

## 3.

The refuge was a room cut square into the rock: timber frame, plank door hanging open, a table, a bunk, a hook on the wall with nothing on it.

Nobody.

Dust lay on the table and the bunk thick and unbroken, like felt. Rat tracks stitched across it. No boots. No boy. No mark where a boy had sat.

The lamp stood in the middle of the table.

It was not a camp lamp. It was brass, a flame safety lamp with a glass and a gauze chimney, the kind men carried before carbide, the kind his father had carried. Older than the camp. Older than the program, whatever the program was. Its flame burned small and yellow and did not move.

So it was not the boy. The boy was gone. Six took that and set it down where he would not have to look at it.

The lamp, then. Under the old tin there was coal; every man on the crew knew that. Coal meant gas. A seep through a crack in the floor, gas creeping into the gauze, and a flame could feed on it for a long time if the gas kept coming. He had heard coal men in the bunkhouse tell it. It happened.

He shelved the lamp as a gas seep, the way a miner shelves anything he has a word for.

"It happens," he said, to nobody.

## 4.

Then he heard what he had just said, and he went cold.

Gas. He had walked into a gas room with an open carbide flame on his head.

He backed out, set the cap lamp on the ledge outside, and came in again by the light of the brass lamp alone, slowly. If it was gas, there was a test, and the old men had taught it to him before he could shave: turn the flame down to a bead and look for the cap. Gas sits on a low flame like a blue hood. The more gas, the taller the hood.

He found the pricker on its wire and worked the wick down.

A bead of clean yellow. No hood. No blue. Nothing.

No gas.

He picked the lamp up. It was heavy, and the weight moved in it. Oil. He tilted it and heard it slosh in the pot, a third full, maybe. The wick was trimmed square. The glass had been wiped.

A lamp like this burned a shift on a full pot, two if you were mean with the wick. Whoever had filled it had filled it since Sunday. Since yesterday, maybe.

He set it down exactly where it had been, on the square of clean wood where the dust had never settled.

Somebody filled the lamp. He decided not to think about who.

He turned his back on it.

## 5.

The job was the only thing down here with a shape, so he did the job.

The crate was in the old workings, east, across the stope. He took his cap lamp off the ledge and went out on the nearer walkway. The planks were dry and gritty with old dust, and his boots left prints in it. The water below lay flat and black. Off to the west the drowned adit opened in the rock, a dark mouth with nothing in it.

He did not look at the water. He counted the planks instead. *Neljä. Viisi. Kuusi.*

On the far side he stopped and turned, the way you check the road home, with the walkway behind him.

The planks were wet.

Not splashed. Wet on top, in marks. Two and two, all the way back, the shape of hands and the shape of knees, as if something had come up out of the stope behind him and crawled along the boards in his own tracks. The hands were wrong. Too long in the finger. Too narrow in the palm. A man's hand, drawn out.

The water lay flat.

Then, somewhere out on it, a breath let go. A long one, the sound a man makes coming up after too long under.

No current. No wind. No drip, now that he listened.

Nothing.

Drip, he told himself. Rats. Rats swim; a wet rat leaves a mark, and water spreads a mark long. Big rats. He did not believe in rats that big, but he believed in them as hard as he could.

He stopped looking behind him.

## 6.

The crate stood among the ore pillars, where the order said it would be. VANE TIN CO, in black stencil, the letters worn.

The wood under the letters was new.

Pale. Sawn this year. Raw at the corners. The lid was not even nailed.

He lifted it.

Three stopwatches, in felt.

A clipboard. On it, a column of numbers, and against each number a time. Some were crossed through. Against *3*: *4 min 10 s.* Under that, a ruled line.

A carbon of Form 9-D, the same form the officer had read to him at the kilns. The top half said what the officer had said. The letterhead above it was blacked out with a solid bar, and under the bar, in type: ANOMALY UNKNOWN. The number in the box was *6*, typed. He had never seen the bottom half. He saw it now.

*Duration (held): ________.*

*Outcome: Recovered / Not recovered.*

*Observer: ________.*

And in the corner of the crate, a square can of lamp oil, stencilled REFUGE LAMP — MAINTAIN.

He stood with the lid in one hand.

Nobody sent a man down a boarded winze for a crate they could have winched. Nobody put new wood under an old name for nothing. The crate was never the cargo. The detail is the test, he thought. The cargo was the man who came to fetch it.

He put the lid down. He picked up the can. He did not know why. His hands wanted something to hold.

Neither box on the form was ticked yet.

## 7.

He ran.

Back over the walkway, on the wet marks, not looking down. Along the ledge. Up the ladder, counting, losing the count, starting again. *Yksi, kaksi, kolme, neljä.* The can banged against the rungs.

His helmet hit wood.

The boards were back over the winze.

He pushed. They did not give. He found the ends of nails with his fingers where they had come through from above. He had not heard the hammer. He had been counting.

"Six!" he shouted. "Six, on detail!"

His number. Not his name.

Nothing came back. The order said *by whistle*. It did not say before it. The winze was shut above him until the whistle, and the whistle was hours off, and whatever the form meant by *duration*, it was not going to be decided up there.

On his head, the carbide flame shrank to a blue tongue. Half a charge. He had known on the skid road. It sputtered once and went out.

Black.

No light. No sound above. No sound below, except the water.

Then, far down and across the stope, small and yellow and steady, the refuge lamp.

The only light on the Deep Level.

He climbed down to it. He went along the ledge with his hand on the rock and his eyes on the bead of yellow, the can held against his chest, and he went into the refuge and sat on the bunk, in the dust, with the can between his boots.

He sat in the dead man's light because it was the only light there was.

## 8.

He did not know how long. The flame did not tell time, and he had no other clock.

The sound of the water changed. It rose and fell in the sump, slow, like breathing. Long in. A long hold. Out.

It came up the stope and lay along the refuge sill, black and level, and stayed there.

The cold came with it. Not mine cold. A cold that got into the teeth.

Then they came.

They came up out of the water at the sill, one, then another, then more, and sat at the edge of the light the way men crouch round the bunkhouse stove before the morning whistle. Close enough for the warmth. No closer.

Men. They had been men.

Long. That was the first thing. Too long in the arm and the shin, as if the cold had stretched them and they had set that way. Pale as tallow. The skin drawn so tight over them that he could count them by their bones: ribs standing out like the slats of a bunk under wet sheeting, hip bones like handles, the cords of the neck. Lips gone thin and back from the teeth. Ears and fingertips black with frost. Nails gone. On one, in the crook of the arm, a stub of rubber tube still taped to the skin, the tape gone grey.

They did not breathe. He watched the nearest one's chest for a long time, and it did not move. Then it heaved, once, all the air going out of it at once, and was still again.

They did not cross into the light.

He had thought, if he had thought anything, that the lamp kept them off.

It did not keep them off. They had come straight to it, and they sat at its edge, and they watched it. Not him. The lamp.

They kept things. That was the worst of it. One rubbed a thumb along two long fingers, over and over, rolling a cigarette that was not there. One tapped the plank of the sill, a count, stopping and starting again the way a man loses count on a ladder. One sat with his head bowed and his long hands folded, the way a man sits in chapel when he has stopped believing and still goes.

One of them had a red bootlace round its wrist, double-knotted.

The wrist was not right any more. Thin as a broom handle, the bone white through the skin, bent back further than a wrist goes and resting that way, as if it did not hurt now. The lace had sunk into it. With his other hand he turned it, slowly, round and round, the way the boy used to turn it in the bunk above Six when he could not sleep.

Six thought about a pond, somewhere, and a girl faster than her brother every winter, and whether anyone had told her what he was now.

The flame was lower. He leaned over and looked into the pot. Nearly dry.

They were waiting the way he had waited at the top of the ladder, for something above them to open. Every number before him had come down with a carbide lamp and a can from the crate, and ended here, on this bunk, in this light, and filled the lamp last thing. The dead man's first. Then Three's. Then theirs. Nobody filled it, the camp said. Everybody filled it. The lamp was theirs to keep lit. It was the only light any of them had.

He could let it go out.

Then there would be nothing down here for Eight but the dark and the water breathing, and these men sitting in it.

He unscrewed the pot and filled it from the can, slowly, not spilling, the way his father had taught him. He trimmed the wick square. The flame stood up. Along the sill, something in them eased. He saw it go through them like heat.

He took off his helmet and turned it over and read the liner once. *Aili.* The pencil was faint. It was evening, somewhere.

He set the helmet on the table beside the lamp, name-side up, for whoever reads names.

At the sill the one with the bootlace moved over to make room.

The water was colder than anything he had touched. Shins. Knees. His belt. It took his breath before he could give it, and he stood with his chest locked and his hands open, and then he made himself breathe once more, deep, because the form had a box for it and somebody up there was holding a watch.

He went under.

Black. Cold in the eyes. Cold in the ears.

He counted. *Yksi. Kaksi. Kolme.* His chest went tight, then tighter, then it was all he was. Hands came round his arms in the dark, long and thin and not unkind, the way men hold a man in a cold river so he does not go alone. *Kymmenen.* He lost the count. He started again. He thought of Aili's handwriting, which was better than his. His chest pulled at him to open.

He held.

He held.

He let go.

The lamp burned, full, for the next number.

Whether those hands felt like God's eternal presence, or sleeping with the devil, we'll never hear it from those drowned lips.

That afternoon, at the kilns, somebody told Eight.
