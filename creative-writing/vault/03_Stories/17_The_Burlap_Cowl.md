# The Burlap Cowl

*Port-au-Levain, spring 1790.*

## 1. Salt

Once a year the Abbot came down the dish-pit stair and said our names.

Nobody else came down. Not the Aboyeur, not the Council's clerks, not the Commons' committee, who had held the Square since the ferry brought the news and who said, now, that all of us were citizens. Citizens of the pit. We scrubbed their plates the same as we had scrubbed the Gallery's.

I was born down there. My mother was sent to the pit for a crumb, in a year before I can remember, and a child born in the pit is a Plongeur by birth. I have no platter in the Hall. A soul without a platter has no name in law. I had a name anyway. The Abbot kept it for me.

The eve of the Blessing of the Flour. Upstairs the Aboyeur carried the dusk loaf down the Hall and set it on the empty platter nearest the Hearth, for a name under seal. We heard his feet through the ceiling. The night began.

We knelt. That is the custom: on the eve you kneel where you work. I knelt where I always knelt, on the old seal of Le Champignon that was ground into the floor before anyone's grandmother. The office of the first heresy. My knees know it better than my hands know my face.

First bell. Nothing.

Second bell. The burlap came down the stair.

It was the cowl. The patch at the knee, the rope belt, the wooden thumb-bowl. It went along the row. It did not stop at the old man beside me and say his name. It did not stop at me. It put its thumb to each brow the way the Abbot did, quick, like a seal on wax, and moved on.

His thumb smelled of hearth-ash. Always. Twenty blessings in my life and every one smelled of the Hearth.

This one smelled of salt.

When it lifted, my brow was cold where it had been. I put my fingers up. They came away green.

I bowed into it anyway. I had waited a year.

I did not wipe it off.

## 2. Damp

I lay on my pallet and explained it to myself.

The Starter had turned. That was all. The Abbot carried the Apostolic Starter in that little bowl from the Abbey to the pit, and the pit is wet, and the stair is wetter, and anything alive goes strange in the wet. Green is a colour bread goes. Everyone in a kitchen knows it.

The Abbot was old. Old men forget names. He had said ours for forty years; one year he could be tired.

And the salt. The Commons had their watch on the Square every night now, red crust on their sleeves, lanterns at every lane. An old man in burlap who would not bless their fire would not walk past them for pleasure. He would have come the long way. By the Salt-Flats. Salt on the hem, salt on the hands.

There. Three things, three reasons.

I did not wash my face. The old man beside me did not wash his.

The damp, I decided. Damp gets into everything down here.

## 3. The bowl

At dawn the flour carts stood at the foot of the Windmill Cliffs, the way they stand every year on the Blessing, the sacks open at the neck. The Abbot blesses the flour of the first grinding, and the island eats clean for a year. That is what the Abbey is for.

A Plongeur does not leave the pit at the Blessing. A Plongeur does not leave the pit. I left it.

I came up the back stair with a tub of plates as my reason and put the tub down at the head of the lane.

The cowl walked past the carts. It did not look at them. It did not lift a hand. The carters stood with their caps off, and it went by them like a man passing a stall he does not owe.

It went to Gallows Square.

The Hall's great south doors stood open onto the Square, as they had since the autumn, so the Commons could see the Hearth from the stage and the Hearth could see them. The committee sat at the long table where the Gallery used to take its supper. Red crust. Ink. A seal on a ribbon.

The cowl climbed the stage. It blessed the committee one by one, thumb to brow. Then it turned to the open doors and lifted both hands to the Hearth itself.

The Square made a sound I had never heard it make. It was not a cheer. It was what a dog does when it is fed after a long time.

Then the cowl dipped its thumb in the wooden bowl for the next brow, and held the bowl high, and I was close enough to see inside.

Grey-gold. The Apostolic Starter, the colour it always was. Dry at the rim. Clean.

The green was not in the bowl. It was on the thumb. It had always been on the thumb.

Not the damp, then. Not the Starter. Not the stair.

For a whole year the Abbot had stood in that Square and refused. The Gallery asked him to bless their Trial by Fire, and he would not. The Commons asked him to bless theirs, and he would not. He blessed bread and he blessed us. That was all he would bless.

He never blessed a fire. Not theirs, and not the Gallery's.

## 4. The platter

With the Hearth blessed, the committee read an ordinance.

Gold leaf on bread was forbidden in a hungry week. Seal of the Commons, ribbon of red. A woman of the Gallery had gilded the crust of her daughter's name-day loaf, three days into a hungry week. A crumb's worth of gold. They had the crust in a dish on the table to show it.

Clémence d'Aubrac.

I knew her before I knew her face. I had scrubbed her plates for eleven years. A child's plate, small, with a gold rim, and it always came down with the crust left on it. The child never ate the crust. Somebody upstairs never made her.

They brought her up onto the stage. She was not frightened the way the poor were frightened when the Gallery did this. She was offended. She looked at the long table as if the cloth were wrong.

She asked for her gloves. Her hands were cold, she said.

It was not permitted.

The Commons used the Gallery's ceremony because it was the only ceremony there was. The cloches lifted along the table. The music, the same three fiddles. The bell. Her loaf was carried in through the south doors by the Aboyeur, who called her name once as it passed, and the Hearth received it.

Then the Hearth received her.

It was the first fire on the island anyone had ever blessed.

Afterward the committee sat down to the same supper the Gallery used to eat after, on the same silver, carried out by the same hands. Ours. I have scrubbed that silver. I scrubbed it that night.

But before the supper, a man of the committee stood and said the blessing had made them a church as well as a council, and a church must count its souls. The dish pit was registered. All of it. In thanks.

They gave me a platter. Tin, not silver, at the far end of the Hall where the steam does not reach. A loaf on it with a seal pressed in. The Aboyeur walked the row and called each new loaf by its owner's name, the way he calls the Gallery's.

He called mine. In the Hall. Out loud. The first time anyone had said it above the pit.

I will not write it. It sounded like the Abbot's.

## 5. Crumb

Dawn. The Great Proofing Bell struck six, and everyone with a platter cracked the loaf sitting on it.

I had never cracked a loaf of my own. I had cleaned up after ten thousand.

I broke it with my thumbs, the way I had seen them do it from the stair.

The crumb was threaded green. Fine lines, like veins in the back of a hand.

I looked up. Along the floor of the Hall, from the foot of my platter, there were footprints. Green. Glowing in the grey light under the cloches, faint, the way a wet stone holds the moon. They went from my platter down the Hall and out to the threshold of the pit stair, and on the threshold there was a round green mark, like a seal stamped into the stone.

I knew the feet. Wide in the heel, the left turned in. I had knelt beside those feet for twenty years.

The old man who scrubbed beside me. Thirty years in the pit. The Abbot had said his name thirty times, once a year, on his knees beside him. He walked the thresholds now, spore on his soles. The Spore-Caller. He did not look at me when I came down. He looked pleased.

They had registered us. I had thought that was a name.

It was a mark. A line in a book for a church that counts its souls.

They had not given me a name. They had given me a number.

## 6. Kelp

I followed the prints.

Not the ones to the pit. There were others, older and fainter, that came in at the foot of my platter from the other side. They went out by the side door of the Hall, down the Wharf steps, and along the edge of the Salt-Flats. They started at my platter. Whatever had marked my bread had come from the Flats to do it.

Low tide. The Flats go grey and flat and wet, and the kelp lies in heaps like wash.

The prints stopped at one heap.

I pulled the kelp off with my hands. I am used to my hands in cold wet things.

The Abbot.

In his undershirt. No burlap. Bare feet, white, the soles cracked the way they were always cracked. His hose was patched at the knee, a square of brown, the same patch as the cowl's, because he wore through both in the same place. Kneeling. In our pit. Forty years.

His belt was empty where the bowl hung.

His face was turned to the side. His mouth a little open, as if he had been about to say a name and had been stopped on the first letter. There was sand in his eyebrows. His thumb was grey with ash.

"Dom Ambroise," I said. Once. Nobody calls an Abbot by his name. I had heard it from the old man, years ago, the way you hear the name of a saint.

Every child in the pit knows the saying. What a dead man wears will walk until someone tells the Bailiff where he lies. I had always thought it was about thieves selling the clothes off the drowned.

It was not about thieves.

The cowl was walking because he was lying here. He was lying here because nobody had told. And I was the only one who knew where.

He had been here since before he blessed me.

## 7. Sails

I put the kelp back over him. I don't know why. Then I climbed.

The Wharf steps, the lane, the corner of the Square. The Bailiff kept his iron staff by the committee's door now, the Commons having taken the Guild's law along with the Gallery's stage. I could see the staff from the corner. Twenty steps.

Then the sails stopped.

Everyone on the island feels it. The Windmill Cliffs are behind everything, turning, and you stop seeing them, the way you stop hearing your own heart. Then they stop, and everyone in the Square looked up at once, the way a room looks at a door.

The Groat-Master. A miller's apprentice I had seen once at the flour carts. He had been blessed at the Square at dawn. He was up there now with the brake on, and the grinding stopped, and the island did what it does when the sails stop. It waited.

By noon there was a new ordinance on the bulletin board. Seal and ribbon. Until the mill turns, flour to the blessed only.

I read it standing at the board. I worked it out with my fingers, like plates.

Tell the Bailiff, and the body is found. Find the body, and the cowl comes off. Take the cowl off, and every blessing it gave is void. The committee's. The Hearth's. Ours. The pit's platters are struck. The pit's flour stops.

The old man. The two girls who came down last winter. The boy with the bad hand. The whole pit. Hungry, in a week with no flour, with no platter, with no name.

I turned round on the steps of the Square.

I went back down. I told myself the stair was too steep.

## 8. Bread

Tomorrow.

The tide would be low again tomorrow. The mill would turn tomorrow, or the day after, and there would be bread, and then I would tell. It was only a day. He had already waited a night and a day under the kelp. One more was not a sin. It was arithmetic.

That night there was a loaf on my platter. Mine. A seal in it, cut on the top with a cross, the way the Abbey marks its bread. The first bread in my life that nobody had eaten from first.

I did not crack it at the Hearth. I took it down to the pit and ate it on the Champignon seal, on my knees, in the dark.

There was green in it. I ate around it. Then I ate it.

It was good bread. That is the worst thing I know.

## 9. Gone

Next low tide I went back to the Salt-Flats. The tomorrow I had promised myself.

The heap of kelp was there. I lifted it.

Sand. A dent in the sand the shape of a man, filling slowly with water from underneath. Nothing else.

No drag marks. Not one. No heel lines, no furrow, no broken kelp. As if he had stood up and walked, or as if something had carried him that did not touch the ground.

I stood there until the water came over my feet.

Now there was nothing to tell. I could say to the Bailiff, *I saw him*. I could say, *under the kelp, by the third post*. The Bailiff would go and lift the kelp and find sand. A Plongeur's word against an empty beach. Tomorrow was spent. I had spent it on bread.

That night the cowl came down the dish-pit stair again.

Not the eve. Not a feast. An ordinary night with the dusk loaf already set upstairs. It came along the row and the others knelt, so I knelt.

It stopped at me.

The thumb. Salt. Cold. It pressed, and held, longer than the first time.

And it said my name.

Not the Aboyeur's way, loud, for the Hall. The Abbot's way. Low, the first letter soft, as if the name were something that could be bruised.

Nobody alive should have had it like that. The Aboyeur had it from a ledger. The committee had it from the register. The way it was said, only one man had it, and that man had said it down here once a year and nowhere else.

He had given it away. Before he died. Somebody asked him who he blessed, and he told them. Maybe he thought it would save us. Maybe he thought it would save him.

He had told it who I was.

## 10. Key

The second blessing took.

I don't know how else to say it. The first was a smear on my brow. The second went in. I knelt there with the cowl's thumb on my face and I knew where he was. Not by the prints. Not by sense. I knew the way you know where your own hand is in the dark.

The cowl put something in my palm. Iron. Cold. A key with the cold pantry's mark on the bow.

The cold pantry is the coolest room in the Sacred Kitchen, and the only one with a lock on the outside.

He was in there. On the stone shelf where the Garde Manger keeps what is kept. Somebody had laid him straight. Somebody with no feet, or feet that leave nothing.

I was the somebody now. I would move him when he needed moving. I would leave no trail; I already knew I would leave no trail, the way I knew I would breathe. The Cellar-Ghoul, they call it. The one who keeps the body.

I sat with him all night. When I went out for water I locked him in behind me, from the outside, the way that door is made, and the key went back on its string round my neck.

The green had come up on him too. On the backs of his hands, at his temples, in the cracks of his soles. I did not wipe it off him either. It was his, now. It was ours.

His hose had ridden up. I pulled it down over his knees.

I kept his knees covered. He had knelt for us enough.

## 11. Ink

The Inquisitor came down the stair in the afternoon.

Flour-white sleeves, a Guild man, young. The kind they set to learning. He had the Magnesium Powder Kit on a strap and white dust to his elbows, and he came along the row looking at feet.

He had been on the Salt-Flats. He had dusted the sand round the third post, and the powder had lifted prints out of the old green. Bare feet. A Plongeur's feet, because nobody else on the island goes barefoot on the Flats. Coming and going.

He stopped in front of me. He looked at my feet. Then my brow.

"Where is the Abbot?" he said.

"Upstairs," I said. "Blessing."

"You were on the Flats."

"I go for kelp. For the scouring."

He looked at me a long time. He had nothing in his kit for a body that was not where the prints led. He had sand and a dent in it.

He opened his ledger and wrote it down. *Plongeur. States: Abbot upstairs, blessing.* He blotted it. He climbed back up because he could not prove otherwise, and the committee would not thank a Guild man who said their blessings were void.

Now it was in a book. In ink. In the Guild's hand, with my mark of the pit beside it.

He wrote down what I said. That made it true enough to hold.

## 12. Fifteen

Five thresholds were marked by then. The Windmill door. The Tavern. The Counting-House. The Apothecary. The south doors of the Hall, last, a green ring on the old stone where the Commons' stage met the Gallery's floor. The pit stair was marked too, but it did not count. The pit is not a building. It is a hole under one.

On the last night they went down.

They passed me in the kitchen on the way to the sub-basement door. The Spore-Caller first, the old man, his feet glowing. The Groat-Master, flour still in his hair from the brake. The Arch-Fermenter, a dockhand, in a leather apron his wife had mended twice at the same corner. I saw the two rows of stitches. Small. Even. A careful woman. Then the others, whose names I knew from their plates, with their eyes open and nothing behind them.

Six at the altar, and the Arch-Fermenter to lead them. Ninety-one steps down. The cowl went last, and did not look at me, because it did not need to.

I did not go down. I had my door.

I sat on the floor of the kitchen with my back against the cold pantry, the key on its string, and his knees covered on the other side of the wood.

The chant came up. Through the ninety-one steps, through the stone, through the floor and into my legs, where the Champignon seal has lived for twenty years. Not loud. Steady, like a mill.

I have always counted plates. Gold-leaf plates in their own tub. Tin plates. Children's plates with the crust left on.

I counted the chant instead. One. Two. Three. As long as a held breath. Eleven. Twelve.

I do not know what it did to the Mother. I do not know what is in her glass now. That is not mine to tell. I was at my door.

Fourteen. Fifteen.

They will have my office in their book, one day. The Cellar-Ghoul. The Council always writes the office down. It does not write the name.

That is all right. The name was never theirs.

Once a year the Abbot came down the dish-pit stair and said our names.

Now the cowl comes every night, and says one.
