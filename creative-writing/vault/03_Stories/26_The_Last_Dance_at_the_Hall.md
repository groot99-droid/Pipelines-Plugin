# The Last Dance at the Hall

## The Notice

The notice went up at noon on Lady Day, in red.

Isaure read it in Gallows Square on her father's arm. The fog was coming off the stones in strips. The board was the old board, with the old nails, and the hand on it was the Committee's.

> *The Ball of the Gallery, on the night of Lady Day, is retained, as anciently used. The consort to play from the gallery stair. The great south doors of the Hall to stand open on Gallows Square, so that the Square may see. The attendance of the Gallery is required, and counted.*
>
> *For the Committee of the Common Loaf.*

Under it, in the same red, smaller:

> *On the Morning Ferry: a crate, for the Committee.*

She knew the words *required, and counted*. When she was small they were printed on the Council's notice for a Fire, about the Commons, every time there was one. She had learned to read partly from that notice.

The words were turned round now. She thought that was the whole point of them.

A truce, then. They had taken the Hall, and the leaf off the rims, and the chairs. They had taken the old order of the platters and stood it on its head. Now they wanted to see the Gallery dance, and the Gallery would dance, and the Square would watch, and nobody gets hurt by being watched. It was a way of saying the thing was over.

The crate would be type for the press, or chairs, or one of the Committee's new bells. They would not have the money for anything else.

Her father read it twice. He had gout in both feet and leaned on her more than he meant to.

"Anciently used," he said, and laughed, and did not stop.

## I to III

At dusk the Aboyeur carried a loaf down the Hall and set it on the empty platter nearest the Hearth, for a name under seal. He called no name. The ball was reckoned from it, as every night is.

The Gallery came in at the cold end.

That was where the gilded platters stood now, by the great south doors, a row of silver with the rims scraped bare. The leaf had come off in the dish pit and gone to the Counting-House by weight. Each platter still had its loaf on it under its cloche, for the morning. The Gallery stood among them in its good clothes. There were no chairs. Her father found the wall and put his back to it.

She counted the candles. She always did. On fog nights as a child she had counted them to know how long the light would last, and she had never stopped.

Sixty-one.

By the door a clerk had chalked a slate: *Candles, 61.* Issued by count. Enough for one night and not a stub more.

The same clerk gave out the cards.

They were the old cards, cream, with a pencil on a silk cord. The pencils had been cut off. The cords hung down with nothing on them.

The cards were already full.

Every dance, I to XII, and against every number a partner, in red. Her first three were men she had known all her life, written in a hand that did not know them. Across the foot of the card:

*Not to be amended.*

All right, she thought. A truce does not write your partners in for you. So this was not a truce.

It was humiliation, though, not harm. For two hundred years the Gallery had put the Commons in a rope pen beyond the doors. Now the Commons put the Gallery on a card. A card was paper. It had never hurt anyone.

So she would dance it well.

If they wanted to watch the Gallery, she would show them what they had been looking at all those years. She stood up straight. The consort struck up on the gallery stair, hautboys and a viol, the same consort that had always played, and the set formed.

The first went cleanly. The second. The third.

The south doors were open, and the Square stood beyond them in the fog. It did not make a sound. She could see faces at the edge of the light, and the shoulders of the ones behind, and the dark past them that was the rest of the island.

At the end of the third she turned and curtsied to the doors. The old Order had the accused bow to the gallery, once, lower than to the Hearth.

Nobody told her to.

## IV

*IV. A minuet. Aymeric de Lanvaux, in absence.*

Her brother.

He had taken the ferry in the autumn of '89, a week after the news came, without telling her, because she was thirteen and would have told their father. The Committee judged the platter, since it could not judge the man. His Wake Loaf went to the Hearth in his place, and the Aboyeur called his name as it passed. She had been in the Square for it. Required, and counted.

The set formed for the minuet, and it had holes in it.

Six. Wherever a card named someone who had taken the ferry, or someone the Hearth had already had, there was a space on the floor the width of a person. The dancer opposite the space danced to it.

She danced to hers.

She bowed to nothing and rose. At the turn she gave her hand to the air at the height of his hand. She went down the set beside nobody and kept the distance a partner keeps.

The Square laughed.

It was not cruel laughter, or not only. It sounded surprised, like people who have never seen a thing and find it funny. Then, at the end, it applauded. Applause had always belonged between courses, at the Gallery's suppers with the doors open, and the Square had been at enough of those to learn when to clap.

She could not dance well. Nobody can dance a figure well with a hole in it. Grace needs someone to be graceful toward. The Square could see that, and that was what it had come to see.

Still, she thought. It was mockery of the dead. Aymeric was wherever the ferry had taken him. In law he was ash already, and the dead cannot be hurt. The cards were about the past. They were the Committee's way of reading out the Gallery's debts.

She put her hand flat on her bodice. Under the silk was her first card, from Lady Day of '89, with one dance filled in, in his hand, because nobody else had asked her and he would not let her stand out.

She found she had kept time for two.

## V to VI

Thibault Lormeau was not in absence.

He was her partner for the sixth. He was older than her father had been at her birth, unhurried, and slightly behind the beat in a way that made the beat seem wrong. He smelled of the bread he never finished. Everyone knew he did not eat his crusts. He kept them for the gulls.

Between figures he showed her his card. Then he took hers, gently, and turned it over to the last line.

They were the same. He had checked others. Every card in the Gallery had the same twelfth line.

*XII. The last dance. Beside the Hearth. At the ferry.*

No partner.

She looked past him to the Hearth.

Two Guild carpenters were kneeling on the flags beside it. One had chalk. The other had the brass finger-breadth from the Counting-House, the High Chancellor's measure, the one the Committee kept. They were marking out a square.

So the cards were not about the past. The last dance was for the living. The crate was not type or chairs. It was for that square.

Everyone on the rock knew what had been done in Paris in January. The ferry had brought that too.

She found the one shelf left.

It would be for the great houses. For the platters that had stood nearest the Hearth for four hundred years, and the houses of the first rank, four finger-breadths of rim. The Committee would want those first, and loudly. Not a cadet line. Not two finger-breadths, scraped.

The carpenters measured the square twice, and wrote the number down.

## Supper

At midnight the supper went out.

It came down from the gallery the way it always had, course by course, on the Gallery's own plate, and then it went on past the Gallery, through the south doors and into the Square. The Square ate it standing. She could hear the silver on the cobbles when someone set a dish down.

The Gallery got one loaf on a board. Red crust. Bread of the Committee's bake.

Thibault broke the crust off his share and put it in his coat.

"For the gulls," he said. "In the morning."

She went to the stenographer's desk.

It was below the gallery rail, where it had always been. The stenographer had always been there. At every Fire of her childhood the same head bent over the same book, and nobody looked at it, because nobody looks at the person who writes things down.

She spoke loudly enough for the doors to hear. If the Square heard it said, it would be true.

"The cards are by precedence. The great houses first."

The stenographer did not look up.

"Who wrote them?" she said.

"I seat the Hall."

"By precedence."

"As the platters stand. Nearest the Hearth first. As the cloches were always lifted."

That was all.

She knew how the platters stood. Everyone did. The Committee had moved them in its first spring, reading out each step from the Ordinances in red. The gilded platters were now at the cold end, in the order of their old nearness, turned round: what had been nearest the Hearth was farthest. The Vane platter stood right by the doors, last of all, because the Mother has no other hand. The cadet lines, which had always been at the far end of the gold, stood now on the Hearth side of it.

Nearest the Hearth first.

"And the Hall is seated again at dawn," the stenographer said, to the book. "From the platters."

She went back to the cold end and counted the platters between hers and the Hearth, the way she counted candles.

Three.

## VII to IX

She danced the seventh and the eighth by the card and did not hear them.

The ninth ended. The Square applauded, and every head at the doors turned to its hands to clap. The polisher had just gone past, a Commons woman, one of the Hall's polishers now. She went down the row with her cloth and breathed on each scraped rim as she passed, because the Committee had written that she could. She was at the far end.

Isaure picked up her own platter.

Silver, her loaf on it, the cloche over the loaf. It was heavier than it looked and lighter than it should have been. She carried it one platter's length toward the south doors, past the platter of the house of Lormeau, and set it down on the other side.

Silver on stone. Very quietly.

It was the oldest crime in the Ordinances, a platter moved. For the Commons it had been one copper and a night's tokens the finger-breadth. She had moved hers the other way, toward the cold, toward the fog and the Square, away from the fire.

She told herself he was kind and would have offered.

She told herself he was older and had had more of it.

She did not tell herself anything about the gulls.

She looked down at the platter where she had put it. By her own hand. Two finger-breadths of rim, scraped, and still a little warm from her palms.

## X to XI

Thibault had seen.

She knew it from the tenth, from how carefully he took her hand when the figure brought them together, as if it might be hurt. He did not go to the row. He did not move the platter back. He did not look at it again.

At the end of the eleventh he bowed to her.

"The twelfth has no partner," he said. "May I?"

The old etiquette lets a gentleman ask for any dance left unfilled. Nobody had ever asked her for one.

She could not say no to him now. So she said yes.

She was counting on the ferry. *At the ferry*, the card said. The ball ended when the ferry came. If the boat came in before the twelfth was called, the twelfth would never be danced, and she would never have to stand opposite him beside the Hearth.

She had three candles left by her count.

The ferry had never once been early.

## XII

It was not early. It was on time.

Grey came into the doorway first, and then the bell from the Wharf, and then a sound from the Square like a crowd taking one breath. Through the south doors she watched the crate come up from the water. Four dockhands carried it on their shoulders through the Square and in at the doors, past the dancers, the stencil on its side turned to the room: *for the Committee*. They set it down beside the chalked square. The carpenters were already there with their tools.

Nobody ended the ball.

Nobody told the consort to stop.

At the sixth bell the Iron-Wardens let one man off the floor. Évariste Vane had not danced all night. He crossed the Hall to the sub-basement door, holding his feeding hand a little away from his coat, and went through it, and down the ninety-one steps to the Mother. She has no other hand. The door closed behind him.

The rest of them were kept.

The twelfth was called.

She danced it with Thibault beside the chalked square.

Behind him the carpenters took the crate apart. Boards came off. They stood the uprights up and made them fast. Something went across the top. Something heavy was lifted with a rope and a word from the older carpenter. With each figure the thing beside the Hearth stood a little taller, and the Hearth's light lay along one side of it.

The Razor.

The viol played in a major key. By the old article the consort played nothing in a minor key before a sentence, and nobody had read a sentence.

The Square applauded between figures. It had learned that too.

Her last counted candle went out at the turn. After that the only light was the Hearth, and the fog coming grey through the doors, and the two together made no shadows.

At the last turn Thibault pressed something into her glove. The crust from his coat.

"For the gulls," he said. "In case."

No candles. No chairs. No one to say stop.

Music.

## Coda

At dusk the Aboyeur carried a loaf down the Hall and set it on the empty platter nearest the Hearth, for a name under seal. He called no name. The night was reckoned from it.

The stenographer had seated the Hall again at dawn, walking the row with the plan while the carpenters finished. At dusk the ushers followed the paper. They read the gilded platters off as they stood, nearest the Hearth first.

*Lormeau.*

*Lanvaux.*

Thibault's name went before hers because his platter now stood nearer the Hearth than hers. She had put it there one place ahead of her, a platter's length, by her own hand, and nothing on the paper said a platter had been moved.

She lifted her own cloche. Under it was her loaf, cold, for the morning. She took the card from her bodice, cream, Lady Day of '89, one dance filled in her brother's hand. She laid it on the loaf and set the cloche back over both.

The thing beside the Hearth had been finished at noon. The consort was gone from the stair.

She still had Thibault's crust in her glove. She had not gone to the window with it. She did not know what the gulls ate when no one fed them.

The next night had begun, and the platters stood where she had put them.
