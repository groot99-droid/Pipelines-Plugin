# The Express Safe

## The count

Monday, the eighteenth of December. Station Rush.

The board by the pump. My notice had been turned again.

The Constable pastes them face out. By the next Rush somebody has taken them down, written on the back and put them up face in. That morning the back was the baker's, in pencil, with flour in the creases.

Three coins behind her crock. Two prints in her flour that would not agree. Then the last of it, at the bottom, pressed hard:

*A thief who pays is keeping an account.*

I read it twice. I liked it. Nobody had ever put it about me so well.

I turned the sheet face out again myself, with the four tacks, and read the front. The front I knew. The ovens. The plate. Then:

*3. The Company Bank, the vault. Not yet.*
*4. The Daily Train, the express safe. Not yet.*

*Height: short, by the bootprint. Scarf: grey wool.*

A man at my elbow said they'd never catch him, a short man in grey in a mill town. The woman beside him said that was half the town, and they both laughed. I had my scarf on. I keep one end tucked inside my coat. I always have.

Then the stone.

Agnes is first up every morning, so she can tell the Row before it asks. Her arm was still bound in its sling from the wolf. She came down calling a name to every door. I stood in the crowd at the pump, put my hand inside my coat lining, and took one round out of the sock.

That is my arithmetic. I'll set it down once, plainly.

A round for every living soul in HollowMere, and one for every man of the law. Not one more. A gun loaded for everyone isn't loaded for anyone in particular. That's a courtesy too, the way I see it. Nobody in this town is singled out by me.

When the stone takes a name, I take a round. That evening I dropped it in the mere off the end of the planks, where the water is black and gives nothing back.

And a gold coin for every score, polished on the scarf first. The baker got hers on the hearth-lip and on the counter. God got brass.

Two scores to go. The safe goes out on the Train. I had a week to do the vault first, and a week is a long time in a town that can't leave.

I have never carried a round for the dead.

## The road

Tuesday, the nineteenth.

The storm came down off the hills in the night, and by the Midday Shift the coach road was gone. Not closed. Gone. A carter turned back at the first bend with snow to his horse's chest, and said so at the Bell.

So it went round the square, the way things go round it. The Train is the only way in. The Train is the only way out. The last train out goes at dusk on Saturday. After that the Line shuts for the storm, and nothing comes.

My week was four days now.

I counted them on the hand inside my coat. Wednesday. Thursday. Friday. Saturday. The safe would have to go on that last train, somewhere between here and wherever it got to. So the vault had to go before it.

No coach. No horse that would go. No road. The Train.

## The vault

Thursday, the twenty-first. After Curfew.

The guard's stool at the bank door was empty, with the cushion on it, the way it always is. One of the escort's dogs lay along the step. It lifted its head when I came and looked at me. Then it put its head down on its paws again. I don't know what it saw. Something it had decided was not its business.

The vault came open. That is all I will say about the vault.

The gold went into the case, a plain case and heavy now. I took the coin from my pocket and rubbed it on the scarf until it caught the lantern. Then I set it on the shelf where the gold had been. The Company would find it in the morning and enter it, and somewhere in the Eastern Office a clerk would wonder.

I never drew the gun. Not one round was fired.

Out in the lane the fog had come in off the mere. I walked with my hand in my coat, counting the sock by touch, the way I do. Other people say their prayers walking home.

They say it's the one bank that can't fail, because the town can't leave it. It hadn't needed a single round.

## Short one

The same night. Under the station boards.

There's a dry place under the boards at the platform's end, where the crew throw their ash. I keep the case there. Some nights I keep myself there too.

I counted before sleep, as every night. The cylinder first. Then the sock, round by round, between finger and thumb.

One short.

I counted again by a match. One short.

There was no casing. The cylinder smelled of oil and nothing else. I would have heard a shot. After Curfew I hear everything in this town: the crew's shovel, the planks at the wood edge, the mere at the dock. Nobody fired anything that night. Not me.

So I'd dropped it. In the vault, in the dark, taking the sock out to get at the coin. That was all. It would be lying on the floor by the shelf. The Company would find it with my coin and know the calibre, which it knew already.

I lay with my hand on the sock. Five fingers on it, and the sock was wrong.

## The name

Friday, the twenty-second.

I went to the board before the Rush, before it was light, to see what the Company had found.

The Constable had been. There was new paste on my notice, with the score written in:

*3. The Company Bank, the vault. Entered.*

Under it was the sum, which I won't put here. Then the traces: *a gold coin, on the vault shelf.*

Nothing else. No round on any floor.

So it wasn't in the vault. In the lane, then. In the fog on the way home, when I had the sock out counting. A round in the mud of the bank lane. Somebody's child would find it in the spring.

Then came the Rush, and the crowd at the pump, and Agnes down the Row with her arm in the sling, calling a name to every door before anyone asked.

I knew the face that went with it. Somebody who paid at the Company Store in exact coppers and counted them out twice on the counter before letting go. I stood behind them in the line once and counted along under my breath. We got to the end at the same time.

Nobody said how. Nobody asks how now. The Undertaker's book stopped asking some time ago.

I put my hand into my coat to take a round out for the name.

The count was right.

I counted again with my back to the pump. Right. A round for every living soul and every man of the law, and not one more. I hadn't taken anything out. A round lost in the fog would have left me short, and I wasn't short. I was even.

One round gone in the night, one soul gone in the night. The sum came out the same as if I'd done it with my own hand at the stone. That's arithmetic. Only arithmetic: two things that come to the same number.

I had a round for that name. I will not write it.

## Upright

Friday night.

The hoods were out after Curfew, for the first time. Flour sacks with the eyes cut out, and torches, going up the Rise after anyone who walked in threes.

I walk alone. I went the other way, down to the platform, to move the case somewhere better.

The case was where I'd left it. I opened it by feel. The gold was all there, still in the bank's own paper. Nobody had touched it.

On the beam above it, in the dark, at the height of my eyes, a casing stood on its end.

I struck a match to be sure. Brass, a .45, spent. It was stood on its base, upright, the way you'd set one down if you wanted it found standing.

I stood one like that at St Brannock's, on the plate, where the coins had been. I know how it looks. It's mine. It's what I leave.

I didn't leave this one.

Arithmetic doesn't leave signs. A number doesn't come down under the station boards in the night and stand a shell on a beam over your case. Somebody does.

At the end of the platform lane one torch had stopped. Just stopped, the way a man stops when he's heard something. It lit the snow round it and the fence and nothing else. I couldn't see anybody under it. I watched it until the match burned my fingers. When I looked again it was going on up the Rise with the others.

Somebody in HollowMere knew me: my face, or my walk, or the place a hand can go. They had been at my case and taken nothing. They were keeping my account.

Someone had tipped me.

## The last train

Saturday, the twenty-third.

I kept away from Harold's sale. At the Midday Shift I saw the square from a doorway. Everybody was standing on their own, nobody in threes, and the long coats stood along the bank front looking at the bank. They'd been looking at it since the Baron fell. Since Thursday night they'd had a reason.

At dusk I went down to the platform with the case.

The last train was in, breathing, its lamps lit along its length. The snow came sideways under the roof.

The ones leaving weren't many. A drummer with his tonic trunk. A Mill Row family a fare short, the mother arguing it at the step. A man who wouldn't tell the Conductor where he was going and paid to the end of the line. And me: one passenger with a heavy case.

I bought one-way at the window, in scrip. Pruett took the ticket at the step, and his crescent came down on it once. He looked at the case, not at me.

The Company counts the crate. I was counting too.

If somebody in HollowMere was keeping my account, I'd leave it square. I wouldn't go owing the town a thing. I stood on the car step as the train took the strain, and I counted the souls aboard I could see. Pruett at the step. The driver and the fireman, up front in the glow. The drummer. The family, every one of them, the smallest on a lap. The man who wouldn't say. I didn't count myself. I never have.

I kept that many rounds.

The rest belonged to the town: a round for every soul back there, and for the law. We went out onto the last mile over the drained lake. I put my hand in the sock and dropped them off the step one at a time into the dark, the way I did at the mere. The snow took them before they landed.

Under the last mile, the talk goes, there are four sticks that never went off. We went over them. They didn't.

Punched once. Counted out.

## The safe

Saturday, dusk. Moving.

I went forward to the express car. That's as much as I'll say about the going.

The lamp in there swung on its hook. Crates. The mail. The safe at the end.

The safe came open. That's all I'll say about the safe.

The Company's sealed sacks went into the case, on top of the bank's gold. I took out the last coin, rubbed it on the scarf until it caught the lamp, and set it on the safe's empty floor.

Four scores, four tips: the ovens, the plate, the bank, the Train. The account was closed.

Behind us, HollowMere was going out like a lamp. Ahead lay Thorne Junction, with the storm in between. The Line would shut behind us, and nothing would come up it for five days. Nothing could come after.

The Company counts the crate, not the contents.

## One over

The same car.

I counted by touch, as every night. The cylinder, then the sock.

One more than I'd kept.

I turned the lamp up and laid them out in a row on the lid of the safe, in the light. I counted them with a finger on each.

One more.

I counted the train again, the only way I could from in there: by what I'd seen at the step. Pruett. The driver. The fireman. The drummer. The family. The man who wouldn't say. Car 3 behind us held the week's boxes, and I don't count the dead.

One round more than I can count souls for.

*A passenger booked in is a passenger counted out.* That's the Company's own rule, in the Rules of Carriage. Pruett counted me in, and Pruett will count me out. If I count myself, the sum comes right. I have never counted myself.

Or there's somebody on this train who is on nobody's count.

No step in the car. No light in the gap between the crates. The lamp swinging, and stopping, and swinging. One over.

## The siding

The brakes take.

I feel it in the floor before I hear it. The crates lean. The lamp swings out on its hook and stays out.

Through the slats the snow is going past slower, and pines, and in the pines a light. Swinging, low. This is Gallows Wood Siding, and the Siding stops on signal only. Somebody is signalling. A lantern, or something that knows how a lantern moves.

The train slows. It doesn't stop. We're still moving.

My hand is on the row on the lid of the safe.

In the next car there's a step. It comes down the aisle toward the express car door, unhurried: the step of a man with a punch in his hand and a count to make.

I have never carried a round for the dead.

I count again. One over.
