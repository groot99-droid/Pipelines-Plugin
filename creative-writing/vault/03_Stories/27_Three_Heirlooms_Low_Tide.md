# Three Heirlooms, Low Tide

*Wednesday 13 December. Low water 10.15, night. Father's mark: short.*

My grandmother started the book the first winter the mere kept a tide. Her hand is on the first pages, in ink, small and leaning. After that it's my father's, in pencil, because pencil doesn't run when the book gets wet. Mine comes in after the twenty-ninth of November.

I don't write tides in it. I check them. At every low he marked I take the book and the lantern down to the ferry post and see whether he was right. When he was, I write *true* in the margin.

He has been right every time.

That night my mother was on the dock behind me with the nets, whistling the two notes. High, then low. Then the wait, where his used to come.

The ferry post has the tar line on it, where Tuck ties up. Everybody knows the tar line. A hand's breadth below it my father cut a notch with his knife, and nobody knows the notch. It's ours. If the water stops above it, the book says *short*. If it goes under, the book says *under*, and on the furthest nights of all it says *door*.

Somebody was at the post.

It was a dark coat, at the edge of my light. Not in the light, just where it gives out. Its thumb was on the notch.

I stopped on the boards. The coat didn't take its thumb away. It was watching the water the way my father watched it, not the way Tuck does. Tuck looks at the tar line. This was looking lower.

The water went down slowly, as if it was thinking about it. It stopped a hand short of the notch. Short, the way the book said.

The coat took its thumb off and went along the bank toward the cave side, not hurrying. It never looked at my light. That was how I knew it had seen it.

When I got to the post there was blue on the tar, right on the notch. It was a smear, the kind a thumb leaves when it has been leaning on the chart at the Lantern, where the circle never seems to be dry.

Pell, I thought. Pell checks his skiff at low water, and Pell leans on the Lantern wall like everybody does.

I wrote *true* against the hour, and under it, *Pell*.

---

*Thursday 14 December. Low water 11.05, night. Father's mark: short.*

At the pump in the morning it was Harold's shelf.

Gert's brooch was gone off it in the night. That was the jet one, with her grandfather's hair plaited under the glass, and it had been her grandmother's. Nothing else was touched, not the ring beside it.

Gert came down the Row with her boy behind her carrying the empty basket. She had scrubbed her step already. She went and stood at Harold's window and looked at the place where it had been.

The Row had it settled before my bucket was full. The Killer takes keepsakes now, they said. It takes something off you so it can remember you.

At the dock I asked Tuck whether Pell had been out at the low.

"Pell?" Tuck said. "Pell's been abed since Sunday with his chest. I took him his water."

I put my thumb on the notch. There was no blue on it now. The damp had had it in the night.

So it wasn't Pell. I had written *Pell* in my father's book and it was wrong, and I rubbed it out. Whoever it was knew the notch, and stood at it the same low water Gert's brooch went.

The coat at the notch took Gert's brooch. I knew it the way you know a net is torn before you find the tear.

The town has a word ready for anything that goes in the night. I didn't have the word. What I had was a coat.

---

*Friday 15 December. Low water 11.55, morning. Father's mark: short.*

So I read the book back, and not for the hours this time. I was looking for why anybody would want the notch.

It's in my father's pencil. Twice a moon there's one word beside two nights of the big low: *door*. Never more than two nights. It's always the second night of the run and the fourth. I'd always read it as his word for the furthest under. I never thought it was a door to anything.

I turned forward. The next run starts on Christmas Eve. *Door* stands against Christmas night, and *door* against Wednesday the twenty-seventh.

At the morning low I walked the bank, out along the channel past where the boats are drawn up, to the cave mouth, where the old iron gate is chained across. Through the bars, at the back, is the place where the mere goes in under the rock. The town calls it the sea door. There's no sea. There are boards nailed across where the water runs in under the stone. Nobody goes near it, because of the cave.

The water was down, and from the bars I could see the boards all the way to the bottom one. The bottom two hung wrong. Somebody had loosed them and set them back. That was all there was to see, and you'd have had to be looking.

On Saturday night Reg didn't come out of the Tin Gallery. The cave takes people. That's all I'll say about the cave.

On Saturday Harold paid Gert what the brooch was worth out of his own drawer, and the Row knew it by noon.

I sat on my bed with the book and worked it out the way my mother works a net, one hole at a time. It was a thief. A thief that took a brooch already pawned, and now paid for besides, so Gert had her money and nobody was hurt. A thief who takes only what is already lost, and goes out by the sea door on Christmas night, and is gone.

A thief that goes is a thief gone. I wrote that in the back of the book as well.

---

*Tuesday 19 December. Low water 3.15, morning. Father's mark: short. Storm.*

The storm came in on Monday night and shut the coach road. I didn't go down to the post. You couldn't stand on the boards. I lay listening to the water, and it never went down. The storm held it up against the boards. It was the first time the book was wrong, and it wasn't my father's fault.

At first light Agnes came down the Row with her arm still bound up from the wolf. She called the stone at every door, the way she does, before anyone could ask. Then she called something else.

"My grandmother's ring."

She said it the way she said the stone's name.

It had hung on a nail over her bed, she said, because she couldn't get it over her knuckle any more. In the night it went. She had been asleep. Nothing else in the room was touched. Her shawl was still on the chair and her scrip was still in the jug.

It had stood over her. It had wanted only the ring.

By noon the Row had barred its doors. The Killer comes in now, they said. It stands over your bed, takes a keepsake and leaves you. Nobody said why it leaves you. Nobody wanted to.

I sat with the book open at the page where I'd written *a thief who takes only what is already lost*. Agnes's ring hadn't been lost. Nobody had pawned it, and nobody had paid her for it. It was taken from a sleeping house, at the low water, the one night the book was wrong.

I didn't rub that line out. I left it there, so I'd have to look at it.

---

*Wednesday 20 December. Low water 4.30, afternoon. Father's mark: short.*

I took the book up to the Constable's under my shawl. I was going to tell. I had it all ready: the coat, the notch, the boards, the two nights.

The Constable was out. The Detective was at the desk, the one there is now, who kept time at the railyard once and still carries the watch. On the blotter there was a stamp, inked, and a file with nothing in it.

"What's that?" he said.

"My father's tide book."

"Can I see it?"

He held out his hand. There was blue down the side of his thumb, the chart's blue. Anybody who leans on the Lantern wall gets it.

"I'd want to keep it a while," he said. "Check it against the chart. You'd have it back."

I looked at the stamp. I looked at the file with nothing in it. My father's book would go into that file. Somebody would check it against the chart at the Lantern, where my father went out at the circle on the twenty-ninth, and they'd write a word on it. The word would be short.

"Does anything move on the water," he said, "at the circle?"

"No."

He wrote it down.

I took the book home under my shawl. I'd lied to the Detective to keep it, and now nobody in HollowMere knew the door lows except me and the coat.

He wrote down *no* the way my father wrote *true*.

---

*Sunday 24 December. Low water 7.50, night. Father's mark: under. First night of the run.*

The last train went out at dusk on Saturday with several people on it. I didn't go to see who. The long coats stood along the platform and looked at anybody carrying anything. The coat wasn't on the train. I knew that without going. A thing that wants a door doesn't take a train.

Two of the long coats had been carried down from the cave side since Thursday. The second one was Saturday, at the low. The pump didn't say how.

At the sale on Saturday my mother bought Ivy Cotter's lantern off Harold's list for the dock. She paid three times what was lent. So he can see where I am, she said. On Christmas Eve she sat out on the dock with it lit and the nets across her knees, and whistled.

I was at the window with the book. Out there the water was going under the notch. The book said *under* for tonight: under, but not far enough. Not *door*. Not yet.

The coat came up out of the dark at the edge of her light, where the light stops. My mother didn't turn round. She was whistling.

It came and stood at my window. I couldn't see a face, only the coat, and a sack with something in the bottom of it.

"You told him no," it said.

I didn't say anything.

"Three things that were somebody's grandmother's," it said. "And the sea door, at low water."

It said it like a list it had said before, to somebody else.

"I have two."

It looked past me at the table, at the book.

"That one was your grandmother's."

The window was open a crack for the lamp smoke. It could have had the book off the table before I turned round. It didn't reach for it.

"No," I said, and shut the window.

On the other side of the glass it waited exactly as long as the water waits at the bottom of a low before it turns. Then it went.

---

*Monday 25 December. Low water 8.40, night. Father's mark: door.*

On Christmas morning there was a twist of paper on the sill, outside the shut window.

It smelled of saltpetre and match-heads. I opened it on the table.

Inside was a jet brooch with a plait of grey-brown hair under the glass. There was a ring too, thin, worn flat on one side where a hand had worked in it.

Gert's. Agnes's.

My first thought was to carry them back. Up the Row, and knock.

But every door on the Row was barred against a Killer who takes keepsakes. Anybody knocking on Christmas morning with Gert's grandmother's brooch in her hand would be the Killer. That's how the Row would count it. It's how I would have counted it.

I had told it no, and now my no was lying on the table in a twist of paper.

I put them in the net locker, under the spare floats at the bottom, where my mother doesn't go any more. The brooch and the ring were hidden in our house now. My no couldn't keep me out of it.

Two grandmothers in our house, and mine in the book on the table.

---

*Later.*

At eight my mother came in off the dock, hung the lantern on its hook and sat down by the fire with her hands in her lap. I took the lantern down again. She didn't ask. She hasn't asked anything since the twenty-ninth.

I had the book under my arm. The brooch and the ring were in the twist, in my fist. I'd been the one keeping them hidden in our house all day, and I couldn't keep them there another night.

The coat was at the post, at the edge of the light.

I opened the book to the page, my father's pencil. *Door*, and the hour.

"Twenty to nine," I said.

That was all. It nodded. We both watched the water.

It went past the tar line, then past the notch, and it kept going. I had never seen it so far down. The channel stones came up out of it black.

I held out the twist and it took it. Then I held out the book.

That was where I chose. Nobody made me. The brooch and the ring had gone out of my hands the minute I let them into the house. But the book was mine, and I held it out.

It put all three in the sack. Three grandmothers' things.

"Nobody's hero," it said. Or that's what I remember it saying.

It had the book now. It had the hour as well. The hour was the only thing I gave it that it couldn't have taken.

---

"Put that out," it said, meaning the lantern.

So I put it out. That was Ivy Cotter's lantern. It had sat unlit on Harold's shelf since October, and my mother had lit it every night since Saturday. I put it out on the boards.

It was dark. Then my eyes came.

The coat went along the bank toward the cave side. For a little while I could hear it on the stones, and then I couldn't.

Far round the water the Company's lamp hung at the Gate, where it has hung for a week now. I didn't look at it long.

Out on the marsh there were lights that aren't steady. I didn't follow them.

I stood at the post with my thumb on the notch. The water came back over my thumb and over the notch, and then over the tar line. Nothing came back along the bank.

Behind me the cottage door opened. My mother whistled the two notes from the dock, in the dark, because I had her lantern and it was out. High, then low. I didn't answer. I have never answered. They aren't mine to answer.

The water was over the door notch again, with the coat behind the sea door, and whatever is behind it was behind it.

---

*Wednesday 27 December.*

There's no heading for tonight. I haven't got the book.

I didn't need it. I've read his pencil on that page so many times I could see it on the ceiling. The fourth night, the second door, at twenty past ten.

My mother let me take the lantern. She lit it for me first.

The water went under the notch at twenty past ten, right on time. I was right without the book, and my father was right without me.

On the notch, under the lantern, there was a thumb. It was fresh and wet, with blue in it.

It wasn't mine. Mine were in my pockets.

Somebody had read the door notch tonight. It could have been the coat, come back for something it forgot. It could have been whoever circles the chart at the Lantern, because that's still circled every morning, coat or no coat. It could have been a man with a watch and blue down the side of his thumb, or anybody at all who leans on the Lantern wall. I've thought about it all night, and I can't make it come to one.

I put my thumb on the notch beside it, so there were two. Then I went home and didn't tell anyone.

Tomorrow at the pump Agnes will call the stone. Somebody will say the Killer took Gert's brooch and Agnes's ring, and I'll fill my bucket. My mother let *drowned* stand. I can let *Killer* stand. It's a short word, and it's the one the town has.

The door lows come round again in a moon, the second night and the fourth. I know where they fall without the book, and whoever owns that thumb will want to know.

I wrote the hour on the back of my hand in pencil. Under it I wrote the one word of his I've got left.

*True.*
