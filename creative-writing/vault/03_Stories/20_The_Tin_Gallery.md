# The Tin Gallery

## 1.

Saturday, 16 December 1893. The fourth night the fog did not lift.

The cough was on the Row. You could hear it from the mouth of the Gallery, door after door, as if one chest were going down the street and stopping at each house. The wolves had come down to the fence at noon. Noon. Mere-rot makes them bold, and everybody knew it, and everybody had stopped saying it.

Norman sat on the timber at the Gallery mouth with Reg.

They were the shut mine's two hands. The Company had issued each of them a lantern, a watch, chalk and rope, so they would have something to hold now that there was nothing to dig. Norman held his watch. He looked at it the way he always did, against nothing. There was nowhere either of them had to be.

Reg cracked his knuckles. Little finger first, then up the hand: the adit, the first level, the second, the long drift, the wet level, the bottom. Top to bottom. The old levels, in order. He did it the way other men whistle.

"I heard it again," Reg said.

Norman did not ask what.

"Two," Reg said. "Under the hill. Last night and tonight, about the curfew. Two, and then nothing." He laid his hand flat on the timber and struck it twice with the heel of the other. The way you strike a plug. The way the plug had been struck.

"Stone settles," Norman said.

"Two."

"Water works on stone. It's had a lake on it."

Reg looked into the mouth. Cold air came out of it, with the smell it had always had: stone that has had water on the far side of it for a long time.

Norman took his hat off and put it on his knee.

"Stone settles," Norman said.

Reg didn't crack the last knuckle.

## 2.

The Curfew bell. Dusk, or what the fog let pass for it.

Reg's lantern was on its nail in the tinners' shed, cold. His coat was gone. His pipe was gone. Reg was not there.

Norman stood in the door of the shed a long time.

Reg knew the Gallery in the dark. He had always said so. He knew it by his knuckles, level by level, and he would have gone down without a light because a light was oil and oil was scrip and Reg had never had scrip to spare. He had heard two knocks under the hill and he had gone down to answer them.

On the path between the shed and the mouth the wolves were already out. Norman could see their eyes at the bottom of the fog, low, patient, moving when he moved.

A pair goes down together. A pair comes up together.

He had gone down with Jory Kitto. He had come up. Jory had not.

He chalked two strokes on the mouth timber, the old way, so anyone passing would know: two down. Then he lit his lantern and turned it up, and walked into the light it made. The wolves held at the edge of it, the way they hold, and did not cross.

The only lit lantern under the hill was his.

## 3.

Past the mouth the Gallery was dark and wet. Water on the rail bed. Water off the roof.

On the path the light had kept everything off him. He had walked inside it like a man inside a house.

Then the bats.

They came off the roof in one sheet. Not away. To it. At the glass, at his face, at the hand that held the lantern. Wings like wet leather, little teeth. One opened the back of his hand. One took a nick out of his ear. He swung the lantern and they came back to it.

Then the rats.

Up the rail bed toward him, out of the dark below, in a long grey run. He braced for them and they did not stop for him. They did not stop for the light. They went over his boots and round his ankles and on up past him, out of the mouth, out into the fog, toward the wolves.

Rats do not run toward wolves.

He stood with blood on the back of his hand, dripping off his knuckles, and watched the last of them go.

They were not running from him.

## 4.

The pump beam stood where it had stood since the plug, rusted at its pivot, over the place where the water began.

On Sunday he had come down this far, as he did some Sundays, and looked. On Sunday the water had stood to the ankle, where it always stood. There was a mark on the timber leg of the beam where the wet stopped.

Tonight the water stood over the mark. A hand over it.

So that was it.

The mere keeps a tide with no sea. Everybody in HollowMere knew that. The water had come back once before, all of it, a whole lake into the hill in one night. It was coming back again, a little, the way the mere came and went. Rats run from water. Any tinner knows that. Rats leave a working before the water does.

The water was coming back. The rats ran from water.

He said it twice.

"Reg," he said, once, down the Gallery.

The Gallery took it.

The water was coming back. That was all.

## 5.

Below the pump beam the rail ran on into the water. On the stone beside it, the smear.

It was wide. Wide as a man's shoulders. It came up out of the water onto the rail bed, and went along the bed a little way, and turned, and went back down into the water again.

Water comes in. Water goes out. It does not climb out of itself, drag across the stone, and go home.

There was something in the smear that was not water. Darker. Thicker. Where it ran down into the edge of the water it lay on top and would not mix, the way lamp oil lies on a puddle, but it was not oil. He put his lantern near it. It shone. It smelled of tin.

In the middle of the smear lay Reg's pipe.

Clay, short-stemmed, the bowl cold. Reg never lit it below ground. He said a pipe underground was a light, and the old hands said you don't carry more light than you need. He carried it anyway. He bit on the stem when he was frightened, and the stem was bitten through.

Norman picked it up.

Reg never dropped that pipe.

## 6.

Reg had slipped.

That was all. Reg had come down in the dark, the fool, by his knuckles, and the wet had taken his feet, and he had gone down hard and hurt himself. And then he had crawled. Down. Toward the knocking. Because Reg was a fool for a knock, and two knocks was a man asking, and Reg would not leave a man asking.

Hurt. Crawling. Dragging himself. That was the smear.

Norman went down after it.

Past the ore cart, lying on its side where it had lain since the plug. Past the crate on its two planks above the water, VANE TIN CO on the lid, the lid pried up and left, the way the whole town knew it was left. The smear went past the crate and down.

He held the lantern up. All the way up, the wick turned high, the way Jory had kept his. Jory had paid for his own oil so he would never be in the dark down here. Norman had laughed at him for it, once.

"Reg," he said. "Reg. It's me. Reg."

He looked at his watch. It said a time. It meant nothing down here.

He held the lantern up, the way Jory did, so Reg would see.

## 7.

Reg was below the crate, on a dry shelf of stone above the water.

He was on his back. His arms were at his sides. He had not crawled there. A crawling man ends on his face, reaching. Reg had been put down, the way you put down something you have carried and mean to come back to.

"Reg," Norman said, because he was still saying it.

Two bites.

One had taken the shoulder and the side of the neck, all of it, down to where the collarbone should have been and was not. One had opened him low on the left side, and what had been inside was partly beside him and partly not anywhere. The dark stuff that would not mix was on him, on his shirt and in his hair. Round him the grit was scored with drag marks, long, in pairs, going away toward the water and coming back.

His right hand was whole.

It lay by his hip, palm down, knuckles up. Broad, scarred, the little finger bent from an old fall. Norman would have known that hand anywhere. He had watched it crack its way top to bottom every day since the mine shut.

He knelt. He did not touch it. He did not crack his own knuckles, though his hand wanted to.

Reg had made it to the bottom of his knuckles.

## 8.

"Wolves," Norman said.

He said it to the dark, aloud, so it would be said.

There was no fur on Reg. Not a hair. There were no claw marks on the stone, none on Reg, nothing raked. A wolf does not come into a cave. A wolf does not come into the light of a lit lantern; it holds at the edge, and it does not cross. Norman had walked through them an hour ago with this lantern and watched them hold.

Two bites. A wolf does not take a shoulder in one.

The town would say wolves. The town always said wolves. Wolves were what the town had.

"Wolves," he said again, so he would have said it twice.

The word went down the Gallery and did not come back.

## 9.

Below the shelf where Reg lay, the lower drift went under.

The level Jory had been working. The level of the plug. Since the night the lake went into the hill, there had been boards laid over the mouth of that drift, heavy, spiked down, so no one would walk into it in the dark and go where Jory went.

The boards were up.

They were not broken. They were not floated off. They lay stacked beside the drift, dry, on the dry stone, one on another, square, the edges true. The way you stack timber you mean to put back.

Wolves do not stack boards. Water does not.

In the grit by the stack there were prints. One pair. Going in to the edge of the drift, and coming back out. Dry boots over the wet. And nowhere over them, on the low roof, any black from a lamp, and nowhere on the stone a drop of oil. Whoever had stood here had come down without a light, and taken up the boards, and stood at the edge of the open drift, and gone back up.

Someone had opened the way down.

Norman did not try to think who. He found he could not get his mind to go up the Gallery after them. It stayed where it was, at the open drift, at his feet.

Someone had come down here in the dark, and gone home.

## 10.

Two knocks.

From the drift. Close. Under his feet, nearly.

Then the water in the open drift rose. No splash. It rose the way water rises in a jug, smooth, and something came up out of it, and the water ran off it, and it came on up onto the stone.

Big. The size of the ore cart. Bigger.

Long and low. The grey of wet slate, and shining like the stuff in the smear.

A mouth at the front. Nothing round the mouth he would call a face.

It did not look at him. It did not look at Reg. It came toward the lantern.

He did not decide anything. His arm put the lantern behind him, and the thing came round for it, and he was in the way.

The first hit took his left forearm. He heard it before he felt it. Then he saw it: the sleeve gone, the meat of the arm gone with it to the bone in one long bite, the white of the bone in the lamplight, the lamp swinging on its handle in his other hand. He was on his knees. He did not remember going down.

It drew back.

Two strikes on a plug, and the plug goes. Every tinner knows the second one. He knew it now.

It wanted the light.

## 11.

One more.

He could see it in the edge of the light, waiting at the lantern's reach as the wolves had waited at the edge of it outside. Not looking at him. Looking at the light.

If he put it out, he would be in the dark with it. If he kept it, it would come for the light and he was holding the light.

The crate stood above him on its two planks, a few yards up the bed from where Reg lay. VANE TIN CO.

He got up. He did not know how. He climbed the shelf on his knees and one hand, holding the lantern by the bail, and he set it on the lid of the crate and turned the wick all the way up. As high as Jory would have. Higher. The light stood up off the crate, bright, and ran down the stone and lay on Reg's whole hand.

Then he backed out of it. Into the dark.

He went up the Gallery the way Reg went down it. By the levels. The bottom, the wet level, the long drift, the second, the first, the adit. Bottom to top. He counted them on the knuckles of the hand that still worked, one by one, and the dark did not get any lighter.

Behind him the light stayed lit.

He heard it reach the light. He heard it reach Reg.

He did not turn.

A pair goes down together.

He came up.

## 12.

The mouth, with no light in it. The fog outside. Grey on black.

He did not go out. The wolves were at the edge of the path, and he had no edge to give them now; the lantern going out is the town's dread, and his was on a crate below the hill. He sat inside the mouth on the timber, out of their reach and out of the reach of the light he could no longer see, and bled. It went from his arm down his fingers in drips onto the stone. He listened to it. He listened below it, for anything coming up.

Nothing came up.

He held his hat on his knee with his good hand. He looked at his watch, against nothing, and could not see it.

The Station Rush. The whistle, far off, out of the pines. The fog going thin and grey.

Someone was standing at the mouth.

No lantern. Just standing, in the thin fog, at the place where the two chalk strokes were. Looking in at him.

Norman looked back.

Neither of them said anything.

Then the someone turned, and walked back down the path toward the town, and the fog took them.

They asked him on the square. Where was Reg.

Reg went down alone, he said. In the dark. Lost in the workings.

He said nothing about the boards. He said nothing about the prints, or the smear, or the thing that came up out of the water, or the lantern burning on the crate below the hill, if it was burning still. They were the Company's words, *lost in the workings*. The Company had written them once already, about Jory. Now he gave them to Reg.

The town nodded. The town said wolves, after, among itself. He let it.

"Stone settles," he said.

He came up.
