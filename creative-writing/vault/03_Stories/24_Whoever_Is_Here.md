# Whoever Is Here

The platform an hour before the Rush. Fog on the boards. One lantern.

He is on the bench already. He always is. He has the lantern end, and when you come up the steps he moves his elbow off the dark end without looking. That is the whole of what passes between you most mornings, and it is more than passes between you and anyone else.

He came up the Line on the eleventh of October. Railyard bunks. Powder under his nails, like all of them. He keeps his scrip in a sock, and a one-way ticket in his hatband.

He breathes on the lantern glass, and with a fingertip writes a number in the fog. He looks at it. He wipes it off before anyone else comes up the steps. You have never asked what it counts. You think it is days.

You come for the bag. You watch it come off the train, and you follow it across the lane to the window, and you stand at the window until the Postmaster has sorted it. Then you go home. Nobody has written to you in four winters.

You keep the last one in your coat. What it says is not much: a house is sold, and the name at the bottom used to be yours too. You do not keep it for that. You keep it for the outside, where your name is written in somebody's hand, in ink, as if it were worth the ink.

On the back of that envelope, in pencil, you have started a list. You did not mean it to be a list. It has a heading.

*Reasons.*

The train comes at the Rush. Nothing has ever come for you on it.

---

## 1. Because someone wrote your name

Thursday, 7 December. The Rush.

The Postmaster puts it into your hand at the window. He does not look at it. He does not write anything down. He is already looking past you at the next one.

Black wax. No sender.

Your name on the outside, in a hand.

You walk home without running, which takes everything you have.

In your room you sit on the bed with it on your knees and do not open it for a long time. You just read the outside. Your name. Somebody sat down and wrote it, letter by letter, and sealed something to it. After four winters.

Then you open it.

One line, printed.

*Burn the Letter & Keep Your Mundane Life, OR Accept the Baron's Contract.*

Under the line, a name.

You know the name. You have sat at the dark end of the bench beside it every morning since October.

The stove is lit. The door of it is open. It would take one movement.

You put the letter under the four-winters one, in your coat, against your ribs. You take the pencil and write the first reason on the back of the old envelope, because it is true and you want to see it:

*Because someone wrote your name.*

Out of everyone in the Grant, at all the rents on all the Rows, it came to you. It picked you out.

The stove burns down without being fed.

Out of the whole Grant.

---

## 2. Because the others burned it

Friday, 8 December.

You go to the window with nothing to post and buy a stamp you do not need, so that you have a reason to stand there.

The Postmaster counts your change into your hand. Then he looks up.

Only his eyes. He holds your eyes for one breath, and he seems to like holding it.

In that breath you read one word. *Again.*

He has seen this name before. Under another seal, to another hand. In November, maybe. That hand did not come back to the window looking like you look. That hand burned it.

He says nothing. He drops his eyes. You take the stamp.

So it did not choose you first.

On the walk back you stop in the lane and take the envelope out and look at the first reason. It still holds, you think, if you turn it a little. It went to someone who would burn it, and they burned it, and it came on. It went past them. It kept coming until it found the one who would not.

You cross out the first reason, because it is not quite right, and write the right one under it.

~~*Because someone wrote your name.*~~

*Because the others burned it.*

Not chosen first. Chosen last, which is better.

---

## 3. Because you could

Saturday, 9 December.

If it came on until it found the one who would not burn it, then you have to be that one. Otherwise it was a mistake to come to you, and you could not bear that.

So you answer it.

That is all there is to say about how.

Saturday's bag has something in it for you. The Postmaster puts it into your hand at the window and does not look at it. Small, in brown paper and string. No sender. Small enough for a coat.

You do not unwrap it in daylight. You do not unwrap it at all. It goes into the coat, beside the letter, beside the four-winters letter, and the coat hangs differently on you now.

You do your chores in pairs that afternoon, the way everyone not important enough to do them alone does them. Your pair talks. You answer. Your pair says you are quiet today, and you say you slept badly, which is true.

On the back of the envelope, under the struck line and the line that stands, you write a third:

*Because you could.*

It weighs less than the letter did.

---

## 4. Because it would lapse

Sunday, 10 December. Before the Rush.

You go up the steps with it in your coat.

He is there. Of course he is. He moves his elbow off the dark end.

You sit.

He takes his hat off, which he has never done, and works the ticket out of the band to show you, as if you had not seen its corner every morning since October. One way. Thorne Junction and on. January. He says it the way people here say "back east": as if it were a place, and you could already see it from the platform if the fog would lift.

"January," he says.

He puts the ticket back. He puts the hat back. He breathes on the glass and writes *28*. He looks at it. He wipes it off.

He has powder under his nails and nothing else. No secret in the sock but scrip. Nothing in his face that the Company could want. He looks at the rails the way you look at the bag.

Your hand is in your coat.

You do nothing.

The train comes. He stands for it, the way he always stands, though he is not getting on. You walk behind the bag across the lane to the window and stand there, and the Postmaster sorts it, and there is nothing for you, as always.

At home you take out the envelope and cross out the third reason.

~~*Because you could.*~~

You could not. It is that simple, and you are glad. Some of you is glad.

Then you think it through at the table, slowly, and you find that there is a way out after all. If you do nothing, nothing happens. The letter waits in your coat. The days go. Whatever it wanted lapses. It goes to nobody, and nobody finishes anything, and he gets on in January.

You write it down. It is a thinner reason than the others. You write it anyway.

*Because it would lapse.*

No train early. No letter. No hand. Nothing.

You let it lapse. That is a thing you can do.

---

## 5. Because it comes for whoever is here

Monday, 11 December.

At the window the Postmaster has a stack of something printed. He is handing copies across the sorting table to the ones who came off the morning train: a drummer, a woman with a carpet bag.

*Who to Ask in HollowMere. For the Use of Persons Arriving by the Daily Train.*

You have not arrived anywhere in four winters.

He hands you one anyway, because you are standing there.

You read it in the lane. Who to ask, and where to find them, and what not to say. A list of rules on the first page, numbered. *Ask in pairs. The town counts alibis in twos, and so should you.* *Don't miss the train.*

On the last page, in the Postmaster's own words:

*If a letter comes for you sealed in black wax, I did not open it. I could not have told you whose it was before it came. It comes in the bag like the rest, and it comes for whoever is here.*

And under it, in the margin, in a narrower hand: *Nor could I. — Clk.*

You read it until the words come apart.

It comes for whoever is here.

It did not pick you out. It did not pass the others over to find you. It came to the window, and you were at the window. It would have come to anyone standing where you stand, at the hour you stand there, every morning, for four winters.

And it does not lapse. That is the rest of it. Somebody burned it in November, and it came again in December with the same name under the line. If you do nothing, it comes again in a week, or two, to the next hand, and the next. It comes on with his name until a hand finishes it.

It comes for whoever is here.

There is one other person on that platform every morning before the Rush.

The next letter might have your name under the line, and his on the outside.

At home you do not cross out the fourth reason. You write the fifth under it, and you do not cross that out either. There is nothing to cross it out with.

*Because it comes for whoever is here.*

It wrote your name because you were there to write it to.

---

## 6.

The night of Monday the eleventh. Before curfew ends.

There is no reason written under this number. You write the number and leave the line empty.

You go up to the platform in the dark. The lane is black. The platform lantern burns, as it does all night.

He is there. An hour before the Rush, and then some. Early, in case.

The fog is on the lantern glass, and there is a number in it: *26*.

He moves his elbow.

You sit down beside him.

Later you stand up.

That is all of it that will ever be written down.

His hat is on the boards. The ticket is in the band. The sock is where it is. You touch none of it. You take only the mark.

You open the little door of the platform lantern and feed the letter to it. It is the only thing you have ever burned of your own. The paper goes. The seal comes away in your fingers, broken, and you let it go, and you do not hear it land.

You do not wipe the glass.

You leave the number.

---

## 7. The notice

Tuesday, 12 December. The Rush.

You meet your pair in the lane, as on any chore day, and you come up to the platform together. For the first time in four winters you are not early. Nobody notices. Nobody has ever noticed when you came.

Then the shouting. Then the crowd. Then the space the crowd makes round the bench.

You do not go near. You stand with your pair at the back, and your pair says *God* and *who* and *the bench, the poor sod, the bench*, and you say nothing, and that is allowed. The town counts alibis in twos. You are two.

By noon the Constable has pasted it up in his corner of the board, with four tacks and flour paste.

You read it in the crowd, over a shoulder.

*For the death of ____________, a man of the railyard bunks who came up the Line in October.*

A needle, it says, or a small-bore pistol. The Doctor puts it before curfew ended. A seal of black wax, broken, on the boards beside him. No letter found.

The Company's three particulars. *Height: unknown. No print on the boards. Scarf: none.*

*Hat: felt, brown, common.*

It is his hat. It was his hat on the boards, with the ticket in the band. And the Company has written it down as yours.

You look at it for a long time. The Company does not know who you are. So it has described you with whatever was lying there. It describes whoever is here with whatever is near them.

Further down: *He will be on the Dusk train. So will others.*

You do your chores in pairs. Your pair talks about the bench all afternoon. The lantern. The hour. Who sits on a platform in the dark before the Rush. Some lunatic. Some poor sod with a ticket.

It has written you onto the Dusk train.

---

## 8. The Dusk train

Tuesday, 12 December. Dusk.

The train is the way out. It always was. *Don't miss the train,* the little book says, rule seven.

You go up to the platform. The bench is empty. Somebody has scrubbed the boards in front of it, and the wet has dried paler than the rest.

The Constable stands at the car step and looks at every face that boards. A drummer. A woman with a basket. A railyard hand with his collar up, whom the Constable stops and turns to the light, and then lets go.

*So will others.*

The others are looked at, every one of them.

There is no fare in your coat. The only fare you know of is in a hatband, and the hatband is with the hat, and the hat is wherever the Constable keeps what is lying around.

You stand at the back of the platform with your hands in your pockets.

You do not move.

The Conductor punches the last fare. The doors go. The train does not wait; it has never waited; the Rules of Carriage say so. The Lamplighter lights the platform post as the last car clears, and the light comes down over the empty bench, and over you.

It goes. You are still here.

---

## 9. Because the Baron wanted it

Wednesday, 13 December, to Thursday, 14 December.

You are still here, and the only thing you know how to do with a morning is be early.

So on Wednesday you go up an hour before the Rush. The bench is there. The lantern is there. The dark end is yours.

You cannot sit at the dark end. It is wrong now. The elbow does not move off it.

You sit at the lantern end, where he sat.

The fog comes onto the glass. You do not write in it.

On Thursday the train comes in with a private car and dogs. The whole platform turns to look at the train, the way everybody looks at the train. You are looking at it too. You hear it and you do not see it. Then there is a hat on the boards, and the Pinkertons have the dogs out, and nobody in the town is looking at anybody.

By noon the platform has it. The Baron is dead. The Baron is dead on the same boards.

And they say the contract was his. All of it. The black wax was the Baron's, and the Baron is dead.

You take out the envelope that evening. You write it slowly. It is the thinnest reason yet, and you know that, and you write it:

*Because the Baron wanted it.*

The hand that sent it is gone. So the wax has stopped. Whatever comes in the bag now is only mail. What you did was the end of it.

The last of it.

---

## 10.

Saturday, 16 December. The Rush.

On the board in the square the Hitman notice has been turned face-in. Someone has written on the back of it.

Ink, from a bottle nearly dry. Small writing, to fit. You do not read it all. You do not know the hand. You read the end:

*The Train comes at the Rush, and the bag comes with it. Check yours.*

You go up the steps. You are early. You take the lantern end.

The fog on the glass. The fog on the boards. Nobody comes up the steps for a long time, and then the train comes.

The bag comes off it.

You follow it across the lane, the way you always have. You stand at the window. The Postmaster sorts. There is someone in front of you.

The Postmaster puts a letter into the hand in front of yours and does not look at it. Black wax. No sender.

You do not look at the face. You look at the hand, which closes on it.

Then it is your turn, and there is nothing for you, and you go back out and up the steps to the bench.

Under the struck lines on the old envelope there is the one you never struck. You find it with your thumb.

*Because it comes for whoever is here.*

The hand from the window is opening it now, somewhere, in a room like yours. Under the line there will be a name. The hand will know it. They will know it because it is someone who is always there, early, in the same place, every morning, in case.

No name. No sender. No reason. Only the bench, and the hour before.

You move your elbow off the dark end for nobody.

It comes for whoever is here. You are here.
