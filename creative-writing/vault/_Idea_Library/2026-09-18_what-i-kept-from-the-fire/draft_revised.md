What I Kept From the Fire

They say I lost the house. I did not lose the house —
I lost the twice-checked lock on a door that no longer has a frame,
I lost the third stair's particular creak, the one that meant *someone's home*,
I lost a lamp left burning for no one, all night, every night, for years.

They say the fire took everything. It took the boxes, not the freight.
It took a detergent-smell folded into a pillowcase like a second skin,
it took the grit of sand that lived in a coat pocket since some forgotten July,
it took the smooth worn dip in the table's edge where an elbow rested through every dinner for a decade.

I did not lose the photo albums. I mean — I did, but I mourn them last.
I did not lose the good china, the drawer of tangled cords, the closet of coats.
I lost a laugh caught mid-word before it could finish,
I lost the rhythm of footsteps that told me who was coming before they arrived,
I lost a ringtone nobody changed because changing it felt like erasing someone.

They say I lost the house. What is a house, if not the sum of what nobody thought to save?

Because now I am still checking a door that isn't there.
Now I am still reaching for a smell that has nowhere left to live.
Now my hand still goes to the table's edge, expecting the worn-smooth dip,
and finds only air, and keeps reaching anyway.

That's the part no inventory lists: I am still keeping.
Not keeping *it* — there is no it, not anymore —
just keeping, the way a hand keeps closing after what it held is gone,
the way a mouth keeps shaping a name after there's no one left to call.

So let the house go. It was only ever the place I kept things in —
it was never the thing I kept.
