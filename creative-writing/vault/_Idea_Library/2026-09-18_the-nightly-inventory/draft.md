The Nightly Inventory

Item one: the light dimmed a second early. Nothing. Logged it anyway.

I've kept this station four seasons now and I know its moods the way you know a spouse's. The lamp warms slow on cold nights, drops early when the bulb's old. I checked the rotation against my watch. One second, maybe less. Filed it under maintenance, next bulb change: October.

I didn't walk up to look at it. I want that on the record, for whatever the record's worth. I sat here and wrote it down instead of climbing the stairs. That was the choice. I didn't know yet it was a choice.

---

Item two. The gull.

Same bird, same rail, every evening since June — I could set the tide table by it. Tonight it won't come down. Circles the light three, four times, screams once, peels off toward the black water and doesn't come back.

Wind's out of the northwest, twelve knots, nothing that should trouble a bird that's ridden worse. I wrote *weather front* because a front was, technically, forecast. Technically is doing a lot of work in that sentence.

The rail sat empty all night. I kept glancing at it between sweeps like it might fill itself back in if I looked often enough. It didn't.

---

Item three. The lamp-room door.

I latch that door myself. I latched it myself tonight, tested it with my shoulder the way I always do. Two hours later it stood open six inches, cold air pushing down the stairwell like something breathing.

Nobody's on this rock but me. I wrote *keeper's own error, re-latch, note for self* because the alternative was a station with a second person in it, and I have provisions for one.

I checked it twice before bed. Then a third time, because twice didn't feel like enough anymore. I wrote the number down. Three. I don't know why the number felt like it mattered, only that it did.

---

Item four.

Found a page tonight, dated nine days back, in my own hand, describing the gull. Details in it I have no memory of gathering — the exact count of its wingbeats before it broke off, thirty-one, written like a man taking inventory of something precious.

I don't remember counting wingbeats. I don't remember writing this at all.

Shelved it as fatigue. Men out here alone start doing things asleep that they'd never do awake — walking, talking, tallying gulls, apparently. *Note to self: sleep more.*

I underlined it. I don't know why I underlined it. There's no one to underline it for.

---

Item five.

I've started counting things. Stair treads — forty-two, always forty-two, except last Tuesday when it was forty-one, and no stair replaces itself. Panes in the lamp room, sixteen, then fifteen, then sixteen again like the room was breathing along with the door. The gap between wave-sets, which any fool knows doesn't hold still, except it had, for years, close enough to trust, and now it doesn't trust me back.

Eye strain, I wrote. A man alone with a lamp burns his eyes counting light.

I've started logging the counts themselves. The stairs, the panes, the sets, dated and timed, so I can check tonight against last night. Which means the log isn't watching the station anymore. It's watching itself. I noticed that and wrote it down too, because apparently that's what I do now with things I notice.

---

Item six.

Found an old logbook in the supply chest behind the spare mantles, leather gone soft with damp, a keeper's hand from — the flyleaf's eaten by mold, I can't get a year. I read four pages before I stopped.

Same incidents. Same order. The gull. The door. A line about wingbeats that isn't mine and is exactly mine, word for word, down to the underline.

I didn't radio it in. Reception's bad this time of year, that's true and also not the reason. The reason is that saying it out loud to someone on the mainland would make it belong to more than just me, and some part of me — a part that used to be caution and isn't anymore — wants to keep it small. Wants to keep it mine.

I burned the old book in the stove. Not this one. I told myself that meant something.

---

Item seven.

There are lines in this log now that I didn't write. Or that I did, in a hand almost mine — the loop on the g a little wrong, the pressure a little wrong — in the gap between when I banked the fire and when I woke at the lamp with no memory of climbing the stairs.

I check the log more than I check the light now. That's the job, I tell myself. Keeping the record straight is the job. I know that isn't true. I write it anyway.

I no longer know which of these entries are mine. I'm not sure "mine" is a shelf big enough to sort them onto anymore.

---

Item eight.

Tonight I decided to end it. Burn the log the way I burned the other one, watch the whole account go to ash in the stove and be done being counted.

I opened to the last page to tear it free first, and there was already an entry there. Past tense. Describing the burning — my hand at the stove door, the pages catching, the smell of it — written before I had done any of it, in the hand that is almost mine.

There's no shelf for this. No maintenance line, no fatigue, no eye strain wide enough to hold it. I am not adding a number to what comes after this. The list has stopped asking me for one.

---

The boat comes at the changeover like it always does. The new keeper is young, unbothered by rock or wind, glad of the work. The station is found in order — lamp turning, stores stocked, log squared on the desk like a man left it mid-thought and only stepped out for air.

Nobody asks where the old keeper's gone. There's no old keeper to point at not being there. There's a coat on a hook that fits no one currently on the rock, and a chair with a seat gone the exact shape of a man who sat in it a great many nights, and nobody remarks on either, because there is nothing about a coat or a chair that asks to be remarked on.

The new keeper sits down at the desk that first night, pulls the log close, and opens to a blank page to begin the way every keeper begins — a small entry, something plain, to get the hand moving.

Except the page isn't blank. There's a line already on it, dated tonight, in a hand that isn't the new keeper's and will never once be suspected of being anyone else's.

The new keeper reads it, nods once — sounds right, sounds like something worth having on the record — and copies it down underneath as their own first observation, because that's simply what you do with a small, true thing you noticed yourself.
