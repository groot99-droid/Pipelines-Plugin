Wikilink back to [[07_Poems_and_Prose/04_When_You_Know_to_Much|When You Know to Much]].

The poem opens in a register of self-doubt about literary ambition — comparing itself unfavorably to "Shakespeare or King Arthur" — before a hard pivot announced by "Some may think I'm crying over a child... But no— know that I weep for someone else. Myself," which converts a meditation on childhood and myth into something much angrier and more personal about being diminished by others. The back half piles up mixed, almost incantatory metaphors — "spit gold that tastes like ash," "build mountains from the wreckage," "I'm already falling, and I've learned how to fly on the way down" — that don't resolve into one consistent image system but accumulate into a mood of defiant vanity, which the poem names as its own subject ("I can be vain. And vanity... is the one thing the destroyed understand perfectly"). The title's deliberately uncorrected typo ("to Much" for "too much") reads as intentional roughness consistent with the poem's thesis that its words don't need to be great, "They're mine" — the closing line makes authenticity, not polish, the standard by which the piece wants to be judged.
