Wikilink back to [[07_Poems_and_Prose/06_Was_I_Good_Enough|Was I Good Enough]].

The poem's most distinctive device is splitting the speaker into three co-existing selves — "I," "myself," and an implied third witness — who argue, cook breakfast, and "slam a door only I can hear" inside one body; it's an unusually literal dramatization of internal conflict that lets the poem show disagreement with itself without resorting to therapy-speak. Small sensory anchors (sun on hands, a stupid coincidence that causes disproportionate laughter, morning air) recur every few stanzas as mood checkpoints, tracking incremental repair against the backdrop of specific family wounds named directly and without softening: "how my dad's absence filled the cabinets / but emptied the room, / how my brother's anger cracked the walls." The poem explicitly rejects therapeutic cliché ("Not in a self-help, / 'love your flaws' kind of way... the watered-down guru garbage") right before earning the same territory sincerely, which is a knowing, self-aware move. It ends not on a declaration of self-love but on a redefinition of love itself — choosing yourself "every single time" instead of grand declarations — which quietly answers the title's question without ever directly saying yes.
