Wikilink back to [[07_Poems_and_Prose/28_Will_o|Will-o']].

The bog-wisp's game rule (it lures, and it blinds a light for five seconds) becomes the length of a man's doubt. Fennick has kept a light on a pole at the fen's edge since the lake drained, for nobody, unpaid, and his confession is organised around the one interval in which he cannot vouch for it. Section I asks *which is mine*; Section III is titled *Five*, and the five seconds of a blinded lantern stretch across the whole poem, the time in which a walker on the fen could have followed the wrong light, and his.

Cyril's drowning comes in through Cyril's two whistled notes for Nora, which *Exhibits A to R* also gives him, so the victim keeps the life the register gave him. Fennick goes over it three ways and settles none: the wisps, a hand, or his own light gone out for five. Cyril's open eyes *turned toward the pole* are Fennick's guilty reading. The poem presents them as how he sees it, and the reader gets no fact to correct it with. The Beast and the Killer stay out of it; the only thing on the fen with intent is the lights, and the poem never says what they are. The last line keeps the doubt and turns it on himself: *Every light on the fen is a promise, and the fen keeps every one but mine.*
