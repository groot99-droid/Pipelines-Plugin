The bestiary as a natural history written by someone whose own work was always signed by another name. Calloway drew her father's plates for twenty years, and the form she finally owns is the most impersonal one there is: Range, Habit, Sign, What it fears, What the town says. The headings do the wave's canon work in one place. Wolves run you for six seconds and go home, keep the edge of the light and never cross it, leave fur and claw marks and no kept footprint; the boar wounds and never kills; the bat and rat wound below ground. Every entry agrees with the Codex Bestiary's lines and Dreads, and *What the town says* is where the superstition lives, kept apart from what she saw.

The dated addenda break the form. Owen, a stranger walking the ties, is entered under the wolf with his life intact, and the specimen wolf of 4 November is a death she paid a society fee to watch. The boar's tusk on 19 October changes the hand that writes: Plate III stops at the boar's shoulder, and every plate after it is *Drawn left-handed*, and the reader reads the injury in the margins before the text admits it. The two pasted slips (Grounds 99 in 1952, typed; the island in 1788, in French ink) use the device of Ledger Entries XVII and XVIII: the guide is older than one and younger than the other, and the three eras share one bestiary without anyone explaining how. The Beast's plate is the piece's centre and its refusal, an empty ruled frame with *Not drawn. Not seen.* in the left hand. The Codex said nothing more would be written; the guide keeps that promise by drawing the frame. It ends on the fare east, folded, and *Dread: the platform at dusk*.
