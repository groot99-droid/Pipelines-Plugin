Introduction

	…I stood upon the edge of the abyss. The cold bit at my skin as the dark mist swelled around me and overhead. I stared into the darkness, straining my eyes to glimpse something… anything. I stayed motionless for some time, praying that I might see some sign of hope, but no. For in the great darkness, no light can be found…

	Books lined the shelves. They covered the floors and tables. Some could even be found in the pantries alongside outlandish fares like goblin's eyes or Ogers’ tongue, all kept bobbing and floating neatly in sealed jars for later consumption. The sun peered in through an open window, and off in the distance, a peculiar cuckoo clock cooed. As the sunbeams stretched across the floor like growing vines of light, they began to touch upon the face of one bizarre creature. Eyes fast shut, holding back golden orbs ornately chiseled by deep brown irises and large, seamlessly bottomless pupils. His pale green skin glistened like sea froth at the first peak of the morning, contrasted by the dark shadows of his room. His limbs, elegant and elongated, lay sprawled about the bed as he wiped his eyes. As he rose, his frame towered above the furnishings in his room; though thin, he stood at almost 8 feet in stature. However, he may not be easily defined by his features or flowing dark locks. His ears quite quickly gave him away as they rose to a point at the top. He was an elf and, by the looks of it, a scholar, for he was draped in monk’s attire. Quickly, he readied himself and left the room, striding into the halls of the endless temple. This was no ordinary temple, nor was it meant for some form of worship to a higher power or home to great beings. This temple was created to protect and oversee the countless worlds safely stowed within, for every book was alive, and inside of it, an entire universe. Each microcosm had its own stories, myths, legends, and even microcosms of its own. This elf, who goes by the name “Elaris Sel Marden,” or for short Elaris, was prized with the duty to oversee and protect the temple’s contents and ensure that no ill fate becomes the worlds that lie within. Chapter One: The Never-Ending Dunes and The Wondering Xandrixes

	Elaris thumbed through a series of books as he savored a bit of roast sea lizard. “Since I am enshrined in this place for eternity, I might as well come to love what I am protecting,” he mumbled with a full mouth. Elaris picked up a plain-looking book with sand on the cover and sifting out through its pages. He turned it over to find the name, but there wasn’t one. “ Well, I guess since this one doesn’t have a name, I’ll just have to read this one and find out what to call it,” he thought to himself. He decided he would leave the book on the table next to a chair and find a cup of tea to enjoy while he was reading. Elaris was trying to make the best of his situation. He had not chosen to become a scholar for the rest of his life, but things had happened that had made this his permanent occupation. He was never to leave, and he could only be replaced when he had died. A small part of him wished not to be here, but this was an honor and a much better outcome than he deserved. In the pantry, Elaris searched for what he thought to be the most suitable tea to go with his morning readings. He pushed aside one last container until he found the one he sought. It was mint herbal tea with a unique herb in it that made scholars more vividly imagine, if not envision, the book they were reading so clearly it was as if they were the narrator of the book. Now sitting in his chair and sipping his tea, he opened the book and began to read.  
		“The desert seems to extend on into eternity. Once, a great explorer named Xandri traveled further than any man had ever traveled, but he still never found the end and died in his last attempt. Not many had ever shown that kind of bravery since.” The storyteller finished and asked if there were any questions. My hand shot up almost by instinct. “What about the Xandrixes? They seem to reside in the desert without a problem. What if someone were to ride one to the end?” I questioned—the room filled with sharp gasps. The next thing I felt was a boxing to the ear.  
	My ear rang loudly, and I could feel the blood pulsing to it—one of the shamans had hit me with his cane. “We do not ride on the backs of the gods. You will be punished if we hear much more nonsense from you, Xandri.” The head shaman, Xadra, warned. I was pulled out of the crowd and taken somewhere more secluded. Another one of the shamans gave the same speech I always get. It always leads back to how I’m the son of the great explorer and should be a better example to my peers who needed someone to look up to, but I didn’t care to be the babysitter for my tribe; I wanted to explore as my father did and none of these people could stop me. I came back to reality after zoning out as I often did. The last thing I heard, and the only thing I heard, was, “I expected a better offspring from someone as great as your father.” I rolled my eyes and wondered what it must have been like to be my father. He had the same experiences I had with the tribes-people. They all held him back, keeping him from his potential until he proved himself. I had to prove myself.  
	Later that night, Xandri lay awake in his room. He stared at the ceiling and thought in silence until, finally, he had come to a resolve. He would traverse the desert and not return until he found the end. He jumped out of his bed and grabbed his pack covered in holes and bits of scant patchwork that was coming apart at the seams. His father, though significant, didn’t leave him much. They were poor, and his father’s death had left him poorer. His mother died in childbearing; he was lucky he hadn’t died with her. After packing, he walked to the mirror and cleaned himself with water from a pitcher. His skin was dark from many years under the sun and full of scales. His clan were descendants of lizards that had lived in the desert. It was said that the Xandrixes had blessed them with their human form so they may worship them and that they would turn them back to lizards if they stopped. Xandri, like his father, struggled to believe that genuinely divine creatures would be so callous. 
	As Xandri walked away from the little hut he had, he imagined that the tribe would be sorrowful with his departure, but he knew this was wishful thinking. After all, he wouldn’t be missed. He had caused more harm than good, constantly being threatened with banishment from the tribe or public flogging. That would be one thing he would never miss: all the rules. They had rules for everything, and it was stifling. Xandri pushed those thoughts to the back of his mind. It was time to focus on better things. With the sky above him and the moon lighting his way, he finally was hopeful. The moon is shown down on the dunes in a beautiful array. It was a sight to behold. The sand looked like shining sheets of silver dotted with splendid purple vines sprawled across the ground like thick webs. Small prickle bushes were scattered across the sand sea. The sight was mesmerizing. He didn’t understand why everyone feared the desert. Then he felt something. It was like a small earthquake running through the ground like a torrent wave. The sand washed forward like a crashing wave of water. He braced himself as the surge of sand swallowed him. 
	Clouds of sand blurred Xandri’s vision as he surfaced. His reptilian features helped him to traverse the desert, and things like this were not uncommon nor unprepared for. Often, great currents of wind would blow large waves of sand across the sand sea, which was the reason for the name. It was still the dead of night and getting colder as clouds washed over the night sky. He decided to sleep in the sand and use its embrace like a blanket to keep in the heat.  
	He woke to the sun beating down upon his head. He felt blistered and parched. The sun had given him a headache. He dug through his pack to find something to cover his head. He now began the journey again. The sand was torrential now. Waves washed to all corners of his vision. If he was not careful, he could be swallowed too deep to dig out and die. He realized why so many people had been afraid to traverse the desert. Of course, he knew a simple secret his father had taught: he had a boy. The sand of this particular desert moved in mysterious ways, and no one knew why, but his father knew how to use it to his advantage. Alongside the vines that snaked across the desert were large woody leaves that looked like the ears of the Zorlop. These leaves could be fashioned to one’s feet and then used to walk upon the waves without fear of getting pulled under. He then tied the leaves about his feet and began his journey again.
