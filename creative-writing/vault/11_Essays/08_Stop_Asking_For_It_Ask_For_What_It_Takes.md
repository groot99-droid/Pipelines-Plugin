Stop Asking For It, Ask For What It Takes

You hear quotes like that all the time, so much so they lose meaning and just become another idiom to pull off the shelf to try and sound less like an idiot when you're in over your head. Here’s the thing though: one day, that quote stuck with me in a way I couldn’t understand until it happened. Some might say it was like all the puzzle pieces coming together after a seemingly infinite lifetime of staring at them, trying to force them to fit whichever way they would go. I had no idea the puzzle was something I knew all along.

I’ll stop rambling and get to the point. In high school, I got assigned a “career and lifestyle presentation.” I procrastinated, as I usually did then and still fall prey to now. The night before, I word-dumped over 3,000 words in a few hours, cut it back to 1,000, and then added the flourishes. In short, within a few hours I had a good paper with very little original content. I chose the lifestyle because of the city I wanted to live in. I chose the city because of the college. I chose the college because of the degree, and I chose the degree because of a hobby. I had that hobby because of a passion I felt—a dream that one day I would be a game designer, which was really just because, deep down, I love all forms of creativity.

Long story short, I read that paper in front of my peers, got the grade, and moved on. I never thought about it again.

That is, until now. At 20, I’ve built my first indie game. Just me and AI. I didn’t get that lifestyle, I didn’t go to that college, and yeah, maybe it was a disappointment when those pieces fell apart on their own. But now that time has passed, I see the picture clearly. I didn’t want all of that circumstantial stuff. What I really wanted was to try my luck at designing a game, to find the place where the creativity clicks and every choice that once felt like a burden becomes a work of passion, because the end result fuels that fire.

I asked life for everything I thought was required to achieve this very small goal. I inflated it, hedged the skills behind a paywall of time, blood, sweat, tears, and money. We often come to life asking for simple skills that only require defeating the fear of doing something new, and we replace that with a 10+ step checklist with variable plans for different faults. We turn life into rocket science, when all rocket science really is, is blowing something so high into the sky it escapes gravity and floats to freedom.

That’s what I’m trying to teach the reader, and myself, day to day. The shackles which refute our ascent are nothing more than the constraints the voices of our doubt have enchained us with. So stop asking for it, and ask what it actually takes.

What it actually takes is usually startlingly simple, though rarely easy. It means stripping away the excuses we disguise as prerequisites.

When I finally sat down to build that game, I didn't wait for permission or the perfect set of circumstances. I didn't wait for a lecture hall to teach me how to structure a narrative. I just started mapping out a 2.5D retro low-poly world. I started drafting the lore, figuring out the social deduction mechanics, and setting up the workflows locally. I realized that breathing life into a project isn't magic—it's just a series of deliberate, unglamorous actions.

We get so caught up in the polished end-result that we refuse to touch the raw materials. But real creation is visceral and heavy. It’s like standing over a massive wood canvas, mixing sand, concrete, and heavy impasto, and physically pulling the layers apart just to force a texture into existence. It's messy, it weighs a ton, and it takes time to cure. If you wait until you have a pristine studio and a masterclass in classical technique to make your first mark, the canvas will stay blank forever.

The same principle applies to everything. If you want to build physical endurance, you don't need a state-of-the-art facility and a personalized kinesiology breakdown. You strap thirty pounds of dead weight to your chest and you start walking miles into the woods until your legs burn and the trail goes quiet. You figure out the mechanics of the terrain by walking it.

This book is about that terrain. It’s about dismantling the massive, intimidating checklists we build to protect ourselves from the vulnerability of trying. It's about looking at the things you want to create and realizing that the only thing standing between you and the finish line isn't a lack of resources, but an unwillingness to just start mixing the concrete. We are going to look at how to stop planning for the life you think you need to do the work, and start doing the work with whatever is right in front of you.

This brings me to my next point: friendship. Let’s be honest, many of us would do all manner of things for a friend that we would never dream of doing for ourselves. We’d call it selfish if we took an entire day to clean the house, cook a massive meal, and clear our evening just to make time to feel important and seen. You might argue, “Okay, but isn’t that just a spa day?” Sure, but compare the guilt of an isolated spa day to the effortless joy of inviting a friend over for dinner. Think about when a friend asks you to stay out late or wake up before dawn. We will throw out our entire schedule, meticulously set our environment, and burn through a biologically insane amount of energy just to perform empathy, maintain concentration, and manage stimuli for someone else.

Fundamentally, friendship is an anomaly. But that’s not exactly the point. The point is to ask what human life would be if our biological systems regulated us so heavily that we only experienced positive benefits from the will to communicate and invest in other people. Why don’t we see that the first step to having the best relationships possible—and doing the best work possible—is putting in that same effort for yourself?

If your best friend came to you tomorrow and said they wanted to build an entire world from scratch, write a massive narrative, or start walking ten miles a day with a weighted vest, you wouldn't hand them a list of impossible prerequisites. You wouldn't demand they go get a degree first, or move to a different city, or wait until they had a flawless life setup. You would tell them to start. You’d stay up with them until three in the morning brainstorming mechanics, or you'd lace up your boots and walk the trail right beside them. You would be their fiercest advocate.

Yet, when it comes to our own ambitions, we become our own worst gatekeepers. We deny ourselves the time, the focus, and the grace we so freely hand out to everyone else. We treat our own creative survival like an afterthought, squeezing it into the exhausted margins of the day only after we’ve satisfied every external obligation.

Asking for what it takes means you have to be willing to advocate for your own goals with the exact same ferocious loyalty you give to a friend. You have to be willing to clear your own schedule. You have to be willing to utilize your own biologically insane amounts of energy to sit down, tune out the noise, and do the heavy lifting your passion requires. You cannot be a secondary character in your own life and expect a lead-role outcome. You have to stop treating your own ambition like an imposition, and start treating it like the most important guest at the dinner table.
