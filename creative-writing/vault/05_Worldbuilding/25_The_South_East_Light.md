# The South-East Light

**THORNE & EXPANSE LINE**
**SOUTH-EAST LIGHT · MARSH EDGE**
**KEEPER'S LOG**

*To be returned to the Company by the Train.*

Keeper: *Abel Penhallow.*

---

## Rules for the Light

*Printed inside the cover.*

1. The light is lit at Dusk and put out at Day. Dusk and Day are kept by the Train. On Sunday, by the keeper's watch.
2. The keeper logs what the light shows. The log is returned to the Company by the Train.
3. Oil is issued by the gallon and docked by the gallon.
4. The keeper does not leave the light between Dusk and Day.

*Folded inside the cover: the order of appointment, one sheet, under a red Company seal. It is signed for the Company in a hand I do not know. The name under the signature has been ruled through, neatly, with a rule. Whoever did that owns a rule.*

---

## Head note

My trade was lamps. Eleven years in a signal box at a junction back east, until the Panic shut the junction and the box with it. A signalman learns one thing before any other. You log what you saw and nothing you did not, because a man who logs what he did not see is how two trains meet. I took this post because it was a lamp and it was paid. I have not been told more than that, and I have not asked.

I came out in November by the ferry. The boy who poles it is Tuck. He brought the post in sections and the lamp in a crate, and the crate came up the Line with the Company's chalk on it and the lid nailed. The Company counts the crate. I counted what was in it.

The light stands on the south-east edge of the mere. A Company post, iron, with rungs, and a lamp at the top in a glass house the size of a bread oven. It throws a steady light. It does not go round. Tuck says there is a light out on the island that goes round, sweeping, all night. He says it the way he says the weather. I have written it down because he said it, and that is all I know of it.

West across the mere, at the far edge, there is another steady light. That one is old. Tuck says it is Fennick's and has been Fennick's since there was a lake. The Company calls mine a light. Tuck says the town will call it Fennick's other one. I expect the town is right. Towns usually are, about names.

Tuck says the town's paper allows one kind of light after the train has gone. "Only the Lamplighter's lanterns may be lit." I asked him whether mine is one of hers. He said it is the Company's. I take that as the same answer.

I have never been into the town. I know it by its lights. That is what this log is for.

---

## The post and the hut

The Company built a hut of field stone beside the post, one room, with the door to the water. I give it in the form the junction taught me: what is kept there, and what can be seen from it.

**The post.**
*Kept:* the lamp, the glass, the trimming scissors on a string. Fourteen rungs. My knees count them for me on the way down.
*Seen from it:* everything in this book.

**The table at the window.**
*Kept:* this log, the pen, the ink in a cup so the frost does not split the bottle. A drawer.
*Seen from it:* the cottage across the south water, on the shore below an alley's mouth. One window.

**The stove corner.**
*Kept:* the stove, the cot, my watch on a nail where it can be read from the cot. The watch was set by the train in November. It has been right since, because I wind it.
*Seen from it:* nothing. The window is on the other wall. I chose it so.

**The oil corner.**
*Kept:* the Company's oil, by the gallon, in its cans. On the wall above them, a tally in chalk: a stroke for every can come, a cross for every can burned.
*Seen from it:* the tally.

---

## What the light shows

*Written the first night, and kept to.*

- **Six lights, lawful, after the train goes.** They always come on in the same order. First, one low and alone, by the rails. Then three close together on what must be a square. Then a wait, while whoever carries the flame goes somewhere I cannot see. Then one up a rise, at what may be a gate. Last, a fourth on the square, after the others, and after the wait. It is always last. I do not know why. I do not need to.
- **Lights that are not the Company's.** A window high in one house, burning green. A candle at an upstairs glass that does not move. A lit window near the square, past the hour, most nights. I enter them as *not lawful, not mine to report*. I do not report them.
- **Fennick's, out west.** Steady, at the same height every night. On Saturdays it stands unattended for an hour. Tuck says the old man goes down to fill his can.
- **Low lights over the fen that are not steady.** They are never twice in one place. Entered as *unknown*.
- **The cottage.** One window lamp, lit at the low-water hour. Someone stands in it with a paper and looks at the water, then at the paper.
- **The light at the boards.** Below the cottage, across the south water, at the hill's foot, there is a black mouth with boards across it, low on the shingle. A lantern hangs there. It was lit at dusk on my first night, the same dusk as mine. Tuck calls the mouth the sea door. There is no sea. There is the mere, and it keeps a tide, and nobody here will say why.

> *The first two nights were ruled through in the Company's ink before the book came out to me. The Company began its count on Saturday. I began mine on Monday. I note that the Company's page and mine do not start together.*

> *Monday. Tuck at Day with the issue and a loaf. A gull came out over the fen with the ferry and stood on the hut roof when he went. It is there now.*
> *At Dusk the train's lamp went up the line into the pines. I climbed. "Clear." I said it to nobody. I said it eleven years to nobody in the box, and I am too old to stop.*
> *From the cottage side, after dark, two notes whistled. High, then low. Then a wait, as long as it takes to answer. Then the same two again. Nobody answered them. I logged it as a signal sent with no reply.*

> *Tuesday. The box for the boards is printed on this night and not on the one before. I looked at the next page. Second and fourth, every page. I do not know how a printer back east knows the nights the water will go down at a mere he has never seen. I rule nothing. I fill in the box.*
> *Wind off the ridge from noon. At Dusk the water stood up against it and did not go down. Boards under. I wrote No in the box, the first time the box has been wrong, and that was the wind's doing, not the printer's.*
> *In the gusts the town's lights went out one at a time. The rise first. Then two on the square. Then the one by the rails. Fennick's held. The boards held, low down, under the hill. Mine held. For most of an hour those were the only steady lights I could see, and mine was the only one on the Company's paper. I have written it down because it was so. It means nothing to me.*
> *The cottage window stayed lit through it.*
> *The two notes. Nobody.*

> *Wednesday. Tuck at Day. I asked him about the wind. He said the coach road is shut below the ridge, and from now on the train is the only road. He said nothing else, and poled back.*
> *At night the crows went up off the roofs over the square, all at once, black against the lit fog, and settled again. Tuck told me in November that they go up when somebody runs under them. Somebody ran. That is the whole entry.*

> *Thursday. At Day, when I came down the rungs, the light at the boards was not there. I did not see it put out. It was burning at the last hour I logged, and at Day it was gone, and the boards were black. I log the fact.*
> *At Dusk it was lit again, in the same place.*
> *Low water at the hour. Boards clear, as printed. My light reaches them across the water when the water is down. Boards whole. Spiked. Shut.*
> *Late, the lamp went dim over my head with oil in it and my hand on the table. I counted on the watch. Five. Then it came back. I had not touched it. Out west, at the same five, Fennick's went too, and came back too. Two lamps, a mile apart, and the same five. I logged both.*

> *Friday. Tuck at Day. A second gull came with him and stayed with the first. I asked him why they come out and do not go back. He said they follow the pole, not the boat. I have not seen one leave the roof.*
> *He said it was rent day in town. He did not say it as news.*

> *Saturday. Tuck at Day with the last of the issue, and the wire off the station window, which he made me read back to him because he cannot read it himself:*
> *LINE CLOSED FROM 24TH STOP.*
> *So no train after tonight's, and no oil after this.*
> *Tally on the wall: five nights at full flame, and a little over.*
> *Snow by afternoon. At Dusk the train went up the line into the pines with its windows lit. I could not count who was in them. A lit window is not a person, and I log lights.*
> *Saturday, so Fennick's stood alone for its hour.*
> *Low water late. Boards clear, as printed. At the hour, lanterns came down onto the shingle under the cottage, one after another, and gathered at the boards around the light that hangs there. They stood close together for a long while, and then they went in past it, and for a long while there was only the one light at the boards. When they came out they came at a run. Fewer went back up the shingle than came down, and closer together.*
> *I have written only what the light showed. I did not count them. I know how it reads.*
> *The two notes. Nobody.*

> *Sunday. No Sunday train in any week, so the rule gave me my own watch for the day, and I took it. At Dusk I lit by the watch.*

> *Monday. At first light: no train. Rule 1 says the light goes out at Day, and Day is kept by the Train. There is no Train. By the Company's paper it is still night, and it will be night until the Company sends a Day up the Line. To keep the rule I should burn day and night until the oil is gone, and then the light goes out for good, lawfully.*
> *I put it out at first light by my watch. I wrote "Against Rule 1" in the column, in full, so that nobody reading this will think I did not know.*
> *The Articles of the Line, which Tuck has by heart from hearing them read at the tavern: "Every clock within the Grant shall be set by the Train." Mine is set by me. I am within the Grant. I log it.*
> *Flame to half from tonight. The tally will not carry full.*
> *Christmas, by the calendar. I remembered at noon. Nobody came.*
> *Low water. Boards clear, as printed. Late, a small light came down from the cottage, carried low, a child's height or a stooping one. It went along the shingle to the post at the water's edge below the cottage. It stopped there for the length of a breath. Then it was put out.*
> *I did not see it again, and I did not see it go back up. A lantern, not lawful, put out. I do not know whose, and I have not written whose.*
> *The window lamp in the cottage burned until Day.*

> *Tuesday. The six came on later than they have. Whoever carries the flame had no train to start from either. Same order: the rails, three on the square, the wait, the rise, the fourth on the square last.*

> *Wednesday. Low water. Boards clear, as printed.*
> *Five lights, not six. The rails. Three on the square. The rise. Then the wait, and after the wait nothing. The fourth on the square did not come on. I waited for it until the cottage lamp was lit, and then I wrote Five.*
> *The light at the boards burned high tonight, higher than I have seen it. Whoever trims it turned it all the way up.*
> *On the rise, lanterns in pairs, walking, with dogs barking under them. I counted the pairs and the stray ones until they were behind the houses. The crows did not go up. They do not go up for men who walk.*
> *The two notes. Nobody.*

> *Thursday. Six again. The fourth on the square came on last, after the wait, as before. I do not know why it was dark last night, and I do not know why it is lit tonight.*
> *The loaf was finished at noon. I broke the heel for the gulls, and something hard went against my thumb: a crumb of wax, pressed with a mark, baked into the bread. Tuck brought that loaf on Saturday and said nothing about it. I do not know the custom here. I have not thrown it away. It is in the table drawer. It looks like it was meant for someone.*

> *Friday. At Day, fog. Up the line, beyond the town, a smudge of light, yellow, low, moving slowly from the east. Then it stopped, about where the first light by the rails stands.*
> *I wrote "the Train?" in the column. It is the only question mark in this book. I am not sure enough to leave it off.*
> *I put the lamp out at that Day and wrote "By the Train", the first time since Saturday that the column and the rule agree.*
> *Tuck did not come. No oil, no word, no bread.*
> *At Dusk I could not see whether the smudge went back into the pines. I did not see it go. I lit by my watch.*
> *Low water. Boards clear, as printed. Nothing at them but their own light.*

> *Saturday. A light I have no column for.*
> *After Dusk, over the town: white, standing straight up out of the ground into the cloud. No fire under it that I could see, no smoke, no flicker. It did not move and it did not spread. It stood.*
> *I wrote "One" in the column, because it was one light, and I log lights. I have written nothing in the Remarks for it. I do not have a word the Company would accept, and I will not log a word I do not have.*

> *Saturday, so Fennick's stood alone for its hour, under it.*
