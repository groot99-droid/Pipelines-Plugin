# The Last Shift's Tally Board

## 1. Custody

Clean slate each shift.

That was the rule. It was painted along the top of the frame in white letters gone yellow, where nobody looking at the board could miss it. Foreman Hask read it out anyway. Hask read everything out.

"Clean slate each shift," he said. "Down on the left. Up on the right. Sign."

Monday, 6 October 1952. The ore chamber of the Blackpine Workings. A slate in a timber frame, nailed to the rock. A row of nails under it for the brass lamp checks. A bucket, a rag, a stub of chalk, a custody chit.

"Who kept it before?"

"Transferred," Hask said. "Sign."

Ned Corwin had been a payroll clerk at a foundry in Pittsburgh for eleven years. Then he signed a union petition, and in the camp's words he became an enemy of the state, and in the Workings crew's words he became Four. Hask had looked down the crew's numbers that morning and stopped at his. *You. Four. You were a clerk.*

A keeper does not go down. He knew that before he knew anything else about the job. He signed with his number.

The DOWN column was not empty.

High up, above where any of the crew would be chalked, was one entry in older chalk and a rounder hand than any hand in the crew:

`A_T_N  K_SS__R`

It was not struck through. It had never come up.

He did the sum on his trouser seam with his thumbnail, the way he had done sums on the foundry's ledgers when the adding machine jammed. Nine men down. Nine men up. And one.

"From today," Hask said, "the count is yours to close."

It would not close. He knew that before the first shift went down.

## 2. Short count

Every shift came out the same. Nine men down and nine men up, all of them struck through. And the old name, which was not.

Residual one.

The camp had a rule for that, and the rule had a rhyme, which is how you knew it was old. Short count, short ration. Hask read it out at the bread line on Wednesday with the sheet held up to the lamp.

"Workings. Residual one. Keeper four."

Ten men. Nine loaves.

"Who's the one?"

"Nobody. A name."

"A name's eating my bread?"

"Your keeper's count is."

He did not answer them. He kept his daughter's arithmetic folded in the lining of his cap: long division, forty-seven by five. She had written *9 R 2* at the bottom, very carefully, and he had taught her that the remainder is not a mistake. It is only what is left over. Under it she had written one line of her own, and the camp censor had put a black bar through it before the letter reached him. He never found out what she had written.

Residual one, every shift, and Hask's pencil going down the sheet.

The crew ate short for his count, and every man in the crew knew whose count it was.

## 3. Washed out

On Sunday, 12 October, Hask came down with a paper folded once.

"Office asked about your name."

"It isn't mine."

"Office asked. Office got an answer." He read it. He read everything. "Do not carry."

So Four read the frame again. *Clean slate each shift.* He read it the way a clerk reads a rule, looking for the word that gives permission. It was there. *Clean.*

He filled the bucket at the drip. He wet the rag. The slate went black and shining under it, then slowly back to grey. The rounder hand came off the way any chalk comes off. There was nothing in it that held on.

Old chalk in the grain, washed out. That was all it had ever been.

Monday's sheet: down nine, up nine, residual nought. The bread came back.

Tadeusz Wrona, Seven, a coal man out of Silesia by way of Scranton, hummed through his teeth at the bread line. He had been carving a bird whistle out of a pine offcut since the first week, a little more every night. That night it had a mouthpiece. He blew one note at Four across the table, for the bread. It was flat, and everybody laughed.

## 4. Payroll Friday

The payroll train whistled in the yard at noon. Up in the camp men were standing in a line for scrip. Down here Four chalked the shift down: one, two, three, all of them, and 7 for Wrona, who went by humming toward the old stope with the whistle in his shirt pocket.

At the end of the shift they came up. He struck them through one at a time. Eight.

He waited for the ninth.

He looked at the board.

Seven was not on it. He had chalked it himself that morning, and now there was no 7, struck or unstruck, anywhere on the slate. In the place where his chalk had been, in the rounder hand, was the old name.

He had washed it out. Old chalk, washed out. He had watched the slate go grey.

Residual one. The same as the first week. A count the office already knew, and already docked bread for, and had already told him not to carry.

Nothing on the board showed that a man was missing.

The board balanced the way it had balanced the first week: one below, and the one was not Seven.

## 5. Transferred

On Monday, 20 October, he went up to the office in his shift clothes and reported a man missing.

Hask turned the Friday sheet round on the desk so Four could read his own hand.

"Down nine. Up eight. Residual one. Keeper four."

"Seven went down."

"Residual's your name."

"It isn't my name. Seven went down. I chalked him."

"Not on your board." Hask tapped the sheet. "Board doesn't show him."

The office could not log a missing man the board did not show. There was no box on any form for it. Four had kept a payroll's books for eleven years, and he understood that before Hask said another word. Hask took his pen and wrote, in the office's hand, in the Disposition field against slot 7:

*Transferred.*

There was a requisition on the spike by Hask's elbow, and Four read it upside down because he was a clerk: LAMP OIL, DEEP LEVEL REFUGE, 1 QT, WEEKLY. He did not know why the Deep Level needed a lamp. He did not ask.

Walking back down the adit, he worked it out. He had been tired. He had chalked a wrong number, or washed one he should have kept. The office had corrected him. That was what offices were for.

On Tuesday a new man stood at the board. He had a pencil behind his ear and slot 7's new billet number on his shirt, and he counted the nails under his breath in German, *eins, zwei, drei*, the way a man counts in the language he first learned to count in.

"Which nail is mine?"

## 6. The check

At the end of October the winze boards came off twice for something the office called a recovery detail. A man with a clipboard took a number down each time. Neither went on the board. The detail officer kept his own count, and it was not the keeper's count. Four told himself that, and kept his own.

On Wednesday, 5 November, the last of the shift came up, and he struck them through, and he went to hang the rag on its nail.

Wrona's brass lamp check was hanging beside the old name.

A brass disc stamped 7. It was wet, and cold enough to sting, and there was a ring of water on the floor under it the colour of the stope water, as if it had been carried up from there and hung, and had dripped. Caught round the nail with it was a thread of something white that he took for string until he touched it. It was skin. A long curl of it, soft as wet paper, the whorls of a fingertip still on it.

Transferred men take their checks with them. They hand them in at the gate with their boots and their spoons. That was the rule, and he had seen it done.

He went down the winze gallery with his own lamp, as far as the boards over the ladder shaft, and put his eye to the gap between two of them.

A long way down there was a light: small and yellow, steady, in the refuge, where nobody had been sent that day. One quart, weekly. Somebody carried it down.

Under the light, the water. Black, flat, and then not flat. It rose a finger's width up the ladder and went back, slow, the way a chest goes. The crew called it the Mother's water. He had thought that was a joke.

At its edge something sat with its head on one side, the way Wrona sat at the bread line.

White. Not pale: white, like bread left in the rain. The skin drawn so tight over the skull that the teeth showed through the cheeks. The ribs standing out like the rungs of the ladder. The fingers gone soft and long, the nails gone, the ends of them split. The eyes open under a skin of water that did not run off.

It was not breathing.

He counted on his trouser seam. Sixty. A hundred. A hundred and eighty.

At two hundred and twelve the chest let go, all at once, a long wet sigh that came up the shaft to him. Then the mouth closed and the count began again.

It was humming. No tune. Just the hum, through the teeth, the way Wrona had hummed when he was carving.

He stood there until his lamp needed trimming. Then he went back and put Wrona's check in his own pocket, and did not hang the rag.

Transferred men take their checks with them, and Seven had not.

## 7. The trade

He did it on his trouser seam, with his thumbnail, the way he did every sum.

He washed the name on the twelfth. On the seventeenth the board had Seven instead, and the name came back. The board did not keep a residual. It kept a man below. If the old name was not the man, the board found one.

Wash the name, and the board takes a man to balance it.

So he left it up.

Residual one, shift after shift. Short count, short ration. On Tuesday, 18 November, the seam dust went up in the far heading, and the adit was shut for forty hours, and the office carried the quota forward against labour. So the crew was hungry twice over, and both times it was his number on the sheet.

Every man came up. Every shift. He struck each of them through and watched each one take his check off its nail.

The crew turned on him by the second week.

"Wash it."

"It's chalk, Four."

"My kid brother could wash a board."

Danny Fitch was loudest. He was nineteen, Two, off the Baltimore docks. He played solitaire every night with a deck that had lost its queen of hearts, and he said he would win without her. At the bread line he said it to Four's face:

"You like it up there. Nice and dry. You like keeping us short."

Four could not tell them the sum. Said aloud, it was a report, and a report gets read out at the office. He kept Wrona's check in his pocket and his daughter's remainder in his cap, and he ate short with the rest of them.

The new Seven counted the men up in German every evening, under his breath, standing by Wrona's nail. Once he stood in front of the old name for a long time, reading it the way a man reads a word in a language he knows. He said nothing about it.

Left up, the name took no one. Nobody went down for it. Everybody went hungry for it.

## 8. Audit notice

On Monday, 1 December, the notice went up on the board in the yard, and Hask brought a copy down to the ore chamber and read it out.

The Inspector-General was coming on Friday's payroll train. Every board in the camp would be reconciled by then. An unreconciled board was the keeper's fault.

"Clean by Friday," Hask said. "Or your billet goes down the winze with the next shift."

"No," Four said.

He said it out loud. It was the first time in the camp he had said it to anyone.

Hask wrote something. The bread was cut again, for the whole crew, by a third.

That night, in the yard, Four went to the Workings rack and looked at his own billet for the first time: slot 4, oak, his number burned in. Just under the number was a knot in the grain, round and dark, like a thumbprint.

His own billet, set against a clean board.

## 9. Closed count

He washed it on Thursday night.

Nobody was in the chamber. The bucket, the drip, the rag. The rounder hand came off the way it had the first time, as if it had never been anything but chalk. He told himself it might be a different man this time, or no man at all. He told himself the Inspector-General's signature might be worth something, even down here.

On Friday morning the Inspector-General came down the adit with Hask and a man carrying a lamp in front of him. From the far end of the chamber he was a coat and a pen. He did not look at the board. He looked at the sheet, and signed it, and went back up into the daylight.

Danny went down on the first shift under that signature. He went past the board with his deck in his shirt pocket and did not look at Four.

He did not come up.

At the end of the shift the old name was back in the DOWN column, in Danny's place, in the rounder hand. Two was nowhere on the board.

On the nail beside the name hung Wrona's whistle, on a length of string. It was finished: the finger holes cut, the mouthpiece rounded. Somebody had done the last of the work below.

Four did not blow it.

Danny's deck was on his bunk that night, squared up. Fifty-one cards, and still no queen.

## 10. In his own hand

> G99-T · WORKINGS · SAT 6 DEC 52 · DAY · KEEPER 4 · DOWN 9 · UP 8 · RESIDUAL 1 · DISPOSITION 2 — TRANSFERRED

He wrote it before Hask asked. Slot 2, transferred, in his own hand, in the field where the office had written it for Seven. It took no longer than any other word.

Then, because the board still read residual one and the bread would be cut again, he wrote it a second time, on the same line, against the old name. *Transferred.*

The office took it. The sheet closed. The bread came back.

The next two Fridays were single lines.

Friday, 12 December: payroll on time. At the platform the guards took a man, a scientist, the men said, in a good coat. After that nothing was found that had been his. No room, no papers, nobody he had been working on. Nothing at all. *It's almost like they had help,* somebody said at the bread line, and nobody asked who.

Friday, 19 December: the steam went off all over the camp, and the arc-lights with it, and in the morning the Inspector-General was not accounted for.

Four heard both and did not follow either. He had a count to keep. Down, up, residual one, transferred. His sheets closed clean every shift.

The word was easy once it was his.

## 11. Last shift

On Saturday, 20 December, the last shift of the season, Hask came down with all of Four's sheets in a stack and read them out, the way he read everything.

"Clean," he said. "Clean. Clean. Good sheets, Four."

Then: "Keeper rotates. The new Two keeps the board from Monday. You go down with the last shift."

"Rotates."

"Rule."

"The keeper before me."

"Transferred," Hask said.

Writing the word had not kept him above. He had thought it would, in some sum he had never quite finished on his trouser seam.

Hask chalked nothing. He never did. Keeping the board was the keeper's job. Four took the stub of chalk and wrote 4 in the DOWN column, under the old name, in his own hand.

He left the residual field empty, for somebody else to fill.

## 12. Clean slate

The new Two was a big, quiet boy with his billet still pale from the brand. He held the custody chit in both hands as if it might tear.

Four taught him the board in four sentences.

"Down on the left. Up on the right. Close it every shift."

Then the fourth.

"Don't wash that one."

The old name had gone thin over the weeks, from the drip and the damp and men's shoulders brushing it. Some of the gaps had spread. Four went over it with the chalk, letter by letter where there were letters, and he left the gaps where they had always been. The hand that came out was rounder than his own. He looked at it for a while. It was the hand he had found on the first day.

He put the chalk in the boy's palm and closed the boy's fingers on it.

He kept Wrona's check in his pocket and his daughter's remainder in his cap. He left the whistle on its nail. Then he went down with the last shift.

The ladder was wet below the boards. The cold came up it like a hand.

The light in the refuge. The water under it, rising and going back.

At its edge they were sitting in a row, the way the crew sat at the bread line. Eight of them, nine. White. The skin pulled tight round bone and split at the knuckles. Ribs. Mouths shut on a breath held so long the cheeks had sunk to the teeth. One had a flat of pine on its knee and was working at it with nails that were not there. One, the smallest, held a single playing card face down against its chest, very carefully, the way a boy holds the card he means to win with.

None of them looked up. They were counting. He could see it in the throats.

Above him, small and far off, he heard the new keeper read the frame aloud, slowly, the way Hask did, the way a man reads a rule he has been told is his now.

"Clean slate each shift."
