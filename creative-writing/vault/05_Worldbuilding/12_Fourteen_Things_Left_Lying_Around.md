# Fourteen Things Left Lying Around

Fourteen things. The Constable wrote them out himself.

He wrote them on Company paper in a round, slow hand, the hand of a man taught late, and he numbered them down the margin. Then he blotted the list and turned it round on the desk so I could read it.

The crepe. The clock. The tide chart. The chalk. The photographs. The shelf. The pelt. The rope. The portrait. The crate. The scarecrow. The stone. The lantern. The boots.

"Things left lying around," he said. "People talk about them. Talk's the trouble."

Beside the list he set a magnifier, a case file with nothing in it, and a rubber stamp still smelling of its box. He inked it on his own pad and pressed it once on the blotter so I could see what it said.

NO CASE.

The riot was behind us. The Baron had come up the Line once already, with his Pinkertons, looked at the square, and gone back down. Now he was coming again on Saturday's train to see whether it had held. And the town was still talking. Lord Aldous Vane was three nights dead, the way he has been three nights dead for as long as anyone here can reckon, and the will read in the manor named *whoever remains when the last lantern goes out*. That had got out of the manor and into the Drowned Lantern, and from there into every chest in HollowMere along with the cough.

"He steps off," the Constable said, "and I hand him a file, and every line in it says that. Nothing happened. Nobody did anything. Saturday."

I said yes. Article IX. He swore me in over the blotter. I had kept time at the railyard for two years. I had a clean hand and a good watch, and I believed (I wrote it down that first night, so I can't pretend otherwise) that a thing left lying around was left by a hand, and a hand, if you looked, had left a receipt.

Collect. Inspect. Cross-reference. That was all the job was.

---

**No. 1. Black crepe. Manor door.**

Collected: one strip, cut from the lower edge with my penknife. Two inches.
Inspected: Company crepe, the cheap weave, badly dyed. The black comes off on the fingers.
Cross-referenced: Company Store ledger, the morning the wire came. *Widow Vane, crepe, 4 yds, scrip.* Her mark beside it.
Stamped.

It took an hour, most of it waiting for the storekeeper to find the right ledger. I stood in the Store with black on my fingers and watched him run his thumb down the page, and when he found the line I nearly laughed.

A widow hangs crepe. The Company sells it. Here is where it was sold.

I pressed the stamp down and held it, the way the Constable had, and lifted it clean. Under the stamp, small, as a note to myself, I wrote: every hand has a receipt. I underlined it.

It was the first thing on the list that made sense. It was also the last.

---

**No. 2. The clock. Vane Manor, the hall.**

Collected: nothing. It stands eight feet and is bolted to the wall.
Inspected: hands at 11:47. Case unlocked. Pendulum hanging still. Weights wound up.
Cross-referenced: nothing found.
Stamped.

The Butler let me in. People say he knows every door in HollowMere. He walked me to this one as if it were the only one.

"You stopped it," I said. "For the death. That's the custom."

"No, sir."

"Then who did?"

"It was stopped when I came into service."

I opened the case. The weights were up. The pendulum hung plumb and did not swing when I nudged it. I put a finger to the minute hand to set it on, gently, the way you free a railyard clock stuck in frost. It did not move. I pushed. It wasn't stiff and it wasn't rusted. It simply would not go.

The Butler watched me take my hand back.

There was no receipt. Nobody had bought the stopping of it. The Store had no line and the Butler had no memory. When I asked at the pump, the town said it stopped the hour the lake drained, and said it the way you say the weather. Said it. Nobody saw it.

So I wrote a second rule under the first: what is older than the books is history, not evidence. A clock that stopped before anyone kept ledgers is not a clue. It is furniture.

NO CASE.

I wiped that finger on my coat for a long time afterwards.

---

**No. 3. The tide chart. The Drowned Lantern.**

Collected: nothing. Mrs Grieves would not have it off the wall.
Inspected: an old harbour chart, island print, the paper brown as toast. The next low tide circled.
Cross-referenced: my watch. The marsh dock. The channel post.
Stamped.

It was older than any book in town, so by my second rule it was history. I had the stamp out before I had my coat off.

Then I saw the ink. The circle round the next low tide was wet. It shone. When I touched its edge it came away blue on my thumb.

"Who marks it?" I said.

"Not me," said Mrs Grieves.

"Who, then?"

"Somebody always has."

Her man Bar went on wiping the same glass and did not look up.

A fen with no sea for three hundred miles. I took down the circled hour and went to the marsh dock with my watch open in my hand, and stood by the post where Tuck ties the ferry. There is a tar line on that post. I set my thumbnail on it at the water's edge.

At the circled minute the water went down a hand's breadth. Not a ripple, not the wind. Down, the way a chest goes down.

People say it's the lake's old channels, running under the stone to water nobody has mapped. People say the chart is the island's, and the island keeps its tides wherever its paper goes. I wrote down both and did not pick one.

But somebody had circled it that morning. This was not history. It was today.

So I made a third rule: the town keeps its old things current out of habit, the way a widow winds a dead man's watch. Habit is not a crime.

NO CASE. My thumb stayed blue for two days.

---

**No. 4. Chalk hopscotch. The square, under the fourth lantern.**

Collected: a pinch of chalk dust, in a paper.
Inspected: ten squares, numbered, in a careful hand. Fresh.
Cross-referenced: the night. Myself.
Stamped.

A habit has a keeper. That was the trouble with my third rule, and I knew it when I wrote it. If the town keeps its old things current, somebody in the town gets up and does it, and somebody can be seen.

So I sat the whole night under the fourth lantern, the one over the chalk and the well. I sat on the well's edge with my coat buttoned and my watch on my knee where the light fell on it. The Lamplighter came to this lantern last, as she always does, and lit it and looked at me and said nothing. Curfew rang. The wolves came to the fence, went along it, and went away. Something in the fog walked behind the pump in short steps and was not there when I looked.

I wound my watch at midnight. I remember that. I remember the click.

At dawn the hopscotch was fresh: ten squares redrawn in clean white chalk, sharp-edged, not a smudge, not a footprint through them. The old lines were gone under the new.

No chalk on the stones round it. No one.

I did not remember sleeping. I did not remember not sleeping. When the train came in at Station Rush I held my watch up to the station clock, and my watch was an hour behind.

I have kept time for two years. I have never lost an hour.

I wrote it in the margin in the Company's word, because it was the only one I had: miasma. Mere-rot in the chest and the eye. A man sits all night in the fog, and the fog takes an hour off him and does his chalk for him. That is what miasma is for.

NO CASE.

---

**No. 5. The drowned-sailor photographs. The Drowned Lantern.**

Collected: nothing.
Inspected: frames behind the bar, black wood and glass. Men in oilskins. Men in water.
Cross-referenced: Mrs Grieves. My note of the day before.
Stamped.

Miasma lives in the eye. If my eye was the trouble, my eye could not be the witness. So from here on I would log nothing I had not counted with someone else.

I asked Mrs Grieves to count the frames with me, aloud.

She didn't want to. She dried her hands and stood beside me behind the bar, and we went along the wall frame by frame, out loud, like children doing sums.

One. Two. Three. The third held no picture at all. "That's Jory's," she said, and went on. She has a heavy way of saying numbers, as if each one costs her.

Five. Six. Seven.

I had been in the day before, for the chart. I had counted then out of habit and put it in the file. I took out the file and showed her the page. Six.

She looked at the page, and at the wall. She did not say I was wrong.

The seventh hung at the end of the row, by the shutters, on a nail I would swear had not been there. It showed a man in a thin coat, standing in water to the waist and turned away toward more water. You could not see his face. You could see his hands, and that he had no hat.

"Who's he?" I said.

"I don't know him," she said, and went back to her end of the bar.

A picture goes up in the night between one count and the next. Somebody had a nail and a hammer and a reason, and I happened to count either side of it. I wrote down the word *coincidence* and looked at it, and it looked back at me like a word in a language I had only just learned.

NO CASE.

---

**No. 6. The NOT FOR SALE shelf. Mallory Apothecary.**

Collected: nothing. There is nothing to collect. That is the item.
Inspected: six slots, five bottles of brown glass, wax-sealed. One slot empty, and no dust in it.
Cross-referenced: Doc Mallory's stock book.
Stamped.

Mallory let me see the book because the Constable had sent me, and for no other reason. Every bottle on that shelf has a line in it, and every line has a name and a date. Five of them are filled.

The sixth line is headed in ink gone brown, and nothing is written under it. It has never been filled.

*Reserved: Det.*

"What goes in it?" I said.

Mallory took the book back and closed it.

A coincidence does not get a line in a book. A coincidence is what happens between people who have never met. Somebody had sat at that counter with a pen and laid a place for me.

Not me, I told myself. The office. The Company reserves things for its offices. An office is a chair, and I happen to be sitting in it.

I stamped it NO CASE. Then, because I wanted the office to be only an office, I walked to the Constable's and asked him who had held it before me.

"Nobody," he said. He did not look up from his desk. "It's new. Made under Article IX. For this list."

I thanked him. I believed him for the length of the street.

---

**No. 7. The wolf pelt. Hale Barn door.**

Collected: one tuft of grey hair.
Inspected: a whole pelt, nailed through the forelegs. Old nails. The pelt not old.
Cross-referenced: the Gamekeeper.
Stamped.

The Gamekeeper was in the barn with the goat, oiling a rifle. Vane paid him to keep the wood. Vane is dead, and he keeps it anyway.

"When did you nail it up?" I said.

"The week the last one went into the Fenwood."

"The last what?"

He looked at my case file, and at my stamp. "Detective," he said. "After the lantern. Went in at dusk with a book like that under his arm and didn't come out the side he went in."

The office is new, the Constable said. Made for this list.

The office had had a man in it before me.

I didn't go back to the Constable's. It was the first thing I did not do. Instead I wrote that the last one had been careless: a man who walked into a fen at dusk after a light everybody knows not to walk after. Careless men go into the Fenwood. I am not a careless man.

NO CASE.

---

**No. 8. The rope. St Brannock's.**

Collected: nothing. The glove is twisted into the rope.
Inspected: the bell rope, hemp, gone black at hand height. Caught in its lay, the cuff and half the palm of a left glove, plain brown kid. The palm worn through by rope and darned over in red wool, badly.
Cross-referenced: the new Vicar. My coat.
Stamped.

The new Vicar came up the Line after the old priest had gone. He stood under the bell with me and looked at the glove the way you look at a dog that might bite.

"They say Father Bray rang it till his palms burned through his gloves," he said. "The day the first train came. They say a lot."

"Is it his?"

"I don't know whose it is."

The red wool was coarse, and the stitches went every way, as if whoever darned it did it in poor light or in a hurry or did not know how. I had no way to read that, and I did not try.

I reached into my coat for my own gloves, to put them on before I touched the rope. They are plain brown kid too, worn thin at the palm from two years of railyard levers. The right one was there. The left was not.

I turned the pocket out. Lint. A pencil end. Blue on the lining from the tide chart.

Half the men in this town wear plain brown kid. Every man who works with his hands wears it through at the palm. Mine fell in the mud somewhere between the barn and here. I wrote that down, pressing hard to keep the hand steady.

A careful man would have noticed when he lost a glove.

NO CASE.

---

**No. 9. The portrait. Widow Vane's parlour.**

Collected: nothing.
Cross-referenced: the back of the frame. Wire, nail, an inch of dust along the top edge.
Stamped.

Widow Vane had me shown into the parlour and did not stand up. The portrait hangs over the letter box, turned to the wall. What the room sees is brown backing board and a maker's label too faded to read.

"You may turn it," she said, "if it's your work."

I stood in front of it with my hands at my sides.

Everything I had turned over that week had given me something back: a wet circle, an hour, a seventh face, a line in a book with my office on it, a glove gone from my own pocket.

I logged the back of the frame, the wire, the nail and the dust. I did not lift it off the nail.

She watched me not turn it. When I was done she rang for the Butler to see me out, and said nothing at all.

It was the first thing I stamped without looking at it.

---

**No. 10. The VANE TIN CO crate. The Flooded Gallery.**

Collected: everything in it.
Cross-referenced: everything in it.
Stamped.

Past its mouth the Tin Gallery is dark and wet. I borrowed a lantern from the Drowned Lantern. Mrs Grieves gave it to me without asking where I was going. There was water to the ankle at the pump beam, and the ore cart lay on its side.

The crate sits on two planks above the water, with VANE TIN CO stencilled on the lid. The lid is half-pried, the nails bent up on one side and not the other, as if whoever was prying it stopped.

I finished it. It was the last thing I pushed.

Straw. Under the straw, a case file. Under the file, a magnifier and a rubber stamp. I knew the stamp before I turned it over.

The list at the front of the file was on Company paper, in a round, slow hand, numbered down the margin. It held the same fourteen things, but not in my order. His went the crepe, the boots, the clock, the stone, and so on, with the drowned lantern last.

Thirteen of them were stamped NO CASE. The entries under them were neat and the cross-references sound. He had found the crepe in the Store ledger, the same as I had. He had counted the photographs with somebody. He had measured the boots. He had a good hand.

The fourteenth line, the drowned lantern, was blank.

He was not careless. He had walked into the Fenwood at dusk to finish, with this file under his arm or one like it. And the file was here, dry, under straw, in a crate somebody had half-pried and left.

I held the lantern over the water a long time before I came up.

NO CASE.

---

**No. 11. The scarecrow in the trench coat. The square.**

Collected: one ticket.
Cross-referenced: his entry for it.
Stamped.

His entry read: *Scarecrow. Square. Coat, trench, Company cloth. Pockets not examined.*

Pockets not examined was the only lazy line in his file. I went to the square at noon, while the Clerk was taking the rent and nobody looks at anything but their scrip, and examined the pockets.

There was stamp ink on the right cuff, old, the purple-black of the Constable's pad, worked into the weave where a wrist would rest on a desk.

In the left pocket was a ticket: one-way, HollowMere to back east, all the way down the Line. Paid. Never punched. Conductor Pruett, No. 14, punches every fare with his crescent, and there was no crescent in this one. It had gone nowhere.

The way out had been in the coat, and he had not taken it.

I took it. I put it in my own pocket, the one with the blue lining, and kept my hand on it.

Then I made the plan he hadn't. Three things were left after this one. I would finish the list and hand the Constable the file on Saturday. The Baron would step off and read it, the train would turn round at dusk, and I would be on it with this in my hand. The list is the only thing here that ends. I'd finish it and use the ticket.

I went to the churchyard at first light for the next one.

---

**No. 12. The stone with tomorrow's date. St Brannock's churchyard.**

Collected: the name.
Stamped.

It stands alone at the end of the row: grey slate, new-cut, and the date on it tomorrow's. It is always tomorrow's. People say the name on it is new every morning, and that whoever it names is the next to die. Nobody says who cuts it. Nobody has ever seen it cut.

I had meant to log it, stamp it and go. It was a stone, and stones end.

But it carried a name today, and it would carry a different one tomorrow, and another the day after, for as long as there were mornings. A thing that renews itself cannot be finished. You could stamp it every day for the rest of your life. You could stamp it on the train.

The name was Edith.

No surname. The town knew which Edith.

I didn't cross-reference it. I put the file under my arm and went to find her door, because she was still alive, and I could still tell her.

---

**Not on the list.**

She lives at the far end of Pump Row, in one room behind a door with the Company's number painted on it. She had the door half shut before I finished saying who I was.

"I know what it says," she said. "Half the Row's been by to tell me. Go home."

"Then let me sit here."

She looked at the stamp in my hand, and shut the door.

I sat on the step with my back against it. There is no lantern on Pump Row; the Lamplighter's round does not come this way. Curfew rang.

After a while someone sat down on the other side. I heard her slide down the wood and settle there, by the bottom hinge.

"You're not here about the rent," Edith said. It was not a question.

She told me about the rent anyway. She was two weeks behind. She did washing for the manor and the mill office, and they paid her in scrip, and every noon the scrip went back to the Clerk. She had a way of saying "two weeks" as if it might, said carefully enough, come to one.

She had a sister, younger, who had gone back east on the train before the riot, to a city whose name Edith said the way you say a name you have only ever seen written. The sister had written twice. Edith was writing back.

"I haven't finished it," she said.

"Why not?"

"I don't know how to say the town's all right."

I didn't know either. I said she could tell her about the train. She said her sister knew about the train.

She coughed now and then and said it was the fog. She asked if they had wolves in the city. I said I didn't think so. She asked what I was writing. I said a list. She asked what number she was. I said she wasn't on it.

"Good," she said.

She told me her sister's name. She told me she had bought a postage stamp for the letter at the Post Office, a real one, two cents in money she had kept back from the scrip, and had not stuck it down yet, because once it was stuck down the letter had to go. Then she stopped talking. I heard her breathing on the other side of the wood, slow, and I sat there with my hand on the ticket in my pocket and watched the dark lane until the fog came white and the whistle went for Station Rush.

I did not sleep. I am certain of that. I checked my watch every hour by the chapel bell, and all night it kept good time.

At first light Doc Mallory came with the Constable, because the Clerk had sent them about the rent. I stood up out of the way. The Constable knocked, and called, and put his shoulder to the door, and the bolt gave. It had been shot from the inside all night, with my back against the wood.

Edith was in her bed. The blanket was straight.

Mallory looked at her a long time and wrote something, then crossed it out and put the pen away.

On the table there was a letter, folded once and not finished, with the stamp lying loose on top.

Nobody had come in.

I went up to the churchyard. I don't know why. The stone at the end of the row already had a name on it, a new one, with tomorrow's date. I did not go near enough to read it.

Two left.

---

**No. 13. The drowned lantern. The Fenwood.**

Collected: nothing.
Stamped.

His blank line. I went out to Gallows Wood and through it to where the planks stop and the fen starts and the black pines stand in water. I stopped at the last dry plank and went no nearer.

It was there in the fog, out past the hanging oak: a lantern, low to the water, glowing. Steady. Not like a wisp. Like a lamp somebody keeps.

I did not see who keeps it, and I did not look. I wrote down that it was there and that it was lit, stamped the page on my knee, and turned round.

On the planks going back, in the fog, there were footsteps behind me. Mine, and a half-beat after mine, the same weight and the same stride. When I stopped they stopped. When I went on they went on.

Mere-rot: the fen in the ear, the false footsteps everybody in HollowMere has heard. I said it out loud, mere-rot, and kept saying it the whole way back to the square.

When I reached the pump my boots were black with Fenwood mud to the shin. I don't remember stepping off the planks.

---

**No. 14. The bootprints. Vane Manor, the hall carpet.**

Collected.
Stamped.

They have been there as long as anyone can say: a track of muddy bootprints across the hall carpet from the door to the foot of the stair, and none coming back. The Company calls them nothing. Whoever logs them never matches them.

His file had them, second on his list, measured with care: length heel to toe, breadth at the ball, the depth of the heel.

I put my own boot down beside the first print.

Heel to toe. The breadth. The heel.

A hundred men in this town wear the Company boot. I have written it. Height is a trait, and a hundred men share it.

But the mud in the prints was wet.

I knelt and put a finger in the third print, and it came up black and cold and smelling of the fen. Fenwood mud, the same as on my boots, to the shin.

The prints go to the stair and stop. They do not come back. Mine go to the door, and I can see them now, crossing the others.

I cannot rule myself out, so I ruled nothing.

The fourteenth thing was mine.

---

I stamped it NO CASE. I held the stamp down the way the Constable had shown me, and lifted it clean.

I went down into the Flooded Gallery again with Mrs Grieves's lantern and laid my file in the crate beside his, with the ticket on top of both, unpunched. I put the straw back. I left the lid half-pried, the nails up on one side, the way I had found it.

On Saturday the Baron's train came in at Station Rush with its Pinkertons and stood at the platform all day, breathing. The Baron went up to the Constable's and came out again, and at dusk the train went back down the Line.

I watched it go from the square. It doesn't wait. It never has.

On Monday I went past the Constable's window. He was at his desk, with Company paper in front of him and a new stamp still in its box, writing in a round, slow hand and numbering down the margin.

Fourteen things. The Constable wrote them out himself.
