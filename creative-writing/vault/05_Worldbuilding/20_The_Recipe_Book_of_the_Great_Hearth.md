# The Recipe Book of the Great Hearth

*Being the Receipts of the Command Desk of the Sacred Kitchen of Port-au-Levain, entered by order of the Sovereign Sourdough Council, from the winter of the year 1787.*

[SEALED: SOVEREIGN SOURDOUGH COUNCIL]

[STRUCK: THE COMMONS OF PORT-AU-LEVAIN]

> A receipt is a law that can be eaten.
> — inside the front board, in the first hand

---

## Of the Desk

**The Desk.** The command desk is the room of the Sacred Kitchen where the day's bake is ordered. It was the Chef's before it was the Council's, and the Council has never moved the inkstand. A receipt enters this book in one way only. The Council orders it, the Sous-Chef's clerk writes it, and the Aboyeur calls it at the expediter. From that hour it is served, and from that hour it is law.

**The Tables.** Every receipt is entered with the table it is served to.
- *Gold leaf:* the Gallery, at the gilded platters nearest the Hearth.
- *Flour-white:* the Guild, which is the Master Proof-Reader, the Spice-Master, the Tide-Watcher, the Hearth-Warden and the Ledger-Clerk.
- *Burlap:* the Abbey of the Crust.

The dish pit has no colour. It is served what is left.

**The One Rule of the Desk.** The measures of the Mother's bread are not written in this book. They are in the hymn, and the hymn is not written anywhere. Where a receipt here needs one of her measures, it gives the verse instead.

## Part One: The Gallery's Pages

*1787 to the twenty-third of September, 1789. Ruled in gold.*

### 1. The Wake Loaf

**Table.** Every table.
**Yield.** One loaf to one soul. **Season.** Every night of the year.

**Of the Receipt.** Use leaven from the parent's line, never a stranger's. Flour and water as the third verse. Steam as the second. The rest as the fourth. Shape it at the shaping bench with the hands of the one it is for. For a child, use the hands that will answer for it. Press the guild seal into the crown before the rest, not after. Set it on its silver platter, under its cloche, in the Hall, and leave it there.

- *For the gold-leaf table:* lay leaf over the seal as the loaf comes from the oven, so that the seal shows gilt at dawn.
- *For the flour-white table:* dust the crown with the Guild's flour, so that the seal shows white.
- *For the dish pit:* shape it from the scrapings of the bench and press no seal. The loaf is counted, not called.

**Of the Law.**
*A soul without a platter has no name in law.*
*An unpaid tariff makes a loaf Unregistered.*
*At the dawn bell every loaf is cracked, and whatever is found in it is found against its platter.*

*[in pencil: The dish-pit loaves are shaped last, after the board has been wiped for the Gallery's. What the receipt calls scrapings is what the Gallery's loaves left behind.]*

### 2. The Loaf for the Empty Platter

**Table.** None.
**Yield.** One loaf. **Season.** Every dusk.

**Of the Receipt.** A plain loaf from the Hall's ordinary bake. No gold, no white, no seal. At dusk, after the Hall lamps are lit, the Aboyeur carries it down the Hall. He calls no name. He sets it on the empty platter nearest the Hearth, for a name under seal, and lowers the cloche. At dawn it is taken away. It is not cracked, and it is not eaten.

**Of the Law.**
*The night is reckoned from this loaf.*

**The Clerk's Note.** This receipt is copied word for word from the book before this one. It was copied into that book from the one before it. It is entered here as it was found. The desk does not amend it.

### 3. The Abbot's Blessing Loaf

**Table.** Burlap.
**Yield.** One loaf for each threshold on the Abbot's round. **Season.** On the Abbey's days.

**Of the Receipt.** A round loaf risen on the Apostolic Starter, which the Abbey keeps and the desk does not. Bake it plain and wrap it in burlap while it is still warm. The Abbot carries it on his round and breaks off a piece at each threshold he blesses. The house keeps that piece on its sill until the next bake.

**Of the Law.**
*A loaf the Abbot has not blessed may be refused at the Hall.*
*A sill with no piece on it belongs to a house the Abbot has not visited, and the Inquisitor may ask why.*

**Of the Heresy.** The Leaven-Rot is entered here because it is a heresy against this receipt. It is the belief that the Mother may be soured on purpose, and that a soured Mother may be prayed through. The desk enters the belief and nothing further.

*[in red, across the foot of the page: After 1790 no burlap came to the desk.]*

### 4. Bitter-Citron Cloche Varnish

**Table.** Flour-white. The Spice-Master's receipt, entered at the Council's request.
**Yield.** Enough for the cloches of one row. **Season.** Whenever the Gallery complains of missing crusts.

**Of the Receipt.** Pare citrons thin. Steep the peel in oil for a week in the Apothecary window. Warm the oil with beeswax until it holds together, and colour it with saffron. Rub it onto the silver cloche with a cloth and leave it. It harms nothing. Whoever lifts the cloche afterwards has yellow hands until morning, and soap will not take the colour off before then.

**Of the Law.**
*Yellow hands at dawn are evidence before the High Chancellor.*
*Whose hands are yellow is a question for the Tribunal. Why they are yellow is not.*

**Entered under this receipt, 1788.** The Commis whose task was polishing the cloches came to the Dawn Tribunal with yellow hands. The varnish had been laid that night, and she had polished the row after it as she was ordered to. Nobody had told her it was there. Verdict: a Guild Fine of four Guild Copper. The fine went unpaid and her loaf was stamped Unregistered. She went to the dish pit by sentence, and the varnish was entered as having worked.

*[in pencil: Rozenn. She hummed the boat songs while she polished, because L'Apprenti may not speak but a Commis may hum. She did the row nearest the Hearth twice over, because the old silver showed her face best.]*

### 5. The Ferry Comfit

**Table.** Gold leaf.
**Yield.** Seven to a dish. **Season.** Weddings, and anyone leaving on the Morning Ferry.

**Of the Receipt.** Blanch the almonds and dry them in the warmth of the lamination room. Boil sugar with water until it threads. Turn the almonds in it a few at a time, and let each coat dry before you add the next, until they are white and as hard as river stones. Lay seven to a dish of the Gallery's silver and give them by hand.

**The Tale.** It is told at the shaping bench, though it is not recorded, that in the plague year a dish of seven was passed at a wedding in the Port Tavern, and that the Poisoner had made the seventh. The tale does not say whom it was meant for. It does say who ate it: the Tide-Watcher's daughter, nine years old. She was saving the sugar shells for her small brother and had six in her apron already. She took the seventh because the dish had come round to her and it would have been rude to leave one.

*What was in the seventh is not written here, and was never written anywhere. The Council forbids the question.*

**Of the Law.**
*The giver eats the first comfit and the last, in sight of the table.*

The custom is the whole use of this receipt. The almonds are only how the custom is carried.

*[in pencil: The brother kept the six shells in a twist of paper. He was a grown man, and a Tide-Watcher himself, before he threw them into the sea.]*

### 7. Supper for a Sentence (the first in this book)

*Bill of fare for the Gallery, in the gallery above the Hall of Platters, with the south doors standing open on Gallows Square. The evening of the fourteenth of November, 1787.*

**The Charge**, as printed on the card: *that the sentenced did take a crumb from the floor of the Hall after the dawn cracking, the said crumb being the heel of a loaf not her own.*
**The Verdict.** Trial by Fire in the Great Hearth.

| Course | Bell | Served |
|---|---|---|
| I. Oysters of the reef, opened at the table | at the first bell | served |
| II. Consommé of salt cod, clarified until the sentence may be read through it | as the Aboyeur calls the charge in the Square | served |
| III. Gilded brioche | at the music | served |
| IV. Venison, off the ferry | after the Fire | served |
| V. Quince, poached in the Spice-Master's sugar | | served |
| VI. A pièce montée of the Great Hearth in spun sugar, its fire in candied orange | | served |

**Etiquette.**
- A cover is laid for Le Critique Gastronomique at the head of the table. It is not served.
- The musicians stand at the gallery rail, facing the Hall, with their backs to the Square.
- Applause belongs between courses, never during one.
- The guests do not rise for the bell. The bell is for the Square.

**The Costing**, in the clerk's hand. The oysters, the venison, the sugar for the pièce, the candles, the musicians and the leaf for the brioche came to one hundred and sixty-four Guild Copper. The sentenced's tariff at the dish-pit rate was four Copper a year. The supper is entered as forty-one years of her.

The Hearth received her between the brioche and the venison. She was forty-four. The venison went up warm.

**After.** As the custom is, the leavings of the gallery's table go down to the dish pit. The Plongeurs eat what was served over one of their own.

*[in pencil: Jacquette Gaudet, a salter's widow. Every Sunday she counted her drowned husband's hooks into a tin, and every Sunday there were thirty-one. The heel was for the child next door, who was four and had been Unregistered since August.]*

### 8. Supper for a Sentence (the second in this book, postponed)

*Bill of fare for the Gallery, the evening of the eighth of April, 1788. Moved to the ninth.*

**The Charge**, as printed on the card: *that the sentenced did speak at the shaping bench in the hearing of the gallery.* The disgrace of L'Apprenti is speaking. The Council made it a sentence.
**The Verdict.** Trial by Fire in the Great Hearth.

**The Courses.** The same as the first, with turbot in place of the venison.

**The Postponement**, in the clerk's hand: *Fog came in off the reef in the afternoon of the eighth, and the sugar of the pièce montée would not set. The Council does not wish the sentence carried out without its supper, and has moved both to the evening of the ninth.*

The sentenced waited out the night of the eighth in the cold pantry, the one room in the Sacred Kitchen with its lock on the outside. The Garde Manger kept the key. He was given water and nothing from the menu, the menu being for the ninth.

Served on the ninth: every course. The pièce montée set. The Hearth received him between the brioche and the turbot.

*[in pencil: Corentin Hébert, apprentice, sixteen. What he said at the bench was good morning. He carved fish out of driftwood with a nail, a mackerel or a cod, each small enough to close a hand on. He left one on the pantry shelf beside the butter. It was a cod. The Garde Manger keeps it with the keys.]*

### 9. Supper for the Crown's Man

*Bill of fare for the Gallery, to welcome the Grand Inspector-General of His Majesty, come off the Morning Ferry. The evening of the twenty-third of September, 1789.*

**The Seating**, nearest the Hearth first.
- The house of Vane.
- Madame de Montaury.
- The house of Courcelle.
- The remaining houses, by the age of their platters.
- At the head, beside the Critique's empty cover: the Grand Inspector-General.

**The Courses.**
- I. Oysters of the reef.
- II. Consommé of salt cod, clarified twice, for the Crown.
- III. Gilded brioche, the leaf laid double.
- IV. Lobster from the Wharf, dressed in the Crown's colours.
- V. Venison, off the ferry.
- VI.

[The gold rule runs on to the foot of the leaf. Nothing more is written on it.]

## Part Two: The Commons' Pages

*1790 to the spring of 1793. Written in red, over and between the gold rules.*

### 10. Of the Desk, Again

**The Desk.** In the year 1790 the Commons sat down at the command desk, in the Chef's chair. They did not move the inkstand either.

**What Was Struck.**
- The Council's seal on the front board. Struck.
- The gold leaf in the Wake Loaf. Struck.
- The words *the dish pit has no colour.* Struck.
- The loaf for the empty platter. Not struck.

**Why the Book Is Kept.** The Gallery wrote its laws here. We will write ours over them, in the same book, so that anyone who reads ours has to read theirs underneath.

**The Tables, Again.**
- *Red crust:* everyone.
- *Flour-white:* the Guild, until it says which side it is on.
- *Gold leaf:* the table that is served last.

*[in pencil: The committee is the dish pit, the Commis and the Wharf. They sit in the Chef's chair now. Their own suppers still go down to the same sink.]*

### 11. Bread of Equality

**Table.** Every table, one loaf. **Entered** 1790.

**Of the Receipt.** One flour for every platter: rye, wheat and bran milled together at the Windmill Cliffs, with the bran left in. One weight and one shape, which is round. No leaf and no white. The leaven is not the Mother's. Set flour and water in a crock on the Salt-Flats wall, with a cloth over it, until the wild leaven catches. Feed it with the same flour. Bake hot.

**Of the Law.**
*No loaf to be finer than another.*
*No loaf to be risen on a line.*
*The gold to be scraped from every platter in the Hall.*

The gold was scraped from every platter in the Hall but one, which was not touched.

The wild leaven is blessed on the Salt-Flats at the turn of each tide. The Gallery called that heresy. We call it bread.

The Mother of Mothers is still fed at dawn by the feeding hand of the house of Vane. Her receipt is in the hymn. Every child on the rock can sing it, and still only one hand may feed her by it.

*[in pencil: The Plongeurs still eat last. It is the same bread now, which is something. It is still last.]*

### 12. The Red Crust

**Table.** Red crust. **Entered** 1791.

**Of the Receipt.** Grate beets from the Salt-Flat gardens and press out the juice. Brush the shaped loaf with it before it goes into the oven, and again halfway through the bake. The crust comes out the red of brick. It tastes of nothing but bread.

**Of the Law.**
*Every platter bakes red.*
*A crust found white is found against its platter.*
*A white crust under a red wash is a Gallery loaf pretending, and is to be cracked before the Square.*

*[in pencil: 1787, a crumb. 1791, a colour.]*

### 13. Soup for the Tumbril Crowd

**Table.** Gallows Square, the crowd, served standing. **Entered** 1792.

**Of the Receipt.** Take onions, as many as the Gallery's cellar holds. Take the ham bones from its larder, and the last of its white flour to thicken. Boil everything in the Hall's great copper from the noon bell. Ladle it into the Gallery's own gilt-edged soup plates. Serve in the order the Aboyeur calls, the dish pit first.

**The Cart.** The sentenced goes from the Hall to the Square by cart. The Hall's south doors open straight onto the Square, so the cart goes out by the north door instead. It goes round the town by the Wharf road and the Windmill road and comes back in by the south, so that the crowd can follow it and be fed when it arrives. The soup is timed to the cart.
- *At the cart's leaving:* the copper is lifted off the fire.
- *At the cart's coming into the Square:* the soup.
- *After the Fire:* the bread, red.

**The Charge.** *A white crust under a red wash.*
**The Verdict.** The Hearth.

Every bowl was served. The Hearth received her while the second copper was being ladled. The gilt plates were not washed. They went home with the crowd.

*[in pencil: The dowager of the house of Courcelle. Her name was Adélaïde. She kept every bill of fare she was ever served, pinned with a ribbon into a book of her own, forty years of them. The committee took the book when they took her. The supper of the fourteenth of November, 1787 is in it, with a small tick in her hand beside the brioche, which means she liked it.]*

### 14. Supper for the Razor

*Bill of fare for the committee, in the gallery above the Hall of Platters, with the south doors standing open on Gallows Square. The second of May, 1793.*

**The Instrument.** The National Razor came off the Morning Ferry in the spring and was set up in the Square beside the Hearth. The desk enters it the way it enters an oven: by where it stands and by the bell it answers to. One stage, two instruments.

**The Charge**, as printed on the card: *that the accused, Évariste Vane, holder of Le Levain, fed the Mother for the Gallery while the red crust went Unregistered, and sat at the Gallery's suppers.*

The bill is the bill of the fourteenth of November, 1787, course for course, written over in red.

| Course | Bell | Served |
|---|---|---|
| I. ~~Oysters of the reef~~ Oysters of the reef, for all | at the first bell | |
| II. ~~Consommé of salt cod~~ Citizen's Broth | as the charge is read | |
| III. ~~Gilded brioche~~ Bread of Equality, red | at the music | |
| IV. ~~Venison, off the ferry~~ The Gallery's venison | after the Razor | |
| V. Quince, poached | | |
| VI. A pièce montée of the Great Hearth in spun sugar, and beside it, in sugar, the Razor | | |

**Etiquette.**
- A cover is laid for Le Critique Gastronomique at the head of the table. It is not served.
- The musicians stand at the gallery rail.
- Applause belongs between courses.
- The committee does not rise for the bell.

*[in pencil: Citizen Vane, the feeding hand. He fed the Mother with his left hand. In his coat he carried his daughter's leaven-tin.]*
