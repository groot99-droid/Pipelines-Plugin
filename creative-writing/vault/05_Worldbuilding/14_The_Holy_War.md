# The Holy War

*Being a Chronicle of the Two Campaigns for HollowMere, kept at the Telegraph Room for the Eastern Office, 17 October – 31 December 1893*

> HOLLOWMERE — EST. BY THE RAILROAD EXPANSE

*Carbon copies. Originals wired to the Eastern Office. Not for the Gazette.*

---

## Form of the Ledger

In October the Eastern Office asked for a weekly count of persons not at work for reasons of religion. The request came on the wire with the rent schedule, and in the same tone. I have kept the count. This book holds the carbons.

The marks are mine. **L** is a soul of the Light: confessed, and seen at St Brannock's. **A** is a soul of the Ash: does not confess, walks at Dusk, rent unpaid. **—** is a soul that is neither. **?** is a soul I cannot enter. A soul is entered under the roof it pays rent on. That is the only rule I brought to this and the only one I trust, because a roof does not lie about where it stands.

I am not a man of faith. I am a man of columns. A column does not care what it holds.

## What the Town Calls It

**The Sowing — the Light's word.** Said in the open, at the pump, after confession.

**Nothing — the Ash's word.** Said nowhere aloud.

**Absence (Religious) — the Company's word.** Entered on the docket, and docked.

**The law of the war.** The first faith to hold every roof holds HollowMere. Nobody has said what it does with it.

## Book I. The Sowing

### Tuesday 17 October 1893

**Of the Light.** The new Vicar came on the afternoon train, private car, with one trunk. The manifest has him as it has all men of rank: *Not counted (Rule 6).* The rule reads, in full: "Persons of rank and their escorts travel in the private car and are not entered on the passenger count." The Company did not count him from his first day. I note it because I count things for the Company, and because I was later to wish someone had.

He came to my counter before he went up to his chapel. He had a pocket watch on a chain. He asked me the time by the telegraph clock all the same, and set the watch to it, and thanked me as if I had given him something. That evening, Mrs Grieves's book has it, he sat in the Lantern and drank nothing.

**Of the Ash.** Nothing entered.

*Margin, pencil:* Something to enter.

### Sunday 29 October

**Of the Light.** The confession line runs out of the door of St Brannock's and down the slope past the old plots. The town has a saying for it already, taken off the man's own lips: "Everyone in Hollowmere confesses to you sooner or later." Some of them come out lighter. Some come out the same and go back the next day.

Those he has brought over walk the lanes after dark in threes, and carry no lantern. They say they see well enough together. I have watched them from this window go past the pump in the black, three abreast, sure-footed as men in daylight, like a lamp split three ways that still gives the same light. The Company asks me, by wire, what the Lamplighter's scrip is for, if the faithful need no lanterns. I have answered that the wolves are not faithful.

**Of the Ash.** At the pump there is a whisper that somebody is already measuring him for a coffin, and waiting to wear his robes. I file it as gossip. Everything in HollowMere starts as gossip. Most of it stays there.

### Monday 20 November

**Of the Light.** Maud went out in the back car at Dusk. The Vicar walked behind it as far as the end of the platform and stood there until the car was out of the lamp's reach. Mrs Grieves's book has him paying for a Liquid Courage for Len the same night.

Bar's Liquid Courage is the one thing in town that keeps a man proof against any prayer, the Vicar's or anyone's, for about two minutes by my clock. The Vicar buys it for others. I have never known what to enter for that.

He sits with whoever cannot pay. He takes his tea black, because milk costs scrip and he will not run a line at the Ovens. He asks me the time whenever he passes, and sets his watch, and I have stopped thinking it strange.

**Of the Ash.** The first *lapsed*. A Pump Row name I had entered as L in October did not come to the line this week, or last. I asked nothing. I moved the mark. The Company's own codes for this are three words, *sown*, *doubt*, *lapsed*, and I have begun to use them because they fit in a wire.

**Wired east.** LIGHT 31 STOP LAPSED 1 STOP

### Thursday 7 December

**Of the Light.** The Gazette came in sealed and was opened here as always. Its notice reads: "The Vicar preaches at dusk." I went, the first time I have gone. It was him. The watch, the open hands, a voice that did not need to be raised. He spoke about debts forgiven, and I could not tell whether he was looking at me. Afterward he came by the counter for the time and set the watch. He said the door of the confessional was open. He said it the way you say the weather.

I did not confess.

**Of the Ash.** The Undertaker's book has taken four since Maud: Elsie on the 25th, Mabel on the 27th, Cyril on the 29th, Hattie on the 2nd. I do not count the dead. I note them, because their roofs change. The Watchman has been fined and stood down since Hattie. Nobody walks the lanes at night now but the threes.

## Book II. The Doubt

### Wednesday 13 December

The wire came at noon: MR THORNE ARRIVING 14TH STOP. I aired the study above the bank, as I am told to. The town says that whoever airs that room knows first, and is not saying. I have never said.

### Thursday 14 December

The Baron came on the morning train, private car, dogs first and his escort after, *Not counted (Rule 6)*. He stepped down onto the platform at the Station Rush, in the press of the crew and the mailbags and the dogs, and there on the boards the Baron fell.

I was at this window. I will not enter how. The Constable has entered *person unknown*. The town has entered a dozen names in a dozen voices at the pump, and I enter none of them. There is talk at the bell of a vote on whoever did it, if whoever did it is ever stood up. The crepe for Vane is still on the rail. They have put another length beside it.

He was carried up to the study I had aired. His escort did not go back on the train. They stand on the square in their long coats and look at the bank, and wait to be told something.

**Of the Light.** The Vicar went up to the study with his candle and sat there an hour. He came down and asked me the time.

**Of the Ash.** Nothing entered. I had no room in me today for the Ash.

### Friday 15 December

The bag came at the Station Rush. In it was one envelope addressed to me by my full name, which the Company has not written since it hired me. The seal is the Company's, in red. Inside is a writ from the Eastern Office, concerning the estate of the late Mr C. Thorne, and a key with a brass tag.

I have read it three times. It uses a word for me that my mother never once said aloud, though she had reason to. It is in my drawer, under the rent schedule.

**Reply.** AWAITING YOUR INSTRUCTIONS STOP

The Eastern Office has never before awaited anything of mine.

*Margin, pencil:* The tag is for the study door.

### Sunday 17 December

The third Sunday of Advent. The telegraph room is shut on a Sunday. I was here regardless, at the drawer.

The stone that morning carried no name. It carried a title: THE VICAR. Agnes read it to the Row at dawn as she reads them all, and the Row went to its doors and looked up the slope at the chapel. I entered it under *Stone*. I do not enter whether the stone cuts the name or only knows it first. Nobody has watched it cut.

At four he knocked on the glass. He had seen my lamp. He asked me the time, for the evening service, and set his watch, and said the pink candle was the one for joy, and that he had always thought it the wrong colour for it. Then he went up the slope in the last of the light with his hands in his coat.

That was the last time. I know the hour because I was here on a Sunday, reading a writ, and for no better reason.

### Tuesday 19 December

**Of the Light.** The Vicar has preached both evenings since, as advertised. This morning he came to the counter for the parish bag and stood where he always stands, in the robes he always wears, with his voice. He did not ask the time. I waited with my hand on the clock-case, and he did not ask. He took the bag and went.

The town has ruled the stone wrong. It named the Vicar and the Vicar is preaching. Agnes says it is the first time. I have entered the ruling as the town's, not mine.

**Of the Ash.** *Sown.* I do not know what else to write.

### Wednesday 20 December

In the night of the 17th, more than one roof dreamt the same thing and told it at the pump in the morning as though it were weather. They dreamt a lavender field on wind-bent hills. They dreamt a lone orange tree, near, standing over it. Behind it there was a sky so blue it hurt to tell. Nobody in the Territory has seen lavender. Nobody in HollowMere has seen a blue sky since the fog came off the mere.

On Monday morning a paper was handed across my counter by someone who would not sign for it and whom I did not look at, because I was reading the writ. On it, in a plain hand:

> "In the end I sought nothing else but to serve God. And serve him I did. With this death's last breath I pray this prayer: may the Almighty grant me the strength of ten in the next life. And in return, tenfold my own."

A second copy, word for word, was in the mailbag when the Postmaster cut it open. The bag had come sealed from the east.

Some say these were the end of his last sermon, at Sunday dusk. Some say they were his last breath, and not in the chapel. I enter both, and the Vicar is still preaching.

### Thursday 21 December

**Of the Light, falling.** I went to the dusk sermon again. It had the same cadence and the same open hands, and every promise was turned inside out like a coat pocket. Debts were not forgiven; they were called in. I did not confess. I was not asked to.

Since Monday the confessional has given back less than it takes. People go in entered L and come out with nothing in them, blank, sitting on the chapel step with their hands in their laps. I have moved eleven marks this week, name by name, and each one cost me the ink of a single stroke. One man from Pump Row, prayed for by both sides inside a week, came out of it snapped. He carries a rusted pitchfork now and both faiths cross the square to keep clear of him.

**Of the Ash: the keeper.** The town has done what the Constable would not, and said it: the Vicar is dead, and what walks in his robes walks only as long as the body is not found. So the body is not found. Where it has been is a catalogue the pump keeps for me without my asking. The chapel loft. A barrel in a saloon back room. A pine thicket at the Gallows Wood edge. The cave. Mrs Grieves's book has her oil barrel a gallon short in riot week, and I note it here only because the pump does, and because that was before.

Bone chimes ring where nothing hangs. The Detective walks toward them with his case file under his arm, and they ring once before he reaches the place, and there is nothing there but the place. Whoever keeps the body leaves no drag in the mud. In this town that is the strangest thing I have entered.

**The lead.** The Undertaker's requisition for sheet lead is on the incoming manifest, in his hand. *Filled / Unfilled:* not entered.

## Book III. The Dark Positions

### Friday 22 December. The collector

Rent day. I went out at the Midday Shift with the cashbox and the keys, and was cornered three times.

At Crumb's Ovens a man stood in the doorway, and Tamsin Crumb paid twice: once into my box and once into his hand. At the Lantern Bar did not reach under the counter. He looked at the man and then at me and poured nothing. In the lane behind the bank, the man took me by the coat. Brass across the knuckles knocked the Company's keys out of my hand into the mud. For the length of a held breath I could not open my own cashbox. I stood there with my hands on it like a man at a locked door in his own house. I entered the sum taken before I entered the bruise.

The mobs began that night. They are men with torches and hoods made of flour sacks, and they go out after Curfew to find the ones who walk in threes. Walking together was what let the faithful see in the dark. Now it is what marks them. By Saturday they had stopped walking together.

A witness was taken by the collar on the square. That night there was cause to ring the bell, and it was not rung, and nobody who could have rung it will say what the cause was.

One the mob found was a woman of Mill Row who minds the mill hands' children through the Shift. She had come over to the Light because the threes walked her home past the Gallows Wood edge, and she had not been walked home by anyone in years. She is alive. She keeps her door now. She still sets out three cups at Dusk, and I am told she washes all three in the morning, though only one has been used.

### Monday 25 December

Rent due, as on every Monday. The Company does not keep Christmas. I went out with the box. Nobody tried my coat. Where a roof was empty, a roof of the Ash whose sleepers walk at Dusk and do not pay, I wrote *forfeit*, as the schedule allows and as I have never before written. The schedule allowed it before. Nobody had been minded to.

The Station's mark is on my own wall, beside the door I come in by. It is ash and something darker, a sign I have no word for, and it was there on Christmas Eve when I came down to the drawer. I scrubbed it with lye. By morning it was there again, with the brush marks of my own scrubbing showing under it.

The tracts come in the mailbags. They come into this room, where the Postmaster holds envelopes over the kettle every morning and I sit six feet away at the key, and I cannot say which hand slips them in. They come in the crates of flour that go up to Crumb's. I will not copy one into this book. A tract is a page that reads like a promise and is shaped like a debt.

The ones the tracts take are the Entranced. They pay no rent and sleep by day. At Dusk they walk out without lanterns, silent, into the Fenwood past the drowned lantern, which is lit — by whom, not entered — or down the Tin Gallery mouth to the Flooded Gallery. Each one brings another the next night. I watch them from this window and count them as they pass the pump, and the count is only what is gone: empty bunks, unswept steps, a cold stove on Pump Row.

The A column has numbers in it at last, and every one of them is a subtraction.

### Tuesday 26 December. The shepherd

Nobody has seen this one. The pump calls them *the one who will carry on*.

Black candles stand on the VANE TIN CO crate in the Flooded Gallery, set out as on an altar. The Detective has been down and come up and said so to the Constable, and the Constable to me, because I keep the wire. At St Brannock's the pulpit has been fouled. The hymn board's cards are gone, the alms dish is gone, and the Vicar's candle is gone from its stick.

The law the pump whispers is this. If the robes fall, the shepherd keeps the power to turn souls. The Ash has insured itself, as the Company insures a train.

## Book IV. The Return

### Friday 29 December

The morning train. Conductor Pruett in the door of the private car, punch in hand, and then not using it: *Not counted (Rule 6)*. A man stepped down into the fog of the Station Rush with the dead Vicar's face. The light stood on him the way low sun stands on still water, too level and too bright, and the fog drew back from it in a ring. He carried a crosier of plain black iron, as tall as himself.

He crossed the square to this counter, and put one hand on the rail, and asked me the time.

I told him. My pen hand would not hold the nib. He took out a watch, the same watch or its twin, and set it to my clock, and thanked me as if I had given him something, and went out.

There are two Vicars in HollowMere. One wears the robes and preaches at dusk. One wears the light. I entered both under St Brannock's, and struck one, and restored it. The column cannot say which. Neither can I.

**Tenfold.** He does not hear confessions one at a time. He stands in a lane and the lane comes over. Mill Row went over in an afternoon: wash-house, wheel-house, every door down to the mere. The row I had entered at noon as *1 L* I entered again at three as *10 L*. The pencil in the margin was there before me each time.

**The crosier.** The wing doors at the manor stood locked under Horace's ring. He struck them once and they stood open, and Horace stood beside them holding keys that had no more work to do. The Undercroft door the same. A torch-mob came at him across the square at Curfew and broke, as a wave does on a post, and went back into the lanes, and nobody was hurt. I enter that too.

**Wired east.** LIGHT UNCOUNTABLE STOP REQUEST INSTRUCTION STOP

**Reply.** INSTRUCTIONS ARE YOURS SIR STOP

### Saturday 30 December, Dusk

It can be done once. The pump knew that before I did.

The man in the robes was on the chapel step at dusk, hands open, and the square filled to hear him. The man in the light came up from the platform with the crosier. He did not speak. He raised it.

The light came down on the square in a pillar from nowhere I could see. It was white as the inside of a lamp-glass and as wide as the pump. It was not on him. It was on the step. The robes went off the man standing there as if a wind had taken them, and went over the rail and down the slope empty.

Under them was a face, and the face was borrowed.

It was a face from this town. I have taken its rent. The one it belongs to was standing beside me at the window, come in from the cold to watch, and made a sound I will not enter.

I enter no name. The face's owner did nothing. Whoever wore it went down the step into the crowd and the crowd let go of them, and the fog took them before the pillar went out.

The body has still not been reported. The pump has the rule ready: once the robes have fallen, the shepherd's power stays. Robes were seen at the Gallery mouth an hour after Curfew. I cannot say they were the same robes. I cannot say how many robes there are.

There is one *—* left in HollowMere, and it is under this roof. Both faiths have come to my door, the one with an open hand and the one with brass across it. So has the Company. The Light cannot say it holds every roof while one is entered as neither. I know what that makes me tonight. I do not write it.

On the desk is a form printed back east for a time like this one. It locks every house door in HollowMere at Curfew, by order, the escort to see it kept. It wants a signature and a date. It would keep the Entranced in their beds. It would keep the faithful off the square. It has my signature. The date is not entered.

Dusk. Below, the faithful are gathering on the scorched stones where the pillar stood, close, shoulder to shoulder, carrying no lanterns. Along the far side of the square the Entranced are walking toward the Gallery mouth, without a sound, one behind another. From this window I count them as they pass the pump.

One. Two. Three. Four. Five.

The fog has come off the mere and taken the end of the line. I cannot see a sixth. I cannot see that there is no sixth.

I am a man of columns. The columns are mine now; I have not said so and will not. The column your name is in at dawn is who you are.

The bell has begun.
