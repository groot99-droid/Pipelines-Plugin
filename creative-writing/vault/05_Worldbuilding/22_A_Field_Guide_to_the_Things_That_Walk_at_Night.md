# A Field Guide to the Things That Walk at Night

A FIELD GUIDE
TO THE THINGS THAT WALK AT NIGHT

*at HollowMere, at the end of the Thorne & Expanse Line,
Coldwater Territory*

*with plates drawn from life where life allowed*

T. CALLOWAY

AUTUMN, 1893

[ For the Society's stamp: ________________ ]

---

## To the Reader

I drew the plates in my father's books for twenty-one years, and he signed them. Birds of the Eastern Shore. Mammals of the Northern Counties. The Lesser Moths. When people praised the moths they praised him, and he let them. That was our arrangement, and I do not complain of it here. He died in April. The Panic took his publisher in May. In June a society back east advertised a fee for one plate of the Black Pine wolf, "from life, with a pelt". I answered as T. Calloway, and they wrote back *Dear Sir*. The fee would keep the house.

I came in on the train of Monday, 2 October. Nobody comes to the end of the line unless they are running from something or hunting for it. I was told that on the platform before I had my bag down. I said I was hunting. The man who told me did not ask what.

I lodge in the lean-to behind Old Fennick's light, at the marsh edge below the Fenwood. I pay the rent in oil. When I bring him the can he takes it and tells me its price, to show me that he knows. My lantern is Harbourmaster Pell's. I got it on his terms, which he states to everyone the same way: "The skiff leaks. Bring me resin and a plank of timber and I will lend you a lantern." I carry my father's pocket watch. I have no gun and want none.

The method of this guide is simple. I watch, count and draw, and I write down what the town says beside what I saw, because here the two are kept in the same book. Each entry gives the creature's range, its habit, its sign, what it fears and what the town says. Each ends on a dread, because in HollowMere everything has one, and the town will tell you what it is before it tells you the creature's name.

Where I have not seen a thing, the plate stays empty.

---

## The Mere and the Fenwood

### Plate I. The Mere-wolf, also called the Black Pine Wolf

**Range.** Gallows Wood, at the near edge where the planks start, and the Fenwood beyond it. They keep to loops in the forest, the same ground walked again and again, as a watchman walks his lanes. At dusk they come to the fence round the square.

**Habit.** Dusk and night. A wolf runs at a living person it can sense and can also see. It does not run at the dead or the hidden, or at a sound alone. The run is short. I have timed it on my father's watch from the end of the planks eleven times: six seconds, never seven. Then the wolf stops, wherever it is, and goes home, as if it had been called and had heard it.

They hunt the edge of the light and never cross it. Mere-rot makes them bold. In the fog weeks they stand at the fence closer together and longer, and they do not turn their heads when the bell goes.

**Sign.** Fur caught on the bark at shoulder height. Claw marks, on wood and on what they kill. No footprints that anyone has kept: the mud takes them and gives them back as water. When they kill, the body is left where it fell. They do not drag it far. They do not hide it. There is one scream, and the town has learned to know it from the other screams.

**What it fears.** A lit flame near at hand: a carried lantern, a torch, a candle, a burning post or a fire. A lit window does not turn them. Moonlight does not. The pale glow on the rotten stumps of the Fenwood does not. It has to be fire, and it has to be close. I tested nothing. I only stood still with mine lit and watched them go round me like water round a stone.

**What the town says.**
- That the six seconds is a leash. The town argues over who holds the other end: the den, or the Gamekeeper, or the dead man whose will is read in the manor. Nobody settles it.
- That children count to six at the fence. I have heard them at it, under the lantern over the chalk.
- That a lantern turned down is quieter to the eye. So the ones going out to do what should not be done go out dark, and take their chance with the wolves.
- That a body in the wood is the wolves'. Twice this autumn the town said wolves. Twice the Doctor's book said knife, and a thread was lifted off a door frame near where the body lay. Wolves do not open doors. The town knows this and says wolves all the same, because it is easier to have the wolves at the fence than a neighbour at the door.

*Dread: the lantern going out.*

**Plate.** Drawn right-handed, at the fence, in the second week of October, by my own lantern. Three wolves, one of them looking at the light and not at me.

**Addendum. Tuesday, 31 October.**

He came in along the ties from Gallows Wood Siding the week before, walking. He was one of the men the Panic puts on the line, going west because east had shut. He sat on the rail beside me one afternoon while I drew crows. He asked what I was drawing, and I showed him. He told me his name was Owen. He showed me a twist of newspaper with larkspur seed in it. He said it was for a garden somewhere it stops. He meant the walking.

He walked out along the ties again at dusk on the 31st, with no light.

At first light: the throat, open. The coat in strips on the brush along the cut, a few yards off, grey wool on the thorns at the height of a man's chest. One boot off. The foot bare and white, and the toes curled. The legs dragged off the ties into the bracken by a body's length and no further, as if whatever did it had been called home before it finished. The face untouched. The eyes open. The seed twist dry in his coat pocket.

I took the seed. I do not know why, and I will not pretend here that I do.

He was not the first stranger the wolves had off the line this autumn. The back car took another in October. Owen was not on the Grant. There is no line for him in anybody's book but this one.

**Addendum. The night of Saturday, 4 November, to Sunday, 5 November. The specimen.**

The Ranger brought it in at the wood's edge with her bow. She will not take a specimen fee and did not ask for one. The Gamekeeper had it on a trestle in Hale Barn by dark, and he gave me the night with it, to measure and to draw, with the goat watching from the end stall. At first light he would have the pelt.

Measurements, nose to root of tail and at the shoulder, are entered on the back of this plate. Larger than any wolf in my father's book. The gums black. The teeth worn on the left side, as if it chewed on one side like an old man. A torn ear, healed. The bolt-hole behind the shoulder, small and clean.

It had been opened at the belly before it came to me. I was not told by whom, and I did not ask. In the stomach: grey hair that was not its own, long, matted with what the stomach does. Bone in pieces too small to name. A horn button, four-holed, like the ones on Owen's coat.

I have written *like*, and I will stop there.

He kept the pelt. It is on the door of Hale Barn now, nailed through the forelegs, head down. The nails are old and the pelt is not. So the Society will have no pelt, and the fee asked for a pelt.

The specimen sketch, overleaf, is the first plate in this book drawn with my left hand. See Plate III.

**Addendum. Wednesday, 6 December.**

A wolf on the square before dawn, at the pump, inside the fence, in fog. The Watchman had the Ranger's bow by then, and he spent the one white bolt there. A woman of Pump Row was bitten on the forearm before the bolt went in. She lives. The Gamekeeper hung the wolf from the beam on the gallows. Mere-rot makes them bold. I had written that line in October as the town's. I write it now as mine.

### Plate II. The Forest Stalker

**Range.** The Fenwood and the cave mouths, at night.

**Habit.** I cannot say whether the Forest Stalker is a kind of animal or a habit of the wolves. The town names it separately, so I enter it separately. A Stalker follows a track, not a walker, and it follows only an unlit one. A lantern ends it, the town says, the way a lantern ends the wolf. My own track was followed once, on the night of 14 October, when my lantern went out on the planks and I walked home by the sound of the water. In the morning there were prints laid over mine in the mud, all the way to Fennick's door. Larger than mine. Four-toed. They did not come up the step.

**Sign.** Prints laid over a person's prints. Clothing shredded and left on the brush, at the height of a man's chest. I do not say that Owen's coat was the Stalkers'. I say that is what the town says it leaves.

**What it fears.** The same as the wolf, the town says. I have not seen it fear anything, because I have not seen it.

**What the town says.** That a Stalker is a wolf that has learned to be patient. That it is not a wolf.

*Dread: your own track behind you.*

**Plate.** Drawn right-handed, from the mud below Fennick's step, the morning of 15 October. A track, and nothing standing in it.

### Plate III. The Boar

**Range.** The wood's edge and the Fenwood. Day and dusk. It is the one creature in this book I have met at noon.

**Habit.** Rooting along the plank-path for what the frost has not got to. Wallowing in the drained channels. Tusk-scoring the bark of the black pines at knee height, the same trees again and again.

**Sign.** Turned ground. Wallows. The scored bark, pale where it is fresh.

It wounds and never kills. I can enter that from life.

On Thursday, 19 October, at the edge of Gallows Wood, it came out of the brush while I was drawing it. Its head went into me low, and then it was gone back into the brush, and I was sitting in the mud holding my right arm against me. The tusk opened the forearm from the wrist nearly to the elbow, and something in the hand stopped answering. Doc Mallory closed the arm at the apothecary bench that evening and told me to keep it still and dry, as if anything here were dry. The arm has closed. Two fingers and the thumb answer. The other two do not, and the hand will not hold a pencil to a line.

From here on every plate in this guide is marked *drawn left-handed*. The reader will see why without the mark.

My father signed that hand for twenty-one years. I had thought it was the one thing of his I would keep.

**What the town says.** That the boar is the one honest animal in the wood, because it wants nothing from you.

*Dread: the noise in the brush that is not a wolf.*

**Plate.** Unfinished. Drawn right-handed, and the line stops at the shoulder.

### Plate IV. The Bat

**Range.** The galleries: the Tin Gallery mouth, and the Flooded Gallery under the Fenwood.

**Habit.** Out at dusk in a stream from the mouth, low over the fen. Back before the light.

**Sign.** Droppings on the timbers of the mouth and across the lid of a VANE TIN CO crate. A bite now and then on a hand that went where they hang.

It wounds and never kills.

**What it fears.** Nothing I could find.

**What the town says.** That the tinners' hands are bitten and they say nothing of it, because the mine is shut and their hands are not.

I drew it at the mouth and did not go further, because a woman who cannot hold a lantern and a pencil at the same time does not go into the hill.

*Dread: the roof moving.*

**Plate.** Drawn left-handed, at the mouth, 26 October. Wings open. The line is poor, and the wings are better than the face.

---

### Plate V. The Rat

**Range.** The Undercroft under St Brannock's. The galleries past the Tin Gallery mouth. The attics of the Row. I have seen it in all three.

**Habit.** By night, and by day where it is dark enough to be night. In company.

**Sign.** Gnawed sacking. Droppings along the foot of a wall. A bite on an ankle.

It wounds and never kills.

**What it fears.** The light, briefly. The cat at the Drowned Lantern, not at all.

**What the town says.** That it is one colony running under the hill, from the chapel's cellar to the galleries. I enter that as said and not as seen.

*Dread: the sound under the floor.*

**Plate.** Drawn left-handed, in Fennick's lean-to, from one that came in for the candle-end and was not quick enough. I let it go afterward. It had been patient with me.

### Plate VI. The Bog-wisp

**Range.** The marsh, low over the fen. The pools in the caves.

**Habit.** Lights low over the fen that are not lanterns. They lead walkers off the path and in. They move when you move and stand when you stand. They are never where the plank goes.

**Sign.** None that can be collected. One that can be counted: a carried lantern near one goes dim, as if it had looked away, for five ticks of my father's watch. Anyone watching sees nothing until it looks back.

**What it fears.** Nothing. It is the thing that is followed.

**What the town says.**
- That they show lights too, and they lure.
- That a man with a book under his arm went into the Fenwood at dusk after a light, and did not come out the side he went in.

I lodge behind the one light on the fen that is not theirs. Old Fennick will tell you which light is his, and one of them is steady. Some nights I have asked him. Some nights I am not sure he answered the same way.

*Dread: following.*

**Plate.** Drawn left-handed, from Fennick's step, the night of 9 November. Two small lights over black water. I cannot say which one is Fennick's.

## Field and town

### Plate VII. The Crow

**Range.** The gallows at the bell. The ridges of the roofs. St Brannock's tower.

**Habit.** In company, and loud. They go up when someone runs below them, so in HollowMere running is being seen. The town has learned to walk.

**Sign.** Noise. They are the first to a thing that cannot get up.

On Tuesday, 28 November, the Tribunal hanged a man at the bell. The crows had his eyes by the second morning. The drop had stretched the neck long, and the head lay over on the shoulder as if he were listening for something behind him. He had mended my boots in October, both heels, and would not take scrip for the second one, because he said the first had been badly done by whoever did it before him. I drew the crows on the crossbar and not him.

In December they had a wolf on the beam as well, and went to its eyes first, the same.

**What it fears.** Nothing in the town.

**What the town says.** That the crows know the stone's name before Pump Row does.

*Dread: being seen to run.*

**Plate.** Drawn left-handed, from the Tribunal bench under its tarpaulin, the morning of 30 November. Seven crows on a beam. The beam is ruled. The crows are not good.

---

### Plate VIII. The Gull

**Range.** The marsh dock and the channels, following Tuck's ferry.

**Habit.** Gulls follow his ferry out and do not follow it back. I have stood at the dock and watched it eight times, and it is true every time. They go out over the fen with the pole and do not come in again. Where they go I cannot say. The ferry comes back alone.

**Sign.** White on the posts of the dock. The cry.

**What it fears.** Nothing I could find.

**What the town says.** That they smell the sea on something. Tuck says they follow the pole, not the boat. A gull this far inland is a wrong thing in a book like my father's, and I have entered it as one.

*Dread: a gull this far from the sea.*

**Plate.** Drawn left-handed, from the end of the dock, 13 November. Two gulls over the ferry. Tuck, at the pole, in outline only. He asked not to be drawn and I left his face off.

## Everywhere the fen breathes

### IX. Mere-rot

*No plate.*

Mere-rot is not a creature. I enter it because the town enters it, and because it is in every entry above. It is the town's word. The Company's word is miasma. They mean the same thing.

**Range.** Everywhere the fen breathes.

**Sign.** The town knows it by its symptoms:
- the wolves grow bold;
- footsteps sound where nobody walks;
- the cough comes.

I have the first from the fence. I have the second from the planks below Fennick's: short steps behind me in fog, and nobody there when I turn the lantern on them. I have the third from the last week of November, in my own chest.

Mother Moll at her charcoal hut gave me herbs for it on her terms: "Four sprigs of herb, dear. The fog is in everyone's chest." I brought her the four sprigs and she made it into something for me. It helps at night. It does not help at dawn.

**What the town says.** That the rot wakes things.

*Dread: the fog in everyone's chest.*

## The last page

**Saturday, 16 December.**

Fennick has gone down to the Lantern to fill his can, as he does on Saturdays. The light is mine to watch until he is back. I have entered the town's creatures in the town's form. The last entry is in the same form.

**T. Calloway: at the light, at the marsh edge.**

**Range.** The planks, the lean-to, the fence, the end of the dock.

**Habit.** Counts to six on a watch that was her father's.

**Sign.** Plates drawn left-handed.

**What she fears.**

**What the town says.** Nothing yet.

Losses, entered:
- The hand. Two fingers and the thumb.
- The pelt, which is on the barn door, and with it the fee. The box on the title page is still empty.
- The cough.

Kept, entered:
- Owen's larkspur seed, still in its twist of newspaper, between Plates I and II.

One fare east is folded into the back board of this book. I bought it this morning with the last of the scrip. The train leaves at dusk.

*Dread: the platform at dusk.*
