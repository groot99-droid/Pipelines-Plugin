# Mrs Grieves's Tab Book

---

## Flyleaf

**THORNE & EXPANSE LINE**
**~~RUSTY~~ LANTERN SALOON**, *with* DROWNED *written over the strike in ink*
**ACCOUNTS RECEIVABLE**
*All accounts in Company Scrip, at the morning rate.*

Under the print, in her hand:

> Not all accounts.
> Fourth book. Opened Monday, 16 October 1893. Kept by me.

---

## House Rules

1. I open a tab. Nobody opens one by asking.
2. No tab for anyone the Clerk has entered Indebted. That holds after noon. Before noon I have not heard.
3. A tab closes three ways: paid, the back car, or me.
4. A closed tab stays closed. It does not pass to the wife, the lodger or the Company. The Company says an account survives a man. A tab is not an account.
5. Nobody drinks on the barman's line.
6. Nobody leaves after the whistle who has far to go. There are rooms upstairs. The Company sets the price of them.
7. What is said over Loose Lips stays in this book. The Gazette can find its own.

---

## The Pours

**Rotgut: ¼ a pour.**
The cheapest thing on the train and the commonest thing in the glass. Four cases a day, when the train is honest. Men drink it after the Shift and do not talk.
*What a day costs.*

**Liquid Courage: ¾ a pour.**
Drunk before something: before the Clerk, before the Bell, before the ticket window. Nobody drinks a second one. A second one is Rotgut, whatever the bottle says.
*What a door costs.*

**Loose Lips Lager: ½ a pour.**
It comes up in the same car as the whiskey and tastes of the trip. What is said over it is said to me, then to the room, and by morning it has a man's name on it and a price somebody would pay. I don't buy it. I don't sell it. I write down that it was said.
*What a name costs.*

The price of a pour does not move. The rate on the board does.

> Arthur. His brother puts a Rotgut on the closed line every week, in Arthur's name, and drinks it himself. I take the scrip. Some weeks he writes a letter to his Ma at the end of my bar. I don't look to see whose name he signs.

> Jack Rudd brought me a bottle off the whiskey and called it a tariff. The train will have a case down as short today. I know which case. Settled.

> Stan calls it a cold. It has been a cold since the thaw. Liquid Courage is not for a cough, and he knows it. He says it works on the part of him that minds.

> Ivy Cotter sat over her glass and did not touch it till near the whistle. Her fare was in a twist of paper, and she counted it out on my bar twice and would not spend a coin of it. She sews for the mill. Evenings she sits in the dark to save the wick. I put her pour against the house. Don't tell them.

> Maud darned Bar's cuff and the pocket of my apron. She will not take a pour as a gift. She takes it as a wage. Paid.

> Ernie asked Clem where the train goes after us. Clem reads everything and did not know.

> Wilf played for his pour tonight like any night, key of G. If he'd been up the hill to look, he did not say. Nobody says.

> The new Vicar came off yesterday's train and was in here by tonight. He drinks nothing. He bought three Liquid Courage for three men who each wanted one and could not run to it, and he sat a while with each. Everyone confesses to him sooner or later. At my bar, sooner.

> Dora Finch read my sums upside down across the bar, the way she does, through her spectacles mended with thread. She found a quarter too much on Len's line and made me strike it. Len owes less because of her.

> Frank said a thing about the girl Nell's fever. I will not write it in my own book. Bar had him out the door before I had my hand off the tap. A stool went, and so did one of Frank's teeth. Frank's line stays open. He owes me ½ and a stool.

> Wednesday, Mr Thorne again, dogs first again. Bar cleaned the sawed-off on the counter while the dogs went past the shutters. He did not look at them. They looked at him.

## The Back of the Book

### Behind the Bar

Six frames. My pencil goes under each one, where the glass stops.

1. A man in an island jersey with a round loaf under his arm. *Sailor. Port-au-Levain. He came over with Mr Vane's people, and drowned at sea back there before any of them came. Mrs Crumb hung him.*
2. Four men in a flat boat on the lake. *Not sailors. Lake men, from before the drain. They called themselves sailors and I let them. Two went into the mere after it was a mere.*
3. An empty frame. *Jory Kitto. Tinner. Nobody ever took his likeness. He drowned under a lake that was there and then was not.*
4. A young man in a stiff collar. *Not a sailor. Nobody. The face came in the frame from back east. People leave sprigs under it all the same.*
5. The barman, with the sign lantern on its hook beside him. *Never saw a sea. Drowned all the same.*
6. A face I don't know, water-stained at the corner. *I did not hang this one. Nobody owns to it. It was on the wall one morning, square, with no nail I had put there. It looks like nobody I know yet.*

**Sailors:** one. **Drowned:** all.
*I'm not sure of the one. I count the sixth as drowned because it is on that wall.*

### The Back Room

The oil barrel. A filling is ½, at the posted rate.

| Who | Fillings | |
|---|---|---|
| The Lamplighter | One each dusk | For the square. Hers, then the wolves'. |
| Old Fennick | One on Saturdays | For his light at the marsh edge. That light. Not the other. |
| Sundry | By the book | |

> Dip, Monday 13th: 31 gallons. Sold by the book since then: six fillings, which is a gallon and a half. Dip, Wednesday 15th: 28½. A gallon short. Nobody owns to it. I have written it as spill. I will go on writing it as spill.

### The Barman's Line

*[a small lantern, drawn in ink]*

He opened the line himself, in his own hand, the first winter the house was a house. He paid it every week. There was only ever one thing on it he did not pay: on the night he went into the mere with the sign lantern in his arms, one Liquid Courage, on the slate. It was the only Liquid Courage he ever put on his own line.

It is not closed. A tab closes three ways. He did not pay it. The back car did not take him, because there was nothing to send. And I have not closed it. I carry it to the top of every page. Nobody drinks on it.

At dusk, when he trimmed the sign wick, he hummed the same hymn, and never all the way through.

> Carried forward: one pour, and half a hymn.

> Tuck says the lantern in the Fenwood is lit again. I do not ask who.

### Bar's Line

Bar keeps the sawed-off under the counter and his own line in my book, like anybody. This book has him two ways. In October he had Frank out the door. On the Tuesday he stood the crew a round for the strike and let the back room to men who bury nobody. On the Wednesday he took two shells off the train and cleaned the gun while the dogs went by. He has not told me which way he is going. I have written both down.

> Maud went out in the back car at Dusk. The Vicar walked behind it. Bar carried one end.

> The Company filed its Articles on Friday, at a quarter to twelve or near it. Clem read me the part where an account survives a man: it goes to the household, then to the lodgers, then to them. Maud had no household and lodged with nobody. She was square with me, and I have written her closed. That is the end of what anybody gets of Maud from this book.

> Bar has the sawed-off under the counter. He has not said which way. Nor have I.

> Low water at two minutes past nine. It wants a quarter to it now.

> Somebody has come in off the square after the whistle, from far enough away. New line. Name to follow.

*The line against your name at closing is who you are.*

*We are not closed.*
