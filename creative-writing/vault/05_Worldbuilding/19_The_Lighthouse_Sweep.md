# The Lighthouse Sweep

*The Sweep-Book of the Tower on the Reef. Kept by the Tide-Watcher of the Guild, under the Council's Ordinance of the Landward Turn. The night of the second to the third of December, 1788. Tide-Watcher: P. Goulet.*

> *Of the Landward Turn.* The lamp keeps the reef. At each of the four watches of the night the Tide-Watcher shall turn it landward and sweep the town once, from east to west. Any soul the beam finds abroad after the proofing bell without a registered seal shall be logged, and the Iron-Wardens shall attend to the log.
> *Rider.* Bearings three hundred to three hundred and twenty, being the houses of the cliff road, are dipped by subscription.
> — Ordinances of the Sovereign Sourdough Council, as painted inside the tower door

---

## Of the Tower

**The Tower.** It stands on the reef point, south of the town, on the last rock that is still the island. Its work is the reef. Ships come in by the light and the drowned go out by it. Four times a night, by ordinance, it turns its back on the sea and looks at the people instead.

**The Lamp.** Oil, burned in a row of wicks before copper mirrors, polished every watch. A clockwork turns the frame so that the light walks round the reef. A weight on a chain drives the clockwork, and the weight is wound by hand. For the Landward Turn the clockwork is unlatched and the frame is cranked round by the keeper's own arm, slowly, east to west, at the pace the ordinance calls *a walking pace for the eye*.

**The Office.** The Tide-Watcher is a Guild office, flour-white. It keeps the beacon, the customs ledger of incoming cargo, and the ferry tally of who leaves. It is paid in Guild Copper by the night, less the yeast-tax on the keeper's own platter in the Hall. The Council's registers note that the office *may become, under the Council's need*, one of two other things. One is the **Wreck-Looter**, who knows what the fog does to a manifest. The other is the **Harbor Master**, who holds the gangplank and deputizes dockhands when the Crown's man comes. No Tide-Watcher chooses which. The need chooses.

**The Predecessor.** The keeper before this one let the light fail on a wreck night. He was made Croissant Rassis, the stale one, and his seal on the tower door was scratched through with a nail and left there, so that the next keeper would have to pass it every time she came up the stair. She has not missed a turn in eleven years.

**The Colours under the Beam.** From the tower no face can be read at any distance, but rank can.

| Colour | Whose | What the beam does with it |
|---|---|---|
| gold leaf | the Gallery | throws it back; the tower sees a second light |
| flour-white | the Guild | glows |
| red crust | the Commons | shows black |
| burlap | the Abbey of the Crust | drinks it, gives nothing back |

The island can be read entire from the reef point without one name in it. The Council considers this the beam's chief virtue.

## The First Sweep: Dusk, the Seating

*The Morning Ferry is long gone. The fog has not come. The beam is long and clean.*

**Bearing 075: Smuggler's Wharf & the Salt-Flats**

**Held by:** the Commons, by night; the customs shed by the Tide-Watcher's own office, flour-white, by day and by ledger.

**In the beam:** Dockhands stacking crates on the wharf that the customs ledger has entered as empty. Their coats show black. Out on the Flats a salt-raker, **Annick**, is working the last of the light before the tide turns. She rakes with her husband's rake, its handle worn pale in two places by his hands, and she holds it lower than he did, in places of her own. Among the dockhands is the keeper's brother, **Gilles**. He was not apprenticed up when she was, and his coat is the same black as the others. He lifts his cap to the tower, as he always does. He does it to the light, not knowing whether she is at it.

**Logged:** *Wharf at the seating. Crates, eleven. All lawful hours.*

**Not logged:** That she counted twelve. The cap.

The wharf is where things come ashore that the Counting-House did not count. The Flats are the Council's, and the salt raked from them is the Council's, less what the rakers are let keep, which is what sticks to their hands. In the customs shed there is a crate with a lid that has never been nailed. A silver piece is left on it, some nights, for the Master Proof-Reader. It is a custom. Whose hand keeps the custom is not entered. The dockhands say it is a way of telling the Hall that it is watched both ways.

**Bearing 050: the Counting-House**

**Held by:** the Guild.

**In the beam:** The Ledger-Clerk at the tariff desk window, counting yeast tokens into the night's columns, both hands moving. The pigeon loft on the roof, dark. On the steps, the Debtor, asleep. He sleeps there because that is where the tariff will find him first in the morning, and he would rather be found than fetched.

**Logged:** *Counting-House lit within. Lawful.*

**Not logged:** A bird came into the loft late, after the seating, when no bird should be flying. She watched it drop through the beam with something at its leg. The Tide-Watcher does not log birds. There is no column for them.

The Counting-House holds the archive, the Guild Copper and the yeast tokens. The tariff falls due every night at the proofing bell. A platter that does not pay is stamped UNREGISTERED, and an Unregistered loaf is a soul the Council has stopped feeding. The Debtor has been Unregistered since the spring. He still comes to the steps.

**Bearing 035: the Port Tavern**

**Held by:** the Guild, by licence. The Commons drink in it, which is not the same as holding it.

**In the beam:** The Hearth-Warden in the door with the iron poker across his shoulders like a yoke. In the yard, the Sot, shackled to the stone pillar until the morning bell, singing to the pillar as if it were a woman who had come back.

**Logged:** *Tavern. One at the pillar.*

**Not logged:** That she knows the song. That it is a ferry song, and the woman in it does not come back.

Here rumour is weighed before the Council weighs it. Its weights are lighter. The Hearth-Warden keeps the rum cellar under the floor, and the rum cellar is a cellar.

**Bearing 010: the Great Hearth & Hall of Platters (by the high windows)**

**Held by:** the Gallery, on the gilded platters nearest the Hearth; the Guild, in the middle rows, flour-white; the Commons, at the far end by the doors, where the draught is.

**In the beam:** The Hall from outside is a field of small lights. Nearest the fire the Gallery's platters throw the beam straight back at the tower, so that for a moment the keeper is looking into her own lamp. The middle rows glow. The far rows are dull. Several have no cloche, and the beam picks out the stamp on the silver where a cloche should be. The platters of the house of Vane sit nearest of all, their silver gone faintly gold from the heat. The Master Proof-Reader is at the scales with a loaf in each pan. Down the length of the Hall the Aboyeur carries one loaf in both hands. He calls no name. He sets it on the empty platter nearest the Hearth and steps back from it.

**Logged:** *Dusk loaf set. For a name under seal.*

**Not logged:** That she looked for her own platter, as she has every sweep for eleven years, and could not tell it from the others. Flour-white, at this distance, is one colour.

The Hearth is the oldest fire on the rock and has never been let go out. The Hall around it holds a silver platter for every soul on the island, and on every platter a Wake Loaf stamped with its owner's guild seal. A soul without a platter has no name in law. The words for the dusk loaf are the Aboyeur's own. The Tide-Watcher copies them into the book every night, as her predecessor did, and his did, and as the first book in the tower does on its first page. The book does not say what the name is. Nobody she has ever asked has known.

On the rock it is said that you are the loaf on your platter when the cloche comes off at dawn. It is said to children as a comfort.

### Between Sweeps

She latched the frame back to the reef and wound the weight. The chain went up through her hands, cold, link by link, and the clockwork took the lamp out of her keeping and walked it round the water again.

Her bread was a Guild half-loaf. She cut it into four on the sill with the customs knife, as she had cut it every night since her first winter in the tower, when she had eaten it all at the seating and been hungry by the fog. One piece for each turn. She ate the first standing.

The sou was on the sill too. It was copper, mainland money, with the King's head on it, worn down to a nose and a ribbon. It had come off a wreck in her first year, out of a drowned man's waistcoat, and it was unlawful to spend on the rock, where only Guild Copper passed. She had never tried to spend it. She kept it King-up. She could not have said why that way and not the other.

She thought about the twelfth crate. She thought about the cap. Then she wrote *eleven* again in the margin, over the first *eleven*, harder, until the nib caught.

## The Second Sweep: the Proofing Bell

*The tariff falls due. After this bell the beam is law.*

**Bearing 005: Gallows Square**

**Held by:** the Gallery, by the High Chancellor's sitting.

**In the beam:** The stage, built against the Hall's great south doors, which are shut. When they are thrown open at a judgment the Hearth can be seen through them from every corner of the Square. The stage is being dressed for a dawn sentence. Cloches stand stacked on it by size, the smallest on top. The musicians' chairs are set out in a half-moon under oilcloth, the stands already up. The Iron Cloche has been hoisted on its chain over the stage and catches the beam like a second, silent bell. On a board at the stage foot is the gallery's supper, chalked in a good hand:

*Consommé of the first catch.*
*Fowl in a crust.*
*A croustade.*
*The loaf of the day not to be served.*

The rope hangs on the old gallows at the Square's east side. It is renewed each spring, used or not. A stage-sweeper, **Loïc**, Commons, is working the boards with a broom. He is paid in what the gallery leaves on its plates, and he keeps the crusts in his shirt for his sister's children. They like the burnt edges best.

**Logged:** *Square. One abroad, licensed to the stage. Lawful.*

**Not logged:** The hour on the board. The sentence has been moved from the sixth bell to the seventh, so that the gallery may breakfast first. The crime is chalked below the hour, as the ordinance requires: *one loaf, taken without seal.* The board gives no name. It does not need one. The gallery knows who is to be lifted, and the Square will see.

Gallows Square is the second-oldest structure on the rock, after the Hearth. In these years the Gallery's justice is done here. The Morning Judgment gives three verdicts: Acquittal, a Guild Fine, or Trial by Fire in the Great Hearth. The third is given in public, with the cloches, the music, the bell, and the supper after. Those sentenced are, in the main, the island's poor, for the crimes the poor have room to commit: a crumb, a smudged seal, a platter left unpaid. The gallery has its own windows over the Hall doors and watches from there, at table. Supper is served when the bell has stopped.

**Bearing 000: the Hall Roof and its Ten Vents (the Sacred Kitchen)**

The beam cannot go underground. The ten rooms of the Sacred Kitchen lie under the Hearth, and the Tide-Watcher knows them as the beam does, by their vents: ten gratings and flues set in the Hall roof, read every proofing bell by smoke, steam, dark and cold.

- **Shaping bench.** Le Boulanger. Guild. Warm, steady. Where a loaf becomes a person's.
- **Lamination.** Le Tourrier. Guild. A thin steam, in folds. The office's disgrace is haste, and the vent has never once smoked in a hurry.
- **Command desk.** Le Chef de Cuisine and Le Sous-Chef, long since become the Council and its clerks. Gallery. A lamp, late, and the smell of wax.
- **Expediter.** The Aboyeur's calling post. Guild. Dark since dusk; he is upstairs, with the loaf.
- **Front of house.** The Critique's empty chair, which the Council keeps so that no one may sit in it. Under the display glass in the street window is the croissant the kitchen keeps for a joke and the island keeps for a relic. When the beam crosses the window it lies there hard as a rock under the display-case spotlight.
- **Hygiene room.** L'Inspecteur Sanitaire, a Crown office in name. The room is kept for the Crown's man, who has not come, and the office is kept in a ledger. The vent is cold.
- **Dish pit.** Le Plongeur, kneeling where the seal of the Champignon was ground into the floor. Commons. Steam all night, every night. Penance, by sentence or by birth.
- **Cellar.** Le Rat de Cave, keeper of the door. Commons. A breath of cold. The office's disgrace is the door.
- **Cold pantry.** Le Garde Manger. The one room in the Kitchen with its lock on the outside. No steam. Through the grating, when the frame is cranked slow enough, someone is humming the founding hymn with the verses out of order, the rest before the feeding. The person is not named in the book.
- **Sub-basement.** The door above the Vault, whose key is one of the two. The ninety-one steps go down from it. No vent; the Vault breathes for it.

**Logged:** *Vents ten. Nine drawing. Cold pantry cold.*

**Not logged:** The hymn.

**Bearing 355: the Subterranean Yeast Vault (by its plume)**

**Held by:** the Abbey of the Crust, at its altar below; the Gallery, by the feeding hand, which is the house of Vane's by its eldest line.

**In the beam:** No one. The Vault is ninety-one steps under the Hall. What the beam finds is the steam rising out of the mountain's throat behind the Hearth, a white column the light turns gold for the moment it passes.

**Logged:** *Plume broad, steady.*

**Not logged:** That she has never been down. That the plume is the only part of the Mother of Mothers anyone above ground has ever seen, and that the fishermen ask the Tide-Watcher for its width in the morning, as if it were weather.

The Mother of Mothers is four hundred years old and lives in a vat of pressurized glass, fed by the mountain's steam. The Vault has two keys and one feeding hand. The feeding hand has been a Vane hand for as long as there has been an archive to say so.

**Bearing 345: the Abbey of the Crust**

**Held by:** the Abbey, burlap.

**In the beam:** The Abbey's walls drink the light. It stands on the mountain shoulder above the Vault, where the steam vents come out of the rock, and the beam goes dull on it as on wet sacking. The Abbot of the Crust, **Dom Ambroise**, is abroad on his night walk with the Apostolic Starter, blessing the loaves of those who leave them on their sills for him. He raises one hand to the tower as the beam passes. He has done it for eleven years. They have never spoken.

**Logged:** *Abbot abroad. Licensed by his cloth.*

**Not logged:** A faint green on the Abbey step. The beam made it brighter going over, and when the beam had gone it was still there, glowing on its own, the shape of a heel and the front of a foot. Then a second, a stride further on, toward the town.

The Abbey answers to the Mother, not to the Council's seal, and the Council has never found a way to tax that. Its Abbot walks at night because the blessing is said to take best in the dark, as leaven does.

### Between Sweeps

She ate the second piece.

Below the tower the Iron-Wardens came down the reef road in their brass-plated leather, two of them, the shock-staves over their shoulders, to collect the log as they did after every proofing bell. She lowered it through the hatch on its cord. The lantern at the hatch showed her their hands and nothing else. The book came back up. Tonight it held no one.

She was relieved, and then she distrusted being relieved, and could not stop doing either.

She polished the mirrors. Out on the cliff path, in the reef light, she saw her predecessor going home with a bucket. He had carried fish heads up that path every night since his disgrace, for a woman who fed cats. He walked as if the path were longer than it was.

The sou stayed King-up.

---

## The Third Sweep: the Witching Hour

*The fog comes in off the water. The beam goes short, to an arm's length at the worst of it. The edges become entries.*

**Bearing 270: the Alchemist-Herbalist's Apothecary**

**Held by:** the Guild.

**In the beam:** The last thing seen clearly before the fog closes. The Spice-Master at the kettle in the window, stirring. Along the counter, a row of cloches waiting their coat of Bitter-Citron varnish, which marks a thief's hands yellow and does nothing else. Beside them the register of poisons, which the Inquisitor reads every quarter, closed and strapped.

**Logged:** *Apothecary lit. Lawful.*

**Not logged:** That a Gallery livery was waiting at the side door. It was a livery, gold at the cuffs, and not a face. The fog took it before the beam came back.

**No Bearing: the Fog**

**Held by:** no one; it holds.

On the Witching Hour the fog is a district of the island and is entered as one. It comes in off the reef and takes the harbour lamps one at a time, from the sea inward, the way a hand pinches out a row of wicks. In it the beam reaches the edge of the Flats and no further. The manifests could say anything; no one could see the gangplank. The Tide-Watcher's other two names are nearer in fog than at any other hour. The ordinance provides that in fog *the log is kept by sound.*

**Logged:** *Fog at the third watch. Beam to the Flats' edge and no further.*

**Bearing 080, the Edge of the Beam: the Tide-Ghouls & Salt-Flat Drowners**

**Held by:** no one. They are not entered in the census. They are entered in the burial rolls.

**In the beam:** At the very end of what the fog allows they come up out of the East Dock tide pools and the seeps under the Kitchen, six of them, onto the cobbles. Each drags a long kelp trail behind it, the trails crossing and recrossing, wet and black in the light. One still wears a net-mender's knot at the wrist, tied the way it is tied on this rock and nowhere else. What they touch they drench: soaked to the knee, heavy, slowed, walking afterward as if through water that is not there. They walk toward the lamps.

**In the beam, besides:** On the Flats, inside the edge, a person with a shuttered lantern, standing still so as not to be seen. The coat shows black. It might be Gilles. The fog will not say.

**Logged:** ~~*Drowners on the Flats, six. One abroad, no seal seen.*~~

**Not logged:** Whether the person moved before the Drowners reached the place where they had stood. Whether the kelp trail she found at the end of the sweep crossed one set of footprints, or two.

What the island says of the drowned is that they come in fog because fog is the nearest thing ashore to the water they left, and that they walk toward lamps because they are looking for the one that would have brought them in. This is entered as what is said.

### Between Sweeps

She did not eat the third piece.

The weight ran down while she sat, and the clockwork slowed, and the lamp stood on the reef unturned, which was where the ordinance wanted it. She could hear the Drowners on the cobbles a long way off, or the tide, or nothing.

The line was in the book. She had written it and drawn her pen through it, and a struck line is still a line. The Iron-Wardens could read through ink; they had been taught to. She thought about tearing the page and remembered that the book was stitched, and that a page torn out of a stitched book leaves its pair loose at the other end, and that the Counting-House counts pairs.

She picked the sou up off the sill and set it down again King-down. It was the first time.

## The Fourth Sweep: before the Six Bell

*The fog thins. The beam is long again, and grey with the morning in it.*

**Bearing 330: the Windmill Cliffs**

**Held by:** the Gallery, which owns the flour; the Guild, which works the stones; the Commons, which carries the sacks up and down.

**In the beam:** The sails, stopped all through the fog, beginning to turn. The weather vane on the mill cap that tells the fishermen whether to go out. The millers' boys asleep against the sacks with their caps over their faces.

**Logged:** *Sails turning. Wind north-east.*

Here the Council's flour is ground. In fog the fishermen read the turning sails the way other people read a clock. When the sails stop, the island waits. The flour that comes over on the ferry came dearer this autumn. The ferry says the mainland harvest was hailed out in the summer and that bread is dear in the French towns. The ferry says a great many things.

**Bearings 300–320: the Cliff Road, the Gilded Houses**

**Held by:** the Gallery, house by house; the house of Vane among them.

**In the beam:** Nothing. She dips the frame as the subscription requires, and the beam passes under the roofs and lights the road and the wall-foot and the drain. Of all the island, the cliff road's windows alone are never lit.

**Logged:** *Bearings 300–320 dipped, by subscription.*

**Not logged:** That she held the crank still at the dip, a moment longer than the walking pace. That she dipped it anyway.

**Bearing 005: Gallows Square, at the Turning**

**In the beam:** A crowd forming under the stage, black coats mostly, standing close for the warmth. The musicians uncovering their chairs and folding the oilcloth into squares. Loïc sweeping a last time, the same boards, though no one has walked on them since. Above the Hall's great south doors the gallery windows are lit from inside and set for breakfast.

**Logged:** *Square filling. Lawful.*

---

### The Six Bell

By ordinance the lamp goes back to the reef at the six bell, before the cloches are lifted. So the one thing on the island the Tide-Watcher has never seen lit is its dawn.

She turned the frame seaward and latched it. The clockwork took it. She took up the ferry tally, as the office required, and counted who went down the gangplank onto the Morning Ferry. She counted by coat colour, because at that range she could see nothing else. Two flour-white. A gold, with a servant. Red crust, nine, showing black in the grey: nine she could not tell one from another, though she tried, and then tried again with the glass, and then put the glass down.

The seventh bell had been set for the sentence. Up the hill, after a while, the music started. She could hear the music from the tower and nothing else, and then the bell, and then nothing.

The sou was King-down on the sill. The fourth piece of bread was in her pocket. She did not eat it. She wrote the last line in the book and blotted it.

**Logged:** *Ferry cast off. Six bell. Lamp to the reef.*
