# Grounds 99, Survey of 2626

**SURVEY OF 2626**
**Settlement:** GROUNDS 99. Called by its people Ninety-Nine.
**Registry file:** 99/R/41.
**Surveyor:** No. 30. Party of one.
**In the field:** Mon 18 Dec to Sat 23 Dec 2626.

---

## 1. Commission and Datum

**Commission.** Re-fix the bounds. Take the census by heads. Dispose of any record whose retention has run.

The registry's file on the place was thin. A ring map with no names on it. A census from before the last one, water-stained. And one clause, copied forward from a file older than the registry, typed in capitals the clerk could not account for:

GROUNDS 99 DESIGNATION TO STAND THROUGH ANY EXCHANGE, AND AFTER.

"It has always been in the file," the clerk said. The registry kept it because it had been kept.

The people of Ninety-Nine call the war the Exchange. They say it the way one says the weather. The file does not say whether the clause meant that exchange, and the surveyor did not enter a guess.

**Datum.** He set it Monday at first light on the oldest slab in the settlement, which the people call the Yard. The concrete there is older than the concrete round it and thinner laid, cracked in a grid like river ice the morning after a hard frost. The surveyor cut a bench-mark at the north-west corner and entered it as BM 1.

Counter at the bench-mark: within the registry's tolerance. At the pump: within. At the south road, where the water crosses it: within, high.

Soil core beside the slab, one arm deep: loam; needles; loam; one dark band, a finger thick; clay. The band was entered as a band.

The bench-mark is the only new mark the surveyor made in Ninety-Nine.

## 2. Ground

Concrete over everything that was ever flat. A ring wall, shoulder high, poured in lifts you can count like the rings of a stump. Blocks of four storeys and blocks of two. Stairs that climb a wall and stop in air. Nobody living can say who poured it. The people say *the builders*, and leave it there.

At the ring wall's corners stand old light masts, with lamps hung at the height a man can reach and crows above the lamps.

Pines in every seam. They split the pours slowly, the way a fist opens in sleep.

To the south the Blackwater has come back over the road. Beaver dams lean on the broken piers of an old trestle: five piers, the sixth gone. South of the piers a rail grade runs off through the trees. Its ties have long since rotted to brown lines in the moss, and only animals use it now. The people say it ran to the end of a line. Which line, and to what end, nobody says. Entered as a saying.

**Contest.** By day the settlement holds the concrete. By night the ground holds itself. The animals did not leave when the people came, and the people stopped asking them to.

## 3. Traverse

**Stn 1. The Yard.** BM 1. Slab, pump, bench-mark. An iron frame is set into the inner face of the ring wall on the north side: a rack of ten slots, each with an iron plate stamped with a numeral, 0 to 9. The concrete was poured round it, not over it, as if whoever poured had been told to leave it be. Each household of Ninety-Nine hangs a tag in its slot: a wooden peg, a bent spoon, a knot of wire, a child's shoe. Nine slots carry a tag. One slot not in use.

**Stn 2. The Long House.** A dormitory block of two storeys, poured round an older timber frame whose posts show through the walls like the bones in a fin. Downstairs, one room split by a half wall, with rats in the half wall. The residents sleep upstairs in two rows, by household number. At dark a mother on the east row counts her children aloud by their numbers, and stops when the count is right. If it is not right, she goes down to the Yard with a lamp. On the surveyor's two nights in the Long House it was right both times. Entered as a custom. The floor was not lifted.

**Stn 3. The Mill.** The concrete stops at the race, as if the pour had come to the water and decided not to argue. No wheel, only a stone sill where a wheel was. The beavers have the race. Some of the timbers in their dam are sawn planks, square-edged and grey as old teeth; the beavers used what they found. Contest: the water, which the beavers are winning.

**Stn 4. The Woods.** West and north of the ring wall. The felling slope has come back as pine, close as wheat. Elk stand on the old stumps at dawn, their breath hanging over them in the frost like smoke over a kiln. In a clearing are two brick domes, both fallen in, and owls nest in what is left. Wolves use the grade at dusk and the slope at night. The surveyor did not walk the woods after dark. Woods north of the domes, after dark: unsurveyed.

**Stn 5. The Workings.** An adit in the cliff north of the Yard, closed with a poured plug. The plug is older than the ring wall. A crack runs across its crown, and at dusk the bats go out and in through it like smoke drawn up a flue. The surveyor did not breach the plug. Interior: not surveyed.

**Stn 6. The low ground.** Under the Workings, the people say, there is more: a lower level, flooded. The only way to it is a drowned mouth west of the ford, where the river goes in under the hill. Water at the mouth: to the roof. Not entered.

There is one report, from a man of the Mill household who fishes the ford. Some nights there is a light under the water at the mouth, low and steady, "like a lamp somebody keeps filling." He did not say who. He did not want it entered. Entered: reported; not verified. The surveyor stood at the ford one night from dusk to the second hour and saw the river.

**Stn 7. The Office.** Walls faced with concrete, roof gone. A telephone body is bolted to the wall. Its wire has been gone so long that the bolt-holes have rusted shut round nothing. The children use it to call one another from across the room. Behind it is a records cell with a steel door that opened when leaned on.

Inside, one box. The paper had gone to pulp, and the pulp to a single grey brick. The label had weathered off except for one stamp in red, faded to the colour of a scraped knee:

RETAIN UNTIL 2626.

The surveyor did not open the brick, and there was no reading it without opening it. Entered by its stamp. See Records.

**Stn 8. The Forge.** A stone hearth in a low block by the Yard, the oldest stone in the settlement. The people of Ninety-Nine keep one fire, and this is it. They tend it in turns by household number, so it is never out. Wolves come to the edge of its light and no nearer. The surveyor warmed his hands at it on the first night. He had not expected to be glad of it, and was.

## 4. Fauna

**Wolves — the grade.** They hold the grade at dusk and the slope by night. They give way to the forge light and to nothing else.
*Contest: the night.*

**Boar — the Yard.** They root at the seams and take the slab back a hand's width at a time.
*Contest: the pours.*

**Beavers — the race and the piers.** They build with whatever the river brings, planks included.
*Contest: the water.*

**Elk — the felling slope.** On the stumps at dawn. They do not move for a person; a person moves for them.
*Contest: the slope.*

**Crows — the light masts.** Above the lamps, on every mast. They watch who goes out after dark.
*Contest: the lamps.*

**Rats — the Long House.** In the half wall, and in the ration bins when a lid is left off.
*Contest: the half wall.*

**Bats — the plug.** Out of the crack at dusk, back at first light.
*Contest: the crack.*

**Owls — the brick domes.**
*Contest: none. They took what nobody wanted.*

## 5. Inhabitants

Ten households, by slot, 0 to 9. Nine slots in use. Sixty heads, and one over count.

The people go by slot and place: Two-of-the-Yard, Six-of-the-Long-House, Five-of-the-Mill. The old say there used to be names as well as numbers, and that their grandparents had them. Asked which names, they shrug. The numbers were what got written down, and the numbers were what lasted.

**Nought.** Keeper of the rack, of household 0, eighty or near it. She drinks from a tin cup with a dent in its lip, and turns the dent away from her mouth without looking. Each dusk she walks the north face of the Yard and touches the plates one by one, 0 to 9, counting under her breath. At the unused slot her hand passes over without stopping, and her eyes do not go to it. She has done it, she says, since she was given the rack, and the woman who gave it to her did it the same way.

**The girl.** About ten. She came down the grade as a baby in a fish basket, the year every slot was full, and no household has room for her on the rack. On the registry's census she is entered as she was entered last time: over count. She keeps a stub of chalk, found in the ballast of the grade, in a pocket she sewed for it herself. She draws numbers on the slab, and the boar root them out. At dusk she sits on the edge of the Yard and watches Nought's hand pass the empty slot.

On the Wednesday she asked the surveyor, "Can a number be taken if nobody's got it?"

The surveyor entered her on the census as head 61, over count. He did not answer, because the commission did not say.

## 6. Records

The commission is plain: a record whose retention has run is to be disposed of. The year on the stamp is this year. The box qualified.

Nought asked for it. The winter was long already, and paper burns, even pulp, even a brick of it, if you break it small. The surveyor allowed it.

They fed it to the forge over two nights, Wednesday and Thursday, a piece at a time on top of the wood. Nobody read it; it could not have been read. The stamp was on the last piece and went last, the red turning brown and then gone.

Entered: *Disposed of per retention. Contents: not read.*

Afterwards Nought warmed her cup at the fire and drank.

## 7. The slot

The unused slot carries no tag. On its iron plate, where a tag would hang, there is a grey smudge worked into the grain of the iron, like old chalk that has been wiped and come back, or was never wiped. No letter can be read in it. The surveyor looked by daylight and by lamplight and made out none. He has set none down.

The people will not hang a tag there, and they will not wipe the plate. Asked whose slot it was, no one could say. Asked whose it is, Nought said, "It's taken," and that was all she said.

The registry has a rule: a slot with no known holder is to be reassigned from the census. There was a head over count, and the case was plain.

The surveyor entered the slot:

*Slot: one. Holder: none known. Mark: one, not legible. Retention: no schedule covers it.*

He did not reassign it.

On the Saturday, at dusk, the girl went to the rack with her chalk. Nought was at the plates, and her hand passed over the empty slot as it always did. The girl reached up and wrote 61 on the iron below the smudge, small, not touching it. Then she sat down on the edge of the Yard where she always sat.

Nobody wiped it. That night Nought counted 0 to 9. Her hand passed the slot, stopped a moment lower down, and went on.

## 8. Certificate

Bounds re-fixed to BM 1. Woods north of the domes, after dark: unsurveyed. Workings and the low ground: not surveyed. Designation stands, as filed. Records past retention: disposed of. Census: sixty heads, and one over count, now entered under a slot that is also held.

Surveyor No. 30. Sat 23 Dec 2626.

Entered as found.
