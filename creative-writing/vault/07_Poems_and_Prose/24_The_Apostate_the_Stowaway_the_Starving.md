# The Apostate, the Stowaway, the Starving

## I. The Apostate. Hale Barn, and the Bell.

The fork is rusted. I know why. I left it out in the wet the week she coughed, and nobody has forked hay since. Rust is what iron grows when nobody asks it for anything.

She talked to the goat as if the goat kept the accounts. She was not afraid of the pelt on the door; a wolf that has already lost, she said, can't hurt anybody. She prayed every night and never told me to whom. I thought there would be time to ask. The fen-cough came up out of the fog in October, and it did not leave time. It left the barn, and me, and the goat, and the goat has kept no accounts since.

Then they came. Not before. After.

One came by day with a candle. He was new off the train, his collar still white from back east, and he told me everyone in this town comes to him to confess sooner or later, and he could wait. One came by night with ash. I never saw a face. I saw what was left on my door in the morning, under the pelt: a mark in ash and something darker, still wet at the edges.

They prayed for my soul. They prayed for my vote at the Bell. They prayed for my hands, which are strong, and which a man in my state might put to anything. They prayed over me in the lane, and in the chapel porch, and on my own step, and each side prayed louder when it found the other had been there first.

Not one of them prayed for her bread. Not one of them prayed for her breath. Where was either of them the week she coughed?

I couldn't put that question down. So I put down everything else.

I took the fork off the wall. I scraped their mark off my door with the tines until the wood came up pale. I walked to St Brannock's with the fork on my shoulder and put out his candle in the porch with the haft. One stroke for each of them. Fair. The first fair thing done in that barn since October.

Now they pray against me, both of them. They cross the lane when I carry the fork, and I carry it everywhere. I stand at the Bell with it, and nobody asks me to put it down, and nobody asks me what it's for. There's talk at the Lantern that if anyone ever puts the new man in the ground, he'll come back tenfold. Talk. If it's true, that's ten more of him praying at me.

Whichever church takes this town, both will have prayed over me once, and I will have the rust to prove I never answered.

I was owed one god. I was sent two, and neither one brought bread.

## II. The Stowaway. A crate stencilled for Thorne Junction.

The platform lantern is lit. She lights it first; she always lights it first. Through the slats I can read the sheet clipped to the car door, if I keep my eye to the gap and my breath slow.

I read what goes. I read what comes. I read what a town is worth.

The Line owed me eleven weeks. I laid its ties out past Thorne Junction before the banks went, and when the banks went the paymaster went with them, and nobody wrote down where. If they wouldn't carry me as a man, I'd ride as cargo. Cargo gets where it's going. Cargo gets counted.

Flour comes in ninety-eight-pound sacks. I weigh less than two of them now. What is a man worth by the pound?

I came in under the bakery's flour, on the Wednesday. I ate it in the dark, raw, a fist at a time, paste on my tongue. I ate this town's bread before it was ever bread. I didn't think of that then. I think of it now, every morning, at the hour the ovens ought to smell of something and don't.

They found me with the Baron's freight manifest in my hand. They put me off at the Siding, by the rule, and I walked the last of it through Gallows Wood with something in the pines keeping pace and never coming closer.

But I had read it. And I kept reading, every morning after, over a porter's shoulder on the platform, all the way down.

Flour: six, six, six, five. Then a word where the number should be. *Cancelled.* Powder: two, two, two, three, six. Lead: three, three, three, four, eight. No flour. Twice the powder.

I'm no ledger-man. I can't tell you what a Baron means by anything. I can tell you what two columns mean when one goes down and the other goes up in the same week, in the same car, under the same lamp.

Who reads a manifest and doesn't learn the town's price?

Tonight the sheet is outbound. Car 3. *Coffin, outbound. Coffin, outbound. Coffin, outbound.* The remarks column says what each of them is taking along, a small thing apiece, the thing they'd have reached for. Nobody's remark says bread.

I'm riding east with them. I'm owed a fare, and eleven weeks, and my name on a pay sheet somewhere back east. I've paid my way in the only coin they don't print: the dark, holding still, among the dead.

A man who rides as freight learns what he weighs. A town that waits on freight learns what it's worth.

## III. The Starving. The square, the wake table, the Bell.

We were told: if you want to eat, you wait for the train. We were told, and we waited, and we were good at waiting. Waiting was the one thing in HollowMere the Company never charged rent on.

The first time, it was one Friday. One word on the sheet. One sack and the sweepings at Crumb's, and the mourners' loaves halved so the dead could still have theirs. Then, on the Monday, the sacks came back. Six of them, white on the platform, like a promise kept.

That was the cruelty. It came back once. It taught us to keep waiting.

Now the sheet says nothing where the flour should be, and we have stopped asking.

The rent still comes at midday. The scrip goes three to the dollar, then four, then five. The wake table still has ten plates. The wolves come closer to the fence, because wolves can count too. Somebody leaves gold at the bakery for bread there isn't, and we have learned you cannot chew gold. A woman stands on a box at the Bell and says what we are all thinking, louder. A man stands near her with a rusted fork, and nobody prays for him now. A crate goes out at dusk heavier than it came in.

We were calm. Then we were frightened. We have said the next word once before, and the Baron came down off the train behind his dogs. We can feel it in our mouths again, where the bread should be.

Mothers chew the crust first and hand it over soft. A child counts the crumbs on the board before she lets anyone sweep them. The tinners eat with their caps on, the way the dark taught them, as if the roof might come in on supper. Nobody here is a lesson. Everybody here is hungry in their own way, and that is the last thing still ours.

What is a company town when the company stops sending the town?

Agnes reads the stone to the Row at dawn now, before anyone looks at the board. We used to ask whose name. Now we ask whether there will be loaves enough for the wake.

We were owed the train. We were the bargain, and we kept our half. We are the line that isn't on the manifest.

The plate on the wake table at dawn is who we are. And the plate is empty.

---

One was owed a god. One was owed a fare. We were owed bread.

Nobody came to pay. Everybody came to count.

What you are owed and what you are sent: the difference is the town.
