# The Daybook of Crumb's Ovens

*Saturday, 21 October 1893*

## I. The cold room. The island's hour.

Up before anyone.

The cold room has a door to the shop, a shelf for the crock, a gap behind the crock where a hand can go, and a place where I am found out. This morning that place is the flour bin. One sack and the sweepings. No flour came Friday. None will come today.

Recipe: one spoon of yesterday, one cup of flour, water to the second knuckle. Stir on the island's hour, not the mainland's. The clock on the wall is the Company's. The crock keeps the other one.

I feed the crock. I feed the town. I feed the dead.

Three nights now, they say, since Aldous Vane. The wire said it, or the square did. I could not tell you which, and nobody in this town would think to ask me. I did not cry. I fed the crock at the hour, and the crock rose, and I took that for an answer, because it was the only one I was given.

People used to ask whether I came inland for him or for the cup. I still do not know. I know the spoon. I know the hour. I know that what rises in this crock is the cup's life and not the cup, and that the cup went where he went.

Who feeds the cup now?

## II. The mixing room. Four.

I don't know. That is the first thing I knead into the dough every morning: I don't know.

Recipe for a Rumour Loaf: flour, water, salt, a fist of the daughter, and one folded paper. Fold the paper twice. Lay it flat under the fold of the dough. Shape it tight so nothing shows at the seam.

The papers come to me unsigned. Under a coin on the counter. Under the door before light. Once, inside a glove someone handed back across the counter. I have never written one. I have folded every one.

I do not read them. I read them. I bake them anyway.

This week the slips go to the Tribunal, and a day is all it takes to harden a rumour into an indictment, and the gallows stands at the Bell. I cannot tell you whether any paper that passed through my hands ever reached the trapdoor. I can tell you I did not ask.

This morning I cut into a loaf I shaped last night, to test the crumb, and there was a paper in it already. Folded twice. Laid flat. Not mine. It could have been a customer's hand reaching over the bench while my back was turned. It could have been the boy who carries the baskets. It could have been my own hands, tired past remembering. I write all three down and I choose none.

If I do not know what I carry, am I innocent of it?

## III. The oven room. Five.

The oven answers that, the way it answers everything: no.

The oven is clay. Vane's men built it behind the tents before there was a town to put it in, and the town grew up around it like a crust. Recipe: fire it till the walls go white. Rake it. Swab it. Bake out yesterday's proof. Do not open the door to look.

On Thursday night, or Wednesday (the week runs together now), someone emptied the till. They left a gold coin on the hearth-lip, the way you tip for good bread. They left a boot in the flour dust, long in the stride, the toe toward the back door.

I swept it.

I sweep every night. That is my alibi, and it is theirs. The Detective came with his case file the next noon and asked whether anything was out of place. I said the floor was clean, and that was true.

I cannot spend the coin. Gold asks where it came from. So it sits behind the crock, in the place a hand can go.

Up before anyone, and still somebody was here first.

What is a gold coin worth in a town where flour comes by train, and the train brought none?

## IV. The proofing room. Before the train.

Less than a sack of flour. Exactly what I did next.

The proofing room is the only room in this house that belongs to tomorrow. Recipe: shape, cover, set in the cold, and leave it alone to become what it is going to be.

Three dead. Dora Finch. Wilf. Percy. One loaf to every mourner and three to each of the dead. That is the island's arithmetic, four hundred years old, and I have one sack and the sweepings.

The Company recognises no custom of mourning, the Articles say, and then they tolerate my table and write: "No rent shall be assessed on the three loaves set for the dead". The bread of the dead is the only bread in HollowMere the Company takes no cut of.

But who is the bread for?

This part is mine, and nobody else's. I cut the mourners' loaves to halves. Every living mouth at that table will get half, so that every dead one keeps three. Nobody ordered it. Nobody will thank me for it. I did it with the scraper in one stroke, the way you do a thing you have already decided.

And there is more. On my way in, before the crock, I went to the stone with tomorrow's date, as I have gone every morning this week, and read the new name on it. I have not written that name in this book. I will not write it here.

But three more loaves are proving under the cloth at the end of the bench, made from the living's flour, and they are not for Dora, or Wilf, or Percy.

Did I bake for a death, or does a death come to be baked for? Nobody has seen the name cut. Nobody has seen me shape these.

Is it grief, done early, or an order filled?

## V. The wake table. Station Rush.

I do not answer. I set the table.

Ten plates. Three for the dead, three loaves to each. Six for the halves, heaped. The tenth I set for whoever remains, and leave.

I know who bought which loaf. That is the whole of my office.

Dora Finch bought the end of the rye and held the seal up close to read it, through spectacles mended with thread, as if a crumb could have writing on it. Wilf paid for a heel once in the key of G, on my step, because he had no scrip and I had no mind to refuse a tune. Percy came in with three marbles and his bootlace undone and asked every time whether the dead ones were for eating, and every time I said, not by you.

I press a crumb into each loaf and seal it in candle wax, white on the dark crust. Every mouth that eats carries off a small hard piece of who is gone.

At the rush the Undertaker's boy comes in with his cap in his hands. Four, he says. Make it four. I ask him four for whom. He looks at his paper and says the paper says four. I count the dead I know of and get three, and the numbers will not lie down together. I do not bake it. I do not refuse it. I look at the tenth plate, already set.

The mourners sit. They eat the halves. Nobody says the loaves are small. Nobody has to.

Up before anyone, flour to the elbows, and the dead fed first.

The island taught me to feed the dead. HollowMere taught me who goes hungry for it.
