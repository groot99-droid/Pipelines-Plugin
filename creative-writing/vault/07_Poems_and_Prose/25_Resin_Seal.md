# Resin-Seal

```
CHAPEL HUT — STORES ISSUE
ITEM ............ RESIN, PINE, 1 TIN
ISSUED TO ....... CHAPLAIN (CIV.)
PURPOSE ......... MORALE
CHARGED TO ...... ████████ LIAISON
```

## I. The Chaplain. The gate, Mon 6 Oct to Mon 3 Nov.

The gate says PRODUCTION IS OBLIGATION. I stand under it at six with a tin of pitch and a cold thumb. The ridge gang comes through in twos, on its way up Widow-Maker Ridge, and I bless them.

The tin was my father's. He kept a church I will not name here, because this place takes the names of things and does not give them back. The pitch goes on the brow in one stroke of the thumb, the way he taught me, and I say what he said:

*Pitch on the brow. Pine at your back. The Lord on the ridge. Go up a man. Come down a man. Keep the wild out of the mind.*

That is the blessing as the guards hear it. It is not the whole of it.

The camp gives me numbers, stencilled on a jacket and burned into a billet. It does not give me names, so I take them, one a morning. I learn one true thing about a man. The next morning I put his name inside the blessing, between *go up a man* and *come down a man*, low, under the wind, where the guard at the post hears only an old preacher mumbling. I will not write their names here either. Each was said once, into the pitch, and that is where they are kept.

I bless the man who whistles through a broken tooth and stops when the dogs look up.
I bless the man who keeps a bootlace from home in his cap and has no boots it fits.
I bless the man who will not tell me his name, because the last man who asked for it wrote it down.

He was right not to. I wrote things down.

Every Friday there is a form called a Pastoral Return. It has a carbon, and a letterhead somebody has inked over before it reaches me. *Spiritual condition of personnel. Attendance. Remarks.* I filled it in every Friday, in my good hand: who prays, who has stopped, who cries in the wash line where he thinks the water covers it. I told myself the paper was the price of the gate, the gate was the price of the names, and the names were the price of my sleeping. Do you see how a ledger balances, if you let it?

One morning in October a man flinched at the pitch. He was not flinching at me. He flinched at the tin coming toward his face, as if it were a lamp held too close. That Friday I wrote one word beside his number: *unsettled.* The next week his number walked out past the woodshed at night between two guards, toward the hollow pine. He came back in the morning and stood his roll. He came back every morning after that, thinner each time, the way a stair wears down. First his belt went in a notch. Then his cheeks went in. Then his wrists were rope. By the end of the month his knuckles were white as chalk, and he ate at the mess table like a man who could not get to the bottom of the plate.

Did my word walk him out there? Or was he already walking, and my word only kept step with him? I have asked it every night since. Nobody at that office answers a chaplain's questions. They only take his answers.

What does pitch keep out, if the gate let it in? Whose mind was I keeping the wild out of, his or mine?

On Monday the third of November he came through the gate last. This time he leaned in to the thumb, and he asked me to say his name. I did not know it. In four weeks of mornings I had learned that he hums in the wash line and never takes a lamp into the dark. I had not learned his name. So I said his number. I will not write it. I have written enough numbers down. He said *amen* before I had finished. I never heard a man say it so like a door closing.

I have one more Return to write, and I will not write it. Tonight I will go out to the hollow pine and give him the only true one: *I wrote you down. I am sorry. Tell me your name.*

I gave them their names back one morning at a time, and every Friday I gave their words to a man with a pen. I was a good shepherd. I was a good shepherd to the ledger.

## III. The Collar. The woods, the night of Mon 3 Nov. The gate, from Mon 10 Nov.

I am linen and starch, and I was his for thirty years. I know a throat. I knew his. It swallowed on Fridays when he wrote the Return. It went still when he said *Lord* and meant it, and it went still less often than you would think.

On the night of the third he did not take me off. He always hung me on the nail. That night he wore me out past the woodshed, past the kilns, into the cold by the hollow pine. The cold there is wrong. It is colder under the boughs than out on the open snow, the way a cellar is colder than the yard.

He found the man sitting against the pine with his knees up, the way a boy sits.

I tell you what I was on. The Reverend's pulse, quick. His breath, short. His throat working on the words he had carried out there: *I wrote you down. I am sorry. Tell me your name.*

The man said his number.

So the Reverend opened the tin and blessed him. Pitch on a brow that was mostly bone. He blessed him in the dark, with nobody to report it to. I want that written somewhere that is not a form.

Then he did what he had never once done. He put his own name inside the blessing, for himself: *Go up a man, Elias. Come down a man.*

Then the breath on me that was not his. Hot, in that cold.
Then the jaw.
Then my starch went soft and red, and his pulse under me ran fast, then slow, then nothing, and the nothing was so cold.

I was taken off him in the morning. I was not folded.

Now I am on someone else.

This throat runs hot in the frost.
This throat swallows before *amen*, the way a man swallows who learned the word under a lamp while someone wrote it down.
This throat has a pulse as slow as something sleeping out the winter, and it does not quicken when the dogs go by.

The skin of it is drawn tight as a drumhead over the windpipe, with nothing between the two. The hair at the nape is too coarse for the comb, and it grows back faster than hair should. The ear tips are frostbitten black and do not seem to hurt him. And the jaw. The jaw has come forward. When he chews, something at the hinge clicks against my edge, a joint set wrong, like a gate that no longer meets its post. Under me there are marks I can feel and cannot read: small, in rows, healed in a hurry. I am cloth. I do not know what was done to him. I know it was done in order.

On Monday the tenth he stood where the Reverend stood, under the gate, with the Reverend's tin. My starch had been brushed brown. The guard at the post looked at me and nodded. A collar is a collar. Nobody looks above one. That is what a collar is for.

He says the blessing, every word of it. Where the Reverend put a name, he puts a number. *Go up a man, Nine. Come down a man.* The men say *amen*. None of them ever heard his own name in it. Each only ever heard the number in his own ear, where the name had been. So the blessing sounds the same. It sounds the same, and it works the other way.

At night in the hut he says his own number to himself, twice, the way a man checks a lock. He hums one line of something that is not a hymn, a tune with a dance in it, from somewhere with a river. He thumbs pitch onto his own brow, waits, and wipes it off. Some nights he cries, and the crying comes out of that jaw wrong. I cannot tell whether he is mocking the blessing or begging it. In thirty years on the Reverend I could not always tell that either.

Was the blessing ever the Reverend's? Or was it only the shape of his mouth, and the shape is all it needs?

They took the names out of the blessing, and it still worked. I have been a collar for thirty years. That is the first thing that has frightened me.

## IV. The Answers. November.

### The Timber-Stalker

You said come down a man. I came down quiet.

I walk the log decks at night, and the old rail bed past the broken trestle, where the ties have rotted to the shape of ties. It runs south, farther than anyone here has walked it. I leave no print on it. I count my steps in my mother's language, so that one count in this camp is not theirs.

Quiet is the only thing here they never issued.

### The Pit-Fiend

You said come down a man. I came down owed.

I work the Workings by lamp. When a man is alone where the drift narrows, I stand in it, and I do not move, until he gives me what the camp gave me: less. I keep an empty matchbook from a dance hall back east. There is nothing in it. I keep it because it closes.

I like it, Reverend. Write that down. You wrote down everything else.

### The Den-Keeper

You said come down a man. I am not one, and I came down anyway.

I keep you in the deep woods, under the boughs, where nobody has reported you. While nobody finds you, your collar walks. I know what that makes me. I keep you anyway. Nobody else in this place would keep anything for you.

The frost keeps you better than I can. Your face is grey and whole. Your throat is not, so I laid pine across it, the way you would lay a cloth on a table. Your hands are whole. I folded them on your chest with the thumb out, because that was the part of you that worked.

I hang what the camp throws away on string around us: a bent spoon, the key off a ration tin, a lid with no can. When anything comes, it rings. Every night I tell you the roll, the way you told God the names. Ten of ten, Reverend. Ten of ten.

Somebody here should be a sexton.

### The Alpha-Born

You said come down a man. I say it myself now.

I have no collar and no tin. The pines weep pitch for nothing, and I take it on my thumb. I say it with no name and no number: *Go up. Come down. Keep the wild.* If they find you tomorrow, the words stay in my mouth. They were never in the collar.

I have come down something that does not need you to say it.

### The sixth

I said *amen* because everyone did. And because his thumb was warm, and nothing else is warm in November.

Six of them were on the ridge at ten past two. All six stood by their bunks at six. Every billet on every rack weighed true. I was on the throat that led them, and I can tell you what fifteen seconds is in this camp. It is the time they give you to be where you belong. The six took it and used it for the other thing. The jaw under me opened wider than a jaw should, and the sound went out over the pines. Then they came back, and they belonged.

He blessed the ridge gang at the gate that morning, under the motto, with the Reverend's tin, and the guard nodded at me.

What is a blessing for, if it keeps nobody out?

It was never a lock. It was a name said once, where nobody could write it down. The Reverend knew that, which is why he said the names low, and why, at the very end, he said his own. They know it too, which is why they say none at all.

Moss put the names in so God could find them. They took the names out so nobody would. The log on your rack at dawn is who you are, and every one of them was there.
