# Eight Phrases

*Sunday, 3 December 1893*

**IT WAS DARK.**

Sunday night, and Hattie on the board where Doris and the Undertaker laid her out. Chalk round her in one line with no gap, from a twist of paper with three sticks left in it. Three candles: one at her head, one at her feet, one in my hand. Then I count. The dead get eight counts, a held breath's length, and eight things they may say. Never a ninth. The dead are poor in words. Eight is all they have, and they share them.

One. *It was dark.* I have heard that one more than all the others put together. Dark in the lane behind the Lantern after eleven, where Mabel went in her boots lined with hymn sheets, the alto line marked. Dark on the channel. Dark under the Bell. What use is a phrase that is true of every death in HollowMere? Everyone here dies after the lamps. The living hear it from me and nod, as if I had brought them something.

I do not tell.

**I KNEW THE FACE.**

Two. A man can come down the Row in a coat that is not his, with a walk that is not his, and say good morning in a voice he has borrowed for the day. A ghost who says it knew the face is telling me about a coat. What the dead saw is not what the dead know, and it is the second one the living come to me for. How would the dead know the difference, when the living never have?

I do not tell.

**NOT THE WOLVES.**

Three. Since the wolves started walking the fence, the town has wanted this phrase and been afraid of it in the same breath. A wolf is a sorrow. A wolf is weather. If it was not the wolves, it was a hand, and the hand passed you the bread at the last wake. Which would you rather I brought up out of the ring, a wolf or a neighbour? Nobody answers that. They ask me what was said instead.

I do not tell.

**BY THE WATER.**

Four. Cyril, on the Wednesday. I drew my ring on the dock before they carried him up to the Undertaker, out of a channel that comes to the ankle at low water. In fog he whistled two notes, high then low, so Nora would know where he was on the water, and she answered with her own. Nora stood outside my chalk with the nets over her arm and asked what he said. I gave her nothing. She whistles the two notes off the dock in the evenings now, and nobody answers, and I could.

What does a drowned man owe the dock? What does the woman with the chalk owe the wife? *By the water* is a phrase I knew before HollowMere. Another man went into the water at the end of this Line, and the first train came in without him on the platform.

I did not tell Nora.

**IT WAS NOT ME.**

Five. The man the Tribunal hanged on the Tuesday. His name is a blank in their book and in the Undertaker's, and I will not be the one to fill it. I drew my ring at the foot of the gallows pine after they cut him down. There was waxed thread in his coat pocket, and I left it there.

Here is the rule I learned before I learned the eight: the dead who were on the knife's side may lie. They keep their side after. So *not me* from a hanged man is worth nothing, and if he says nothing, that is worth nothing too. If the dead can lie, what is a Medium for?

I have an answer, and I do not like it. There is one liar I am sure of in every ring I draw. I have been sure of it since the first train.

I do not tell.

**TELL HIM.**

Six. Back in this room. Hattie. Doris told her the news before I came in, the way Doris always does; she said so at the door. Over Hattie's copper, in white capitals, HATTIE: the one word she could write, chalked where she would see it first thing every morning. A son back east, and a letter every week, a line at a time through the Postmaster, for a scrip. A jar of buttons on the shelf, one off every dead man's shirt she washed for the box, so that something of each of them stayed up here.

The Watchman is at the door. They took his lantern off him today, and he holds his hands as if it were still in them.

"What did she say?"

*Tell him.* Tell whom? The son who will get no letter this week? The man in the doorway with nothing in his hands? The phrase is the same two words, whoever it is for.

I do not tell him.

**I AM COLD.**

Seven. Maud, the Monday Car 3 took her out. She took her Rotgut as a wage, never as a gift, and darned both of Bar's cuffs so they matched. I held her ring that night behind the back car, with all eight laid open, and she picked none. She has not picked one since. She never said much, living.

I have envied her ever since: eight counts with every word open, and nothing taken. Who needs a Medium to know the dead are cold? There was a cold once that smelled of rain, under the hill, and I was not there for it. I was in a room over a laundry, ironing other people's collars, waiting for a letter in pencil.

I do not tell.

**HAS IT COME?**

Eight. The only question among them. Seven of the phrases are for the living. One is for me.

Jory. I have not said his name tonight until now. I came on the first train to meet a man who had written that he would meet it. He did not. Every night since, in every ring I draw for him, he picks the same one.

"Has it come?"

And I say what a living woman would say. It has. He asks again. I say it again.

In my pocket, a small wooden spoon with a notch for the thumb, the bowl still rough. I do not say who gave it to me.

That is the liar I am sure of. The dead may lie to keep a side. I lie to keep a man asking.

Eight. Hattie's flame leans. She has chosen. I heard it. It is hers.

Tomorrow I take the chalk to Harold's step, three stubs in a twist of paper, and ask him to keep it out of my reach, to learn whether Jory asks without the ring.

The rule says the knife's dead may lie. It was never written for the one who answers them.

I do not tell the living what the dead know. I tell the dead what they need to hear.
