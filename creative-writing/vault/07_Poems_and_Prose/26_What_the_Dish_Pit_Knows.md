# What the Dish Pit Knows

## I. Le Plongeur, 1787

I kneel on a word I cannot read.

It was a seal once, the seal of Le Champignon, the office of the first heresy. When they abolished the office they ground its seal into the dish-pit floor, so that someone would always be kneeling on it. Someone is me. Before me it was my mother. My knees know its letters better than my eyes know anything.

I did not choose the floor. I did not choose the water. I did not choose whose plates.

The Council's book says the Plongeur is penance, by sentence or by birth. Mine was birth. I was born to this floor the way a loaf is born to its platter, and nobody asked the loaf.

In the spring they gave a salt-raker to the Hearth for a crumb. It was the heel of a gilded loaf, found in his cap. I did not see it. Down here you hear it. The music comes first, through the south doors from the Square. Then the bell. Then every cloche in the Hall lifts on the one note, like the whole Gallery drawing breath.

He whistled through the gap in his teeth. He mended my bucket once with a cod-bone needle and would not take a copper for it. His name was Mathurin. I say it once because no one upstairs said it at all.

Then the supper came down.

Crust with the gold leaf still on. A cream sauce left to skin. Three sugared swans, one with its neck bitten through.

I know what it cost. It cost one Mathurin.

What the dish pit knows: every plate comes down.

At dusk the Aboyeur walks the Hall above me, calling every loaf by its owner's name. I hear him through the floor. The first loaf of the night he sets on the empty platter nearest the Hearth, for a name under seal, and he calls nothing at all. I do not ask whose. Nobody below the Hall asks. Nobody above it does either.

Who washes the plate a death was eaten off? I do. Who asks what it cost? Only the water, and the water does not repeat it.

The loaf on your platter at dawn is who you are. The plate in my sink at dawn is what you did.

## II. Le Commis, 1788

Ninety-one steps. I carry flour down them, and water, and ash. I set them at the foot of the vat and I climb back up without touching the glass. A Commis may carry, but may never feed the Mother. That is the whole of my office. I have done it four years without a crumb of complaint, and without a crumb.

The Gallery's flour. The Gallery's water. The Hearth's ash.

Whose ash? The duty does not say. I have not asked. I carry it.

In the autumn the Gallery gave the Hearth a boy for a tassel. He had cut it off a cloak in the crowd on the Wharf: gold thread, a thumb's length of it. The Fire was set for the sixth bell. Then a lady of the Gallery remembered the fish. Fish will not wait. So the Fire was moved to after the fish course, so that the cloches could be lifted on the bell and the soup would not go cold.

He kept a pocket of odd buttons. He sorted them by colour on the Wharf steps: bone, horn, brass, and one of real silver he would not trade for bread. His name was Yannick. The island called him the Cutpurse, which is what it calls every boy with quick hands. Then it called him nothing.

That night I carried the Hearth's ash down and the Gallery's bottles up. Down, up. Down, up. Everything to her. Nothing from her.

Did I ever eat a loaf I carried for? Did anyone ever hand me back so much as a heel? You know the answer. You have eaten it.

But hands that only carry learn the weight of everything, and the taste of nothing.

I carried their plates down and their ash down, and I called it work.

## III. L'Apprenti, 1789

There are several of us. We are the ones who watch. The disgrace of our office is speaking, so here is everything I did not say.

I saw a washerwoman fall behind on her yeast-tax in the wet spring, when her youngest had the cough. I did not say it.

I saw the Ledger-Clerk's stamp come down on her platter: UNREGISTERED. I did not say it.

I saw the Inquisitor ask, the Bailiff take and the Chancellor decide, in the order the law gives them, as neatly as a menu. I did not say it.

She stitched each of her children's initials inside their caps in blue thread, so that she could find them by feel in the dark. Her name was Soizic. The island calls her the Debtor now, the way it gives every Unregistered death that name, as if owing were all she ever did.

I saw the Gallery choose their seats for the Square by the gilt on their sleeves. I saw a lady of the Gallery ask whether the bell might be rung a quarter-hour early, because her boat had the tide at seven. It was rung early.

I did not say it. I did not say any of it.

Is a watcher a witness? Is a witness a hand? If I had spoken, whose platter would have been empty that dawn: hers, or mine, or both?

Silence was my office. I kept it better than they kept their word.

## IV. Le Plongeur, September 1789 – 1790

On the twenty-third of September the news came in on the Morning Ferry. I heard it in the water before I heard it in words. That night the supper for the Crown's man came down uneaten.

Full plates. Cold plates. Plates no one would sign for.

In all my life of plates I had never seen a full one come back. I stood with my hands in the water and did not know what to scrape.

Before the month was out the Gallery gave the Hearth one of its own, and the Square that watched was red.

Then the lower rooms filled. Dockhands, Plongeurs, apprentices, the Unregistered. Red crust was pinned to their sleeves where the Guild wore flour-white and the Gallery wore gold. They stood in my dish pit because it was the one room in the Sacred Kitchen nobody above us had ever wanted.

In 1790, the year the Abbot died, the new faith came down the stairs. It had been a heresy. Now it was the Commons' religion. They knelt where I kneel, on the Champignon's seal, and they called the floor holy. They called it an honour to kneel there.

I have knelt there since I was born.

If the floor is holy now, what was I? The saint, or the rag?

They made my penance their prayer, and asked me to move over.

## V. L'Apprenti, 1791

The kitchen has a committee now.

Plongeurs and Commis sit at the command desk, where the Chef once ordered the day's bake. The tribunal's table stands in front of Le Critique Gastronomique's chair, empty for two hundred years. No one sits in it. The Council kept it empty so that no one could overrule the Council. The committee keeps it empty for the same reason. Nothing it decides can be overruled by a chair.

I am its clerk. Speaking was the disgrace of my office. Now speaking is my duty. I read the denunciations aloud from the expediter, where the Aboyeur stands to call the loaves, and my voice carries the length of the Hall.

In the summer they gave the Hearth a cadet of a gold-leaf house. It was for a gilt button worn on a fast day. A crumb, the other way round.

It was the same bell. It was the same cloches, lifted on the same note. The supper after was the same, and red crust ate it. The plates went down the same stairs to the same sink.

He pressed sea-pinks in a psalter. He was nineteen. He had never once been down the kitchen stairs, and on the last morning he asked me, quietly, which way they went. His name was Armand.

But I had watched this before. I knew the words before they gave them to me.

They gave me back my voice and told me what to say with it.

## VI. Le Croissant Rassis, 1792

Lamination is patience in folds. The disgrace of the office is haste.

I was the Tourrier for thirty years. I folded butter into dough, and gold leaf into the butter, for the Gallery's pastry. The thinner the leaf, the more they paid. The more they paid, the faster I folded.

I folded for their weddings. I folded for their christenings. I folded for the suppers after the Fire, and I did not ask whose Fire.

Now the committee has given me the title that is not an office. Le Croissant Rassis. The stale one. It stands on a card in the front-of-house case, and I sit in the case above it, hard as a rock under the case lamp, so the island can learn what a man is worth who folded gold for the Gallery. Children tap the glass. Their mothers let them.

Am I stale because I was kept too long, or because I was kept at all?

I laid gold on bread for people who sentenced bread. I know it. I knew it with every fold.

Haste was my sin. Theirs is the same, in a redder coat.

## VII. The Sin-Eater, spring 1793

They pay me a copper and I eat a crust over the empty platter, and the sin goes into me and not into the ground. That is the island's custom, older than the Council. Nobody writes it down. Everybody pays.

I ate for Mathurin. I ate for Yannick. I ate for Soizic, whom the island calls the Debtor, and her children paid me in buttons because they had no copper. I kept the buttons.

Now the gold-leaf houses send for me. They pay better. The crust is finer.

Sin tastes the same on gilt as on burlap.

The Sot sings at every Fire. He sang for the Gallery's suppers and he sings for the committee's. It is the same tavern song, the same wrong note in the third line. Nobody has ever stopped him. Nobody has ever needed to. The stage never changed, so why should the song?

In April a thing came off the Morning Ferry in crates and a great deal of straw, with a Paris mark burned into the wood. The National Razor, they call it. They set it up beside the Hearth. One stage. Two instruments. I did not look at it long. It is a thing, and things are not what I am paid to eat for.

Now the feeding hand waits upstairs for the Commons' tribunal. He is a Vane, of the only house whose hand has ever touched the Mother. They have asked me to be ready with a copper's worth of crust. Ready is not needed. Nobody can tell me which I will be.

I have eaten the sins of both halves of this island, and I am still hungry.

## VIII. Le Plongeur, May 1793

I am on my knees on the seal.

The others went up. Plongeurs sit at the command desk now. Apprentices read from the expediter. The faithful come down and kneel beside me, and go back up. I stayed where I was born.

The committee's plates came down tonight. It is the same porcelain, and the gilt rim is chipped now. There is red crust in the crumbs where gold leaf used to be. I scrape them the same way. The water does not know the difference.

At dusk the Aboyeur set the first loaf on the empty platter nearest the Hearth, for a name under seal. He called nothing, as he did the night I was born, as he did every night of the Gallery and every night of the Commons. No revolution has moved that loaf an inch. I do not ask whose.

The feeding hand is upstairs. His tribunal is at dawn. I do not know if his plate will come down to me tomorrow, or who will have eaten off it, or whether his platter will stand empty when the cloches lift. I will not know until the plates come down.

What the dish pit knows: every plate comes down. Every hand that eats is a hand I wash after.

They changed the colour of the crust. They never changed who kneels.
