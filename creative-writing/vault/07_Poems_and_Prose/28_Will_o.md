# Will-o'

## I. Which is mine

Dusk below the Fenwood, and I climb.

Once it was the oak, limb over limb, at nineteen. Now it is a pole. My knees count the rungs for me, and the can knocks my hip on every one.

I keep it lit. I keep it trimmed. I keep it for nobody, going nowhere in particular.

There is no lake now to bring a boat home across. There is a fen, and a town on the lip of it, and a light on a pole at the edge of the two, and an old man under the light. That is the whole of the arrangement.

On Saturdays I fill my can at the Lantern, and Mrs Grieves puts it in her book. If you bring me oil I will take it, and I will tell you what you paid for it, so you know that I know. Nobody pays me to burn it. The Company pays the Lamplighter by the post, in scrip. She passes my pole and does not reach for it. If she reached, I would stop her.

What is a light for, if it brings nobody home?

A light is not for where you are going. It is for knowing where you are.

## II. The others

Out on the fen there are other lights.

Low. Lower than any arm would carry them. Moving against the wind. Moving when nothing moves. Never twice in the same place.

They are not lanterns. Nobody holds them. They lead walkers off the planks and in, and the fen does the rest.

Mine is steady. Theirs are not. I say it the way my father said it of his. The same height every night. The same place every night. The same colour every night, since before this town had a name to put on a stone.

But I hear what people say. They have stood at the end of the planks, they say, and could not tell which one was mine. They say it kindly. They think it is a thing about the fog.

I take it the way I took the lake. Personally.

If you cannot tell them apart, what is steady worth to you?

Steady only saves the ones who wait long enough to see it stay.

## III. Five

One of the low ones comes nearer. Mine goes out.

I have not touched it. The oil is in it.

One. A walker on the planks looks for the steady light.
Two. Finds only the ones that move.
Three. Steps toward the nearest.
Four. Steps again, and there is no plank.
Five.

Mine comes back. I did not light it.

Here is what I have not told the box at St Brannock's, or the Lantern, or my father, who is not here to hear it. Up on the pole, in the five, I cannot tell either. In the five, I have wanted to go.

Who keeps a light that goes out when it is wanted most?

Five is long enough to choose wrong, and too short to choose again.

## IV. Two notes

Cyril fished the channels. In fog he whistled two notes, high and then low, so that Nora on the dock would know where he was on the water, and she answered with her own. I heard them from the pole for years, the way you hear your own heart. Some nights I lifted a hand to them, though no hand could be seen through that.

Low water was circled on the chart at the Lantern, and he went out at the circle.

Two notes. Nora's two. Two notes again. Then none.

Was mine lit? I will not tell you it was dark. I will not swear to you it was burning. I was on the pole, and I was counting, and I do not know what I was counting.

They brought him in at noon. I went down to look, because I had heard him every night and I owed him that.

Face down in a channel that would not cover a boot. One boot gone. A fist of reed he would not give up. Fen in his mouth, and the mouth open, as if on the first note. His eyes open too, turned toward the marsh edge, where the pole stands.

They wrote *drowned*. A man does not go under in water that will not reach his shin. Everyone who stood in it knew that, and nobody had another word.

Was it theirs? Was it a hand? Was it mine, gone out for five?

Nora whistles from the dock every evening. I do not answer. I am not the one she is calling, and I am the only one who hears.

## V. The one under the water

You will want to ask about the other one. Everyone does, sooner or later: the lantern under the black water by the old oak, still burning, as steady as mine.

Don't ask me who keeps it lit.

I have heard it is my father's lamp, gone down with the limb. I have heard it is the sign of the old Rusty Lantern, carried into the mere by a barman who never came up. I have heard both. I say neither.

What I will say is this. When I was nineteen, nobody had drowned in my keeping, and I was proud of it the way a young man is proud of a thing that is his. When the limb went I watched the lamp go under, and I stood and waited for it to go dark.

I did not trim it. I did not fill it. I did not ask.

So what is a keeper whose first light keeps itself? And if there are two steady lights on the fen now, how would you know which one the fen is keeping?

I keep the light I can reach. The other one keeps something I won't name.

## VI. Steady

There is someone at the end of the planks tonight, counting. I can see the light stop and the head turn. One light. Two lights. Five. Maybe it is the Lamplighter on her way back. Maybe it is you.

I will climb. I will trim. I will stand under it till the fog goes, the way I stood under the oak when there was water to stand beside.

It will not be enough. It has not been enough since the lake went. One of the low ones is coming in now, not steady, nearer than it was.

If you are the one counting, hear me once.

"Wait five before you follow anything. Wait five, and then wait."

Every light on the fen is a promise, and the fen keeps every one but mine.
