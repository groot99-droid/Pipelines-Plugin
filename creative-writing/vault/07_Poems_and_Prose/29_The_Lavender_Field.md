# The Lavender Field

*HollowMere. Written Monday, the eighteenth of December, 1893, on the dock, about the night before.*

**I.**

Sunday. The seventeenth. After dusk. The Bell.

I came because the stone said THE VICAR at dawn, and Agnes read it to the Row the way she reads them all, and the Row went to its doors and looked up the slope.
I came because the chapel bell did not go when the service ended.
I came because the dock is no place to be after dark when nobody is going to answer anyway.

Somebody rang the Bell. I don't know who. In this town that is enough.

We were fewer than had been in the pews. You could count the dark doors on the Row and know who wasn't there.

What do you bring to a wake when nobody has said there is a body?

**II.**

It came where the square should have been. It was in front of every face at once, and I can't tell you where it came from.

A field. It came in squares, like a net held up to a lamp, each square its own colour. A colour I have no word for. Somebody at the pump said *lavender* this morning. I let them have the word.

Hills, bent. The whole field leaning the way the reeds lean off a boat's wake.

One tree, near, standing over the rest. Fruit on it the colour of a lamp-flame.

And the sky. Blue. Not fog-white. Not mere-grey. Blue. Cloud on it thick as wool before carding.

It slid past the way the bank goes by when you're rowing. Then it came toward the tree, the way the dock comes to you when you ship the oars.

There was music, and St Brannock's has no organ.

I counted it. I count the way I count between Cyril's two notes and mine: high, low, and then the wait before I answer. One. Two. Nobody else was counting. I'm sure of that, and I don't know why it matters.

Twenty-one.

There was nobody in it. I looked for a face out of habit, his. Nobody. That was how I knew it was his.

**III.**

Then the words came, under it, white, standing the way the Gazette's type stands, with no voice to them.

I read slow. I read with a finger, and there was no paper to put it to, so I put it to the air, and the words waited for me. They waited the way he did on the bank of the channel, the day I took the Seal and couldn't find what to say.

*"In the end I sought nothing else but to serve God.*
*And serve him I did.*
*With this death's last breath I pray this prayer: may the Almighty grant me the strength of ten in the next life.*
*And in return, tenfold my own."*

Strength of ten. I can mend ten fathom of net in an evening. I can't think what he meant to carry with it.
Is a prayer still owed if nobody hears it said? Is it owed if the whole square does?

**IV.**

It went out like a lamp turned down, not blown.

We looked round, all of us, the way you look round at a wake for the widow. I know how that looks. They looked at me that way on the twenty-ninth.

And the robes were there.

At the back, under the eave, where a man stands to hear a town out. The collar I knew. The black from the collar down. Standing still, watching the place where the tree had been.

Whoever was under it had watched it with us. Had they counted too?

Who wears a dead man's coat to his wake?
Who stands at the back of a man's memorial and keeps so still?
Who among us looked longest, and was seen looking?

People looked at the robes, and their shoulders came down. I watched it go along the square like a gust going down the Row. It was easier. A coat is easier than a field.

I said, *He's here,* to nobody. Then I said, *They're here.* I don't know which of those was the lie.

**V.**

I said dream.

I said dream at the pump this morning, with my bucket, the way you say fog.
I said dream because a dream cannot be taken to the Constable.
I said dream again tonight, on the chapel step, because at dusk the robes stood up in the pulpit and preached, and the town went in to hear it, and so did I.

The Undertaker wrote *drowned* for Cyril, in water that would not cover a boot. He wrote it because the line wanted a word. I let it stand, because I had no better one. I know what it is to let a short word stand. I have been doing it since the twenty-ninth.

And this too. This morning I turned over the twine account and wrote his words on the back in my plain hand, slow, every one, the way they had waited for me. I took them down the hill and put them across the Clerk's counter. He was reading something in his drawer and did not look up. I did not sign.

I reported him the only way this town allows. Unsigned.

The pump says the Postmaster cut the bag from the east and there was a second copy in it, word for word. The bag came sealed.

Who wrote that one?

I didn't sign because the robes had seen who read slowest. I read slowest. I always have.

**VI.**

This evening I whistled from the dock, the two notes, high, then low, the way I do every evening. Nobody answered, the way nobody does.

At the same hour, up the slope, the robes preached, and the town answered. *Amen.* Aloud, together, through the fog. I could hear it from the boards.

That is all I know that the town doesn't. I know how the quiet sounds when the dead are really gone. I have heard it every evening since the twenty-ninth. Tonight I heard the town answer something that is not him.

I took the Seal for the house. The house is me now. I don't know what that makes the Seal.

**VII.**

Nobody in HollowMere has seen a blue sky since the fog came off the mere. Last night every one of us saw one, and this morning not one of us had.

A dream is what a town calls the thing it saw together and will not report.
