# Sayings of the Fork

## I. Pump Row, first light

*Say it before they ask, and it is only news.*

That one is mine. I made it myself, on this lane, going up.

I am Agnes, of Pump Row, the door next to yours, Edith. Every morning it is the same three things: the shawl, the lane, the stone. Then I come back down with a name in my mouth and call it in at the doors before anyone has their boots on.

This is a young town with old sayings. Some came in on the Line in people's mouths, from back east and from further than that. Some we made here, after the drain, and they took faster than weeds. Harold says *before the drain* about everything, bread included. Nobody laughs any more, because it is a saying now, and you don't laugh at a saying.

We say it about the bread. We say it about the weather. We say it about the dead, because the dead are weather here.

How old does a saying have to be before it is true? Two deaths old. One death is bad luck. Two is a custom.

You never learned them, Edith. You said *two weeks* about the rent, as if saying it carefully enough could bring it down to one. That was your saying. It never took.

*A saying is a thing said twice by people who were right once.*

## II. Lights

*Keep a candle lit and the wolves keep their distance.*

We teach that one first. The children have it before their prayers.

A window is not a light to a wolf. Nor is the moon. Nor is the moss. A wolf is afraid of a flame, and of nothing that only looks like one. So we say: carry it. A lit window is for the people inside it.

A lantern carried is a lantern owed. The Lamplighter goes round at dusk and lights the town's lanterns for scrip. She leaves the fourth one on the square till last, the one over the chalk and the well, and we say you don't walk under that one until it's lit.

*A dark lantern walks two ways, the careful and the guilty.* We say it of anyone we see with the shutter down. Nobody says which of the two they are.

*Not every light over the fen is Fennick's.* Follow none you did not see lit.

And what does a candle keep off, besides wolves? Nothing. I have sat up with one all night. The rest come indoors.

*A flame keeps the wolves off. Nobody ever said what keeps the rest.*

## III. Wolves

*A wolf runs a short mile and goes home.*

That is what we say. Run if you must, and run toward a flame. Never go into the wood unlit at dusk. A wolf has a home and goes back to it, which makes it kinder than some.

What it kills, it leaves where it fell. A body in the mud with the throat opened. The coat in strips. Fur. Claw marks on the fence post. Never a footprint. The wolves do not come back for what they leave, and they do not wipe their feet.

I say all this with my arm bound to the elbow. On Wednesday, before light, at the pump, one did not go home. I have the saying in my mouth and its teeth in my arm, and the saying is still right, mostly. Mostly is what a saying is.

There is a girl who strayed at twilight, got cornered, and was brought back. She tells the truth now, about everything: the price of eggs, whose step is dirty, what she saw. The town finds her harder to bear than the wolves.

*The wolves get blamed for what the wolves did, and for what they didn't.* It is the easiest thing to say at a door. Wolves, we say, and we shut it. But Doc Mallory bends over a body, and sometimes the word the Doc writes down is knife. And sometimes there is a thread on the door frame, and wolves don't wear scarves.

*Wolves leave fur. Nobody ever found a wolf's thread.*

## IV. Water

*Never wash at the well after dark.*

It isn't the water. It's who sees you at it.

A wet bucket at dawn is a question. A clean rag has done nothing yet. A bloody rag has done it once. We say these over the washing, and we laugh, and we look at each other's buckets.

Hattie had the wash-house at the foot of the Rise. She wrote to her son back east every week. On her shelf she kept a jar with the lid on. In it was one button off every dead man's shirt that came down to her to be washed for the box. Something of him kept back above ground, she said, and no one would miss one.

Since Hattie, the Row has a new saying: *Keep one button back.* The women say it at the tub. Not all of them know why. That is how you know it has become a saying: it has stopped being Hattie.

What does the well remember? Don't ask me. I only draw from it.

*Water takes the blood. It leaves the bucket.*

## V. Doors

*A lock keeps people out. It has never once kept anyone in.*

A broken door stays broken. A key that opens every door leaves a hand on every door. A lock put on from outside does not last the night. *Horace knows your last house, never your hour.* We say that to the young ones when they go walking out. They blush, and they should.

And you, Edith. Your bolt was shot from the inside. Your blanket was straight. I brought your name down the Row myself, and I could not tell anyone the day.

But the lock kept nobody in, Edith. It did not keep you.

Your letter to your sister is still on your table. It is folded once, not finished, with the stamp lying loose on top. I have looked at it through your window every morning since. I have not gone in and posted it. Should I have? Whose letter would it be, if I did?

*Bolt the door for the living. The dead were never the ones coming in.*

## VI. Hiding, counting, the bell

*Don't count the dead twice.*

The children get this one from the hiding game. Whoever opens a hiding place is written into it. Quiet feet are a bad sign, or a child. Children see height and scarf, so we teach them to look at boots, not faces. Whoever finds it says it, and the body stays where it is: we move the living to the square, not the dead.

Elsie counted her eggs twice into her apron every morning, behind the Row. She named her hens after Company officers. The one called the Clerk laid nothing. They found her in the hen run with her back to the fence and the eggs in her apron. Eleven, the Undertaker said, as if she hadn't known.

Since then: *don't count the dead twice.* What does it mean? Nobody agrees. That's how you know it's ours.

And who rings a bell for nothing? Nobody, the first time. The second time, it is whoever was left standing nearest the rope.

*A bell rung for nothing is a bell rung for you.*

## VII. Bottles, watches, faces

*Never take a bottle from a woman who keeps two.*

It is said of Mother Moll. It isn't fair, and it's said anyway.

Stand by one door a night, and never the same door twice. A bandaged man wakes knowing the height of whoever came for him. Everyone tells the Vicar sooner or later, so tell him early, while it's small. Wenna's dead have only a few words between them, so ask them short. The Gamekeeper does not go alone.

Trust her height. Faces are lent. Blood on a coat can be paint. A noise in the lane can be thrown. The knife has an heir.

The one we say lowest is this: watch the hands of whoever asks loudest who did it. I have asked loud. Everyone on the Row has. So at the bell we watch each other's hands, and that is a kind of prayer too.

Whose face did you borrow this morning, to come out in?

*Trust the height. Faces are lent.*

## VIII. What we don't give sayings to

*When you hear feet and see nobody, don't answer.*

Mother Moll says the fog is in everyone's chest. Stan Pike had it. Arthur had it. We hear it through the walls at night and we say *bless you*, which is not a saying, only a hope.

We say the wolves. We say the wisps. We do not say the Gallery.

Reg and Norman sit at the mouth with their hats on their knees. Reg cracks his knuckles in the order of the old levels, top to bottom. Something is asleep down there, and the mere-rot wakes it. That is all anyone on the Row will put into words, and we use as few as that.

A saying is a kind of name. You don't name a thing you want to stay asleep.

Gives you away to what? Don't ask me that either.

*In the wood a light keeps you. Under the Gallery a light gives you away.*

## IX. The fork

*Every trade comes to a fork, and the fork comes at night.*

Tamsin at the ovens can bake to the Clerk's list or to her own. Bar has two roads behind the bar, and only one of them is the street. The Undertaker signed for a roll of lead on the platform. We all saw him, and nobody asked what it was for. The Clerk posts the scrip in the morning and counts it at noon, and the two numbers have stopped agreeing.

Nobody says which way any of them went. We say: *Nobody chooses a fork. You find you have walked one.*

And here is the road's fork, where the lane splits below the churchyard. Up the one way to the stone, or out the other past the post at the edge of Gallows Wood. *At the fork, take the lit side*: every child on the Row has that one. The post is still burning out at the wood's edge. Up the other way, the sky is coming grey over the stone. Which is the lit side, at first light?

I could read the name and carry it down first, the way I always do. Or I could walk out past the post and let the Row learn it from the bakery and the boy and the cart. They would learn it either way. The town is ahead of the stone now. If I don't read it, does it stay uncut? It doesn't. It never has.

You never had a fork, Edith. You had a bolt and a blank.

*The lit side is not always the safe side. It is only the side you can be seen on.*

## X. The stone

*Carry in what you love at curfew.*

That one is Rose's. Every night at curfew she took her geranium in off the sill, the only flower on Pump Row, holding it against her like something that might wake. Rose died yesterday. Last night nobody carried it in. It is still on the sill, in its tin.

Every saying is somebody. It is Nora on the dock, answering two notes with two notes, though nobody whistles first now. It is Elsie counting twice. It is Hattie's one button. It is Rose's tin of red at the window. It is your two weeks, Edith. We say them so we don't have to say who.

I take the lit side. I go up. Under the binding my arm aches the way the sayings say a bite will before weather. The stone is where it was. I read it, and the name is in my mouth, ready, before anyone has asked.

*A saying is a name worn smooth. Say it first, and no one has to hear whose it was.*
