# The interface that is not there

Ninety-Nine's interface is mostly an absence. Doc 4 §14 rules out a settings menu, a volume
slider, a tutorial, an endings gallery and "Any `<button>` that is not diegetic", and its
2026-10-03 amendment extends the rule to the choices themselves ([[sec:DOC4:14]]). What still
sits over the canvas is small:

- each choice's words, standing in the room over the place to walk to;
- spoken lines and company text as captions;
- a walk hint that fades after nine seconds;
- the touch joystick;
- a sound toggle;
- a fire-exit sign that says EXIT.

The owner's answers frame this chapter: "Keep EXIT as it is", the labels are "Never: always
truthful", and the owner plays on "Desktop". All three already hold in the build. The one tension
is that the truthful labels sit inside a building whose outcomes are not truthful at all, and the
chapter argues that this split is the design working.

## EXIT, the one button

**Describe.** EXIT is a small low-contrast button in the bottom-left corner, coloured like a fire
exit sign (green `#7ec98f` on `#0a2e12`, at 45% opacity until hovered;
`ninety-nine/style.css:314`). Its header quotes the doc's own rule: "Quitting is an ending, not an
exit". The player who leaves is filed as PENDING REVIEW (`ninety-nine/src/bail.js:1`,
[[sec:DOC4:9]]). On desktop a mouse click bails at once, because the pointer had to be released
from pointer lock first, so the click is deliberate. Escape no longer bails, because under pointer
lock Escape is the browser's own "release the mouse" key. On touch, the first tap arms it
(**EXIT?**) and only a second tap within 3 s leaves. A three-finger press held for 1200 ms also
bails, so it never collides with one thumb walking and the other looking
(`ninety-nine/src/bail.js:26`, `ninety-nine/src/bail.js:52`).

**Compare** *(Claude's analysis)*. Right Door, Wrong Exit, this game's structural reference, has
a SKIP tab that also ends a room early. There it moves you *forward*. Here EXIT moves you to an
ending, and to the one ending that is a waiting room. The sign says EXIT, and it leads into the
file. That is Right Door's title, "Right Door, Wrong Exit", made into a control.

**Critique** *(Claude's analysis)*. "Keep EXIT as it is" is well supported. EXIT is the only way to
stop that the game owns, and it routes the stop into the fiction rather than out of it. On
desktop the release-then-click sequence is a real two-step confirmation that the touch path has
to imitate with EXIT?. One small gap: the sound toggle is also a non-diegetic `<button>`. §14's
body names the bail-out as "the one exception", and only the amendment adds the sound toggle. It
is worth making that one sentence agree with the build, so the spec's rule has exactly the
exceptions the game has.

## Truthful labels in a lying building

**Describe.** Each threshold's words stand in the world over its zone. They fade in on approach,
and when the zone is stepped into they "stamp": the rule under them stretches, and they go white
and fade (`ninety-nine/src/labels.js:1`). They are set in the company typeface, and a test scans
every label for "you" (C4). A room can ask for dark words on a pale halo where its walls blow out
to white (S1 H), or for a dark plate on a bright busy ground (the street)
(`ninety-nine/src/labels.js:6`). The words name the act: TAKE A SEAT / STAY STANDING, STAND / RUN,
PART THE CURTAIN / TRY THE DOOR ([[scene:S1]], [[scene:S2]], [[scene:S3]],
`ninety-nine/data/scenes.json:69`).

The *outcomes* converge. In S7 C, REFUSE is met by "THE CAR IS ALREADY IN MOTION." and the same
ride down. In S7 H, TURN BACK ends in the same fall as JUMP. In S8 H, SWIM FOR THE PILLARS is taken
down the grate "anyway (C3)" ([[sec:SCENE_FLOW:s7-the-descent]],
[[sec:SCENE_FLOW:s8-the-delivery-the-dive]]).

**Compare** *(Claude's analysis)*. In The Stanley Parable the narrator's words and the doors
disagree, and that gap is the joke. Ninety-Nine keeps the words honest and lets the architecture
disagree. The player always does exactly what the floor says, and it never matters in the way they
hoped.

**Critique** *(Claude's analysis)*. "Never: always truthful" is the right call for this game's
argument, and it reads more strongly against the owner's stage 0 answer ("Yes, both": no correct
answer, no escape). If the labels lied, a player could blame the trick for the loss. With honest
labels the player chose, and the building absorbed the choice. The one place to watch is that the
labels are company typeface and company text (C4). So "truthful" here means *the company is
truthful about the act and silent about the result*. That is consistent with a company that will
call him "you" only once, at the end. It is worth saying in Doc 4 that the labels never lie, as a
rule beside C4, because the next scene added will be tempted to break it.

## The walk hint and the captions

**Describe.** The walk hint reads `CLICK TO LOOK · W A S D WALKS · SHIFT HURRIES` on desktop, or
`LEFT THUMB WALKS · RIGHT THUMB LOOKS` on touch, and fades after 9 s
(`ninety-nine/src/director.js:913`). Doc 4 §14 rules out a tutorial: "The intake form is the
tutorial". Spoken lines and company text land as captions at the bottom of the screen
(`ninety-nine/style.css:279`).

**Critique** *(Claude's analysis)*. For a desktop-first owner, the desktop hint is the one that
matters, and it is complete: look, walk, hurry. It never names EXIT or Escape, which fits EXIT
being "easy to ignore" (the CSS comment on the button). Pointer lock does teach one hidden step
that the game relies on: Escape releases the mouse, and only then is EXIT clickable. A first-time
desktop player who wants out has to discover that. That is probably right for this game, but it is
the one control nobody is told about.

## Desktop first

**Describe.** On desktop the player clicks to capture the mouse, walks with WASD and hurries with
Shift (`ninety-nine/src/walk/controls.js:112`). Touch has a joystick on one thumb and a look drag
on the other, a two-tap EXIT and the three-finger long press (Doc 4 §13, [[sec:DOC4:13]]). The
autopilot and the headless walkthrough drive the game through the director, not through either
input (`ninety-nine/src/dev/autopilot.js`).

**Critique** *(Claude's analysis)*. Desktop first means the touch path is the less-played one, and
it is also the more complex: the arming EXIT?, the three-finger press, the label ink on small
screens. Neither the autopilot nor the walkthrough presses a key or touches the screen, so the
touch path is checked only when someone plays it on a phone. That is acceptable for an owner who
plays on desktop, as long as the README says so. Otherwise a phone player will find the
three-finger bail by accident, or never.
